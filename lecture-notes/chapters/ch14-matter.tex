\section{Two types of matter}\label{sec:c14-types}

Welcome to the 14th lecture \ts{14}{1}{0:00}. ``This will be a rather brief lecture and it is on matter.'' One should rather give a four- or five-lecture course on matter than one, so it cannot do the subject justice; only what is needed to continue with what the school is mainly interested in is done. There are essentially two types of matter, not physical types but more like \emph{theoretical} types:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{point matter}: for instance a massive point particle;
  \item \textbf{field matter}: for instance the electromagnetic field.
\end{itemize}
What is their status? In the real world neither exists, since both are \emph{classical} matter types, and only classical matter is looked at in these lectures. From the point of view of general relativity \emph{field matter is the fundamental type}, whereas point matter plays a big role in the phenomenology of general relativity. For instance massless point particles can be discussed as the geometric-optics limit of electromagnetic theory (light rays), which may give rise to an action for light particles although in the fundamental field theory there is no such thing; and it will be field matter that generates the curvature of spacetime: ``if we say matter curves spacetime, we take field matter''. One can always take point matter and make it into fake field matter (problems in the tutorials on this). So this is more of an academic distinction: sometimes it is easier to express physical ideas in one, sometimes to write down equations in the other; the claim is not that nature has two types of matter, but the emphasis is on field matter, and point matter is of phenomenological importance. To be very pure one would talk only about field matter and derive the geometric-optics statements from it, ``however that seems overly academic'', so one starts with point matter \ts{14}{1}{0:20}\ts{14}{1}{0:42}\ts{14}{1}{1:23}\ts{14}{1}{2:26}\ts{14}{1}{2:47}\ts{14}{1}{3:07}\ts{14}{1}{3:28}.

\section{Point matter: the law of motion}\label{sec:c14-point}

The postulates P1 and P2 of chapter~\ref{ch:c13} (a massive particle runs such that its tangent vector lies inside the local cone, a massless one such that it lies on its boundary) already constrain the possible world lines. But how does a particle \emph{really} move? That is the question of the law of motion, possibly in the presence of forces. In Newtonian mechanics one has forces acting at a distance: a source somewhere and a particle reacting to it. In relativity this makes no sense any more: the picture is that a particle source sources a field (for instance an electromagnetic field), the field propagates elsewhere, and only then can another particle coupling to the field feel it there: \emph{one replaces forces entirely by the field concept, interaction fields} \ts{14}{1}{3:48}\ts{14}{1}{4:31}\ts{14}{1}{4:53}\ts{14}{1}{5:14}\ts{14}{1}{5:34}.

\paragraph{Without external forces.} A postulate, which will later turn out not to be a postulate at all but a \emph{consequence of the Einstein field equations}:

\begin{definition}[Postulate: the actions of point particles]\label{def:c14-actions}
The dynamics of a \emph{massive} particle with world line $\gamma$ is given by the action
\[
  S_{\mathrm{massive}}[\gamma]=m\int\mathrm d\lambda\,\sqrt{g\bigl(v_\gamma,v_\gamma\bigr)\big|_{\gamma(\lambda)}},
\]
the length of the world line times the mass (the mass does not matter for the Euler--Lagrange equations, where it cancels, but later interaction terms will be added and then it is of relative importance), with the world line future directed, $g(T,v_\gamma)>0$ (so the tangent vector lies in the half cone, which is why these half cones were chosen). For a \emph{massless} particle,
\[
  S_{\mathrm{massless}}[\gamma,\mu]=\int\mathrm d\lambda\,\mu(\lambda)\,g\bigl(v_\gamma,v_\gamma\bigr)\big|_{\gamma(\lambda)},
\]
without the square root, with an additional Lagrange multiplier $\mu(\lambda)$ (another function, ``a slick way to do it'') that is varied together with $\gamma$. Whenever an action is written down, the dynamical law is that the world line is a \emph{stationary curve} of the action: the equations of motion are its Euler--Lagrange equations.
\end{definition}
\ts{14}{1}{6:16}\ts{14}{1}{6:38}\ts{14}{1}{7:18}\ts{14}{1}{7:40}\ts{14}{1}{8:01}\ts{14}{1}{8:23}\ts{14}{1}{8:44}
In the massive case one could leave out the square root and normalise the curve to the parameter for which the Lagrangian is one all the time (as in chapter~\ref{ch:c10}). The variation with respect to $\mu$ gives the equation that forces the curve to be a \emph{null} curve (``that is how you call that in the jargon''): its tangent always lies on the boundary of the cone; the variation with respect to $\gamma$ gives the equations of motion of the massless particle \ts{14}{1}{8:44}\ts{14}{1}{9:04}\ts{14}{1}{9:25}.

\begin{coursenote}[Student question: you forgot the square root]
In the massless action there is none, on purpose. These curves cannot be parametrised by their time: time does not run along them, although that is a meaningless statement, because time was defined only for observers, whose tangents lie inside the cone (Schuller almost says ``time-like'' again and stops himself). Time is not defined along null curves, so a square-root length is meaningless there \ts{14}{1}{9:46}\ts{14}{1}{10:06}\ts{14}{1}{10:27}\ts{14}{1}{10:47}.
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{Why actions.} One does not really insist on it, but it is convenient to start from an action and derive the equations of motion rather than writing them down directly. The biggest advantage: \emph{composite systems have an action that is the sum of the actions of their parts}, possibly including \textbf{interaction terms}. Example: one action for one particle $\gamma$, one for another particle $\delta$; they have nothing to do with each other. To describe how they interact, one adds an interaction part containing both, in terms that are products or similar, so that on varying the total action the equations for $\gamma$ get a contribution from there and likewise the equations for $\delta$. ``Of course the interaction terms are modelled such that what comes out is what you desire. It is just a convenient tool'' \ts{14}{1}{10:47}\ts{14}{1}{11:12}\ts{14}{1}{11:33}\ts{14}{1}{12:16}\ts{14}{1}{12:37}.
\begin{coursenote}[Student question: is the additivity of actions an implicit assumption?]
No secret: there is one big generating functional; if the parts depend on different objects, variation with respect to one does not see the other, so one can add them without harm and not lose information. For the interaction term the answer to ``how do you know that you can add it?'' is that it is \emph{designed} such that it produces precisely what one wants. ``There is nothing deep in an action, really, is my claim'': in quantum mechanics the whole action counts, not only the stationary points, but from a classical point of view it is a convenient device \ts{14}{1}{15:30}\ts{14}{1}{15:51}\ts{14}{1}{16:12}\ts{14}{1}{16:32}.
\end{coursenote}

\section{Coupling to an external field: the Lorentz force}\label{sec:c14-lorentz}

The presence of external forces, or rather, from the point of view above, the presence of \emph{fields to which a particle couples}: in jargon, an interaction term with the field (one had interaction between two particles; one can have interaction with a field). ``Actually we will never look at something like this; it is just to illustrate the concept.'' The prime example: take a massive point particle and add an interaction term with an \emph{external} field, assumed given, a covector field $A$ (a $(0,1)$ tensor field on spacetime, e.g.\ the electromagnetic potential) \ts{14}{1}{13:00}\ts{14}{1}{13:21}\ts{14}{1}{13:43}\ts{14}{1}{14:03}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c14-coupled}
  S[\gamma;A]=m\int\mathrm d\lambda\,\sqrt{g\bigl(v_\gamma,v_\gamma\bigr)}+Q\int\mathrm d\lambda\,A\bigl(v_\gamma\bigr)\big|_{\gamma(\lambda)},
\end{equation}
the covector eats the only vector there is, the velocity; $Q$ is the charge (if $Q=0$ the field may be there but the particle does not feel it). The semicolon before $A$ says that the action is varied with respect to $\gamma$ only: $A$ is given and not varied \ts{14}{1}{14:24}\ts{14}{1}{14:44}\ts{14}{1}{15:06}\ts{14}{1}{16:53}\ts{14}{1}{17:14}.

\paragraph{The Euler--Lagrange equations.} In a chart, from the first part with the right parametrisation one has the geodesic part $m\,g_{mj}\bigl(\ddot\gamma^j+\Gamma^j{}_{ik}\dot\gamma^i\dot\gamma^k\bigr)$ (chapter~\ref{ch:c10}), which alone ($Q=0$) is the geodesic equation. The interaction part has $L_{\mathrm{int}}=Q\,A_m(\gamma)\,\dot\gamma^m$, giving
\[
  \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\frac{\partial L_{\mathrm{int}}}{\partial\dot\gamma^m}-\frac{\partial L_{\mathrm{int}}}{\partial\gamma^m}
  =Q\,\partial_sA_m\,\dot\gamma^s-Q\,\partial_mA_s\,\dot\gamma^s
  =Q\bigl(\partial_sA_m-\partial_mA_s\bigr)\dot\gamma^s=Q\,F_{sm}\,\dot\gamma^s,
\]
where $F_{sm}:=\partial_sA_m-\partial_mA_s$ is the \textbf{Faraday tensor} (the derivative of $A$ depends on the position along the world line; this is the miracle where the two derivatives combine into the field strength; Schuller first writes ``$\mathrm d/\mathrm d\lambda$ of the wrong thing'' and a student corrects him, ``it is nonsense, thank you, I was already in the field theory'') \ts{14}{1}{17:34}\ts{14}{1}{17:55}\ts{14}{1}{18:15}\ts{14}{1}{18:37}\ts{14}{1}{18:57}\ts{14}{1}{19:17}\ts{14}{1}{19:39}\ts{14}{1}{20:00}\ts{14}{1}{20:20}\ts{14}{1}{20:43}\ts{14}{1}{21:03}\ts{14}{1}{21:23}\ts{14}{1}{21:44}.
Taking the term to the other side, with the index raised (``it is good advice not to pull indices up and down, I know; I start with the index down because if you really do the variation you arrive at this; only afterwards, with an overall multiplication by $g^{ac}$, the equation with an index up'') one gets the \textbf{Lorentz force law}
\[
  m\,\nabla_{v_\gamma}v_\gamma=-Q\,F^{a}{}_{m}\,\dot\gamma^m,\qquad\text{coordinate free:}\quad m\,\nabla_{v_\gamma}v_\gamma=-Q\,\bigl(F(\,\cdot\,,v_\gamma)\bigr)^\sharp,
\]
as on the board (see the note on the sign below), the force on a charged particle in an electromagnetic field: the open slot of $F$ eats another vector, which makes the whole thing a covector field, and the index is lifted with the inverse of the musical map (``chart independent''). The right-hand side contains the $E$ and $B$ and the velocity \ts{14}{1}{22:06}\ts{14}{1}{22:27}\ts{14}{1}{22:53}\ts{14}{1}{23:13}\ts{14}{1}{25:02}\ts{14}{1}{25:22}\ts{14}{1}{25:43}\ts{14}{1}{26:04}.
\begin{coursenote}[A sign to check]
(Not said in class.) Following the steps above with $F_{sm}=\partial_sA_m-\partial_mA_s$ gives $m\,(\nabla_{v_\gamma}v_\gamma)_m+Q\,F_{sm}\dot\gamma^s=0$, i.e.\ $m\,(\nabla_{v_\gamma}v_\gamma)_m=Q\,F_{ms}\,\dot\gamma^s$, the opposite overall sign to the one on the board; on the board the intermediate result was written with $F_{am}\dot\gamma^m$ and the opposite sign of the $\partial A$ combination, so the slip is in the sign convention of $F$ or in the step ``$=Q\,F$''. Schuller warns elsewhere in the lecture: ``sign conventions are notoriously dangerous in this context''; ``homework: check all signs in today's lecture'' at the end of the lecture. The physical sign depends on the convention for $A$ and for the sign of the charge, and is fixed only by comparing with the lab.
\end{coursenote}
The coupling to $A$ produces exactly what one wants, the Lorentz force law, \emph{that is why this is the right coupling}; apart from this, it also has the nice property that both terms are \emph{reparametrisation invariant}: a reparametrisation of the second term cancels exactly against the change of the measure, so the whole thing is reparametrisation invariant. To see that this is really the Lorentz force law one must ask an observer: extract the $0$ and $\alpha$ components with respect to the frame of a particular observer, resolve the summation convention in that frame (chapter~\ref{ch:c13}), and one sees the $E$ and $B$ terms \ts{14}{1}{23:34}\ts{14}{1}{23:57}\ts{14}{1}{24:21}\ts{14}{1}{24:41}.

\section{Field matter}\label{sec:c14-field}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: classical field matter]\label{def:c14-field}
It is very difficult to give a definition of matter that does not over- or understate it: the standard model has several types of matter (interaction fields, Dirac fields and so on), but here only \emph{classical} field theory is considered, ``and once I am there I could actually say it is just Maxwell theory, because really the only classical field theory out there in nature is Maxwell theory''. In general relativity one is prepared to consider as \textbf{classical field matter} any tensor field on spacetime whose equations of motion derive from an action. ``It is really a very silly definition.''
\end{definition}
\ts{14}{1}{26:27}\ts{14}{1}{26:53}\ts{14}{1}{27:13}\ts{14}{1}{27:34}\ts{14}{1}{27:55}\ts{14}{1}{28:19}
\begin{example}[Maxwell theory]\label{ex:c14-maxwell}
Now the action is not for a curve but for a whole tensor field, here the covector field $A$. It is an integral over all of spacetime, pretending for simplicity that one chart covers all of $M$ (otherwise one uses a partition of unity, ``we are not going to write this down''):
\[
  S_{\mathrm{Maxwell}}[A;g]=-\frac14\int\mathrm d^4x\,\sqrt{-\det g}\;g^{ac}\,g^{bd}\,F_{ab}F_{cd},
\]
where $F_{ab}$ appears twice and the indices are related by $g^{ac}g^{bd}$ (Schuller: ``the $F_{ab}F_{cd}$ with the indices related by $g^{ac}g^{bd}$ and then a $1/4$ in front or what''), and $F_{ab}=2\partial_{[a}A_{b]}=\partial_aA_b-\partial_bA_a$ is a short form for the antisymmetrised derivative. Since $g$ has signature $(+,-,-,-)$ its determinant is always negative, so to take the square root one puts a minus sign: this is the only adaptation compared to the Riemannian integration (chapter~\ref{ch:c12}), compensating the Lorentzian signature. This is the famous $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$ action, a relativistic formulation; because of the factor $\sqrt{-\det g}$ it works on \emph{curved} spacetime (one probably saw it with $\eta$, whose determinant is $-1$, so the factor is invisible; but in a chart that is not the trivial one, or in a non-Lorentz transformed chart, the term appears nevertheless). In this lecture the curved spacetime metric is assumed fixed, with all its little cones, for the time being (the overall constant of the action is not written out on the board [?]; the $-\tfrac14$ is the standard one for this signature).
\end{example}
\ts{14}{1}{28:40}\ts{14}{1}{29:01}\ts{14}{1}{29:22}\ts{14}{1}{29:43}\ts{14}{1}{30:04}\ts{14}{1}{30:25}\ts{14}{1}{30:46}
\begin{coursenote}[Student question: classical versus non-classical]
``What would be a non-classical metric?'' One could still write down such actions, but their Euler--Lagrange equations would not tell what is going on: one would have to do quantum field theory with them. \emph{Classical} here refers not to non-relativistic but to non-quantum; Maxwell theory was written down before relativity but carries relativity in it \ts{14}{1}{31:06}\ts{14}{1}{31:26}\ts{14}{1}{31:47}.
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{The Euler--Lagrange equations for fields.} Be careful: everything under the integral, including $\sqrt{-\det g}$, is the \textbf{Lagrangian density} $\mathcal L$: it is a spacetime scalar because everything is contracted, but the density factor makes it a Lagrangian density, and one must carry it along. In a chart (``always do it in a chart''), for a Lagrangian depending on $A_m$ and its first derivatives,
\[
  \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial A_m}-\partial_s\Bigl(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_sA_m)}\Bigr)=0.
\]
The $F$ is just a short form for $2\partial_{[a}A_{b]}$ (one may act with a covariant derivative on $A$ and antisymmetrise: the Christoffel symbols drop out, as checked in the tutorials); the real degree of freedom of electrodynamics is \emph{not} the $E$ and $B$ field but the underlying potential $A$, and one must vary with respect to the potential: in terms of $E$ and $B$ the action is $E^2-B^2$ and varying with respect to $E$ and $B$ would give $E=0$, $B=0$, ``that cannot be it''. If the Lagrangian depends on higher derivatives the series continues with alternating signs plus, minus, $\partial_s\partial_t(\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_s\partial_tA_m))$ and so on, and one stops when the Lagrangian density no longer depends on them \ts{14}{1}{31:47}\ts{14}{1}{32:09}\ts{14}{1}{32:30}\ts{14}{1}{32:51}\ts{14}{1}{33:11}\ts{14}{1}{33:33}\ts{14}{1}{33:53}\ts{14}{1}{34:13}\ts{14}{1}{34:35}.
The calculation is a bit stressful and not carried out; the result is
\[
  \nabla_m F^{ma}=0,
\]
the \textbf{inhomogeneous Maxwell equations} (the name because with a current $J$ coupled by $+Q\,A(J)$, as in the coupling of \eqref{eq:c14-coupled}, the right-hand side would be the current, for instance from a point particle: ``you would only have that current if this was coupled to a current''). The \emph{homogeneous} Maxwell equations follow from $F$ being defined as the antisymmetrised derivative of $A$: $\partial_{[a}F_{bc]}=0$, i.e.\ the exterior derivative of $F$ is zero (``you checked on the problem sheet that this is the same''). To compare with the Maxwell equations as they appear in Jackson, go to an observer frame and extract $E$'s and $B$'s (flat spacetime; in curved spacetime one gets something maybe corrected) \ts{14}{1}{34:56}\ts{14}{1}{35:16}\ts{14}{1}{35:36}\ts{14}{1}{35:57}\ts{14}{1}{36:17}\ts{14}{1}{36:38}\ts{14}{1}{36:59}\ts{14}{1}{37:20}.
(A student asks whether $\partial$ in the definition of $F_{ab}$ is the definition: yes, Schuller was a little sloppy with notation, and the notation $\partial/\partial x^a$ there was declared as the short form.)

\begin{example}[The Klein--Gordon field]\label{ex:c14-kg}
Another example liked by textbooks: a scalar field $\phi$, a $(0,0)$ tensor field, with action over the whole manifold (same assumption that it exists like that)
\[
  S_{\mathrm{KG}}[\phi;g]=\int\mathrm d^4x\,\sqrt{-\det g}\,\Bigl(\tfrac12\,g^{ab}\,\partial_a\phi\,\partial_b\phi-\tfrac12\,m^2\phi^2\Bigr),
\]
possibly with all kinds of extra terms (``for instance this one'', the mass term): a Klein--Gordon field of mass $m$ [the numerical factors are the standard ones, not written in the captions]. ``Do not take it too seriously'': the mass gets meaning only in quantum theory again (it could be a Higgs field); in classical physics there is no fundamental scalar field, ``we do not see it''. The reason for talking about matter at all: the Einstein equations have matter sources, so parts of the curvature of spacetime are determined by the matter content \ts{14}{1}{37:40}\ts{14}{1}{38:02}\ts{14}{1}{38:22}\ts{14}{1}{38:43}\ts{14}{1}{39:04}\ts{14}{1}{39:26}.
\end{example}

\section{The energy-momentum tensor}\label{sec:c14-emt}

Third, the energy-momentum tensor of matter fields. The definition given here does not fit the one met in field theory classes, but it can be shown to coincide (not easily, and only more or less recently clarified: ``it has been an issue of long debate'') \ts{14}{1}{39:47}\ts{14}{1}{40:07}\ts{14}{1}{40:28}.

\paragraph{The prelude: an action for the metric.} So far it was always ``let us assume there is a smooth manifold equipped with some Lorentzian metric and a time orientation''; but \emph{which} Lorentzian metric? The answer: there will be dynamical equations for the metric that need to be solved, and the solutions are the metrics to use: the Einstein equations. To obtain them one writes down an action for the metric tensor field itself, $S_{\mathrm{grav}}[g]$ (``to be specified later''), added to the matter action of whatever matter fields (an $A$, maybe a $\phi$ if one is courageous):
\[
  S_{\mathrm{total}}[g,A]=S_{\mathrm{grav}}[g]+S_{\mathrm{Maxwell}}[A,g].
\]
Before, the semicolon in $S_{\mathrm{Maxwell}}[A;g]$ meant that only $A$ was varied and $g$ considered given; now, with an action for $g$, one varies the total action with respect to $g$ as well: the semicolon becomes a comma. Varying with respect to $A$, the gravity part does not see it and one gets Maxwell's equations. Varying with respect to $g$ one gets a contribution from the gravity part, called $G_{ab}$ (two indices, as $g$ has two; carried down if one varies with respect to $g$ with indices down) and a contribution from the matter part, from the $g$ appearing in it (e.g.\ pulling the indices on $F$), called $-2T_{ab}$ with factors fixed, and a prefactor $1/16\pi G$, $G$ the Newton gravitational constant (for those who want to get the factors right), and the whole thing ought to be zero \ts{14}{1}{40:48}\ts{14}{1}{41:08}\ts{14}{1}{41:29}\ts{14}{1}{41:52}\ts{14}{1}{42:21}\ts{14}{1}{42:41}\ts{14}{1}{43:01}\ts{14}{1}{43:22}\ts{14}{1}{43:43}\ts{14}{1}{44:03}\ts{14}{1}{44:23}\ts{14}{1}{44:43}.

The gravity part is a general feature that one does not change; one considers different matter present. A matter action that does not depend on $g$ would be a \emph{topological field theory} (``very exotic, very interesting from a theoretical point of view''), but all the classical matter in nature, and also the Klein--Gordon field and the standard model matter, even classically, depend on $g$. So the gravity term stays fixed while one considers various matter actions, and it is convenient once and for all to associate to the matter action the object obtained by varying it with respect to $g$. One then always gets the same type of equation,
\[
  G_{ab}=8\pi G_{\mathrm N}\,T_{ab},
\]
the \textbf{Einstein equation} (the left side to be described later): the secret why one is interested in this object \ts{14}{1}{45:05}\ts{14}{1}{45:26}\ts{14}{1}{45:46}\ts{14}{1}{46:07}\ts{14}{1}{46:27}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: energy-momentum tensor]\label{def:c14-emt}
Let $S_{\mathrm{matter}}[\Phi;g]$ be the action of any matter field $\Phi$ (``could be anything'') with Lagrangian density $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{matter}}$. The \textbf{energy-momentum tensor} is
\[
  T^{ab}:=\frac{2}{\sqrt{-\det g}}\Bigl[\frac{\partial\mathcal L_{\mathrm{matter}}}{\partial g_{ab}}-\partial_s\frac{\partial\mathcal L_{\mathrm{matter}}}{\partial(\partial_sg_{ab})}+\dots\Bigr],
\]
the variation of the matter action with respect to the metric (the density taken out), i.e.\ the Euler--Lagrange expression of $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{matter}}$ with respect to $g_{ab}$, up to the convention-dependent overall sign.
\end{definition}
\ts{14}{1}{46:48}\ts{14}{1}{47:11}\ts{14}{1}{47:33}\ts{14}{1}{47:53}\ts{14}{1}{48:14}\ts{14}{1}{48:35}
(The factor $2$ is conventional ``in order to make it coincide with the standard energy-momentum tensor''; a student remarks that because of it one gets the $2$ in the Einstein equation: yes, a $\tfrac12$ in front; Schuller's board mixes the placement of the $2$ and the $\tfrac12$ and he corrects himself: ``sorry, it is a $\tfrac12$ in front''. The exact normalisation of the density is not written on the board and is the standard one [?].) \emph{Sign conventions are notoriously dangerous here}, because $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{matter}}$ contains $g$ and its sign goes in (a colleague that morning had named it a ``Schwinger [Schr\"odinger in the captions] minus''). One chooses all sign conventions, this being the last, such that $T$, here defined as a $(2,0)$ tensor but also definable as a $(0,2)$ one, satisfies
\[
  T\bigl(\varepsilon^0,\varepsilon^0\bigr)>0,
\]
where $\varepsilon^a$ is the dual of the frame of an observer: this is \emph{positive energy density}. ``Only at the end of the calculation do you measure and see whether it is plus or minus'' \ts{14}{1}{48:55}\ts{14}{1}{49:16}\ts{14}{1}{49:36}\ts{14}{1}{49:57}\ts{14}{1}{50:17}.

\begin{example}[The energy-momentum tensor of Maxwell theory]\label{ex:c14-tmaxwell}
With the indices pulled down with the metric, for $S_{\mathrm{Maxwell}}$ one finds
\[
  T_{ab}=F_{am}\,F_b{}^m-\tfrac14\,g_{ab}\,F_{mn}F^{mn}
\]
[overall sign and index order not verified, as in the lecture: ``I hope the signs are right, or something of the type'']. One can extract all these quantities from the energy-momentum tensor \ts{14}{1}{50:37}\ts{14}{1}{50:59}\ts{14}{1}{51:21}\ts{14}{1}{51:42}\ts{14}{1}{52:02}.
\end{example}
\begin{coursenote}[Student question: why only positive energy, e.g.\ for Klein--Gordon?]
In Klein--Gordon one often keeps also the ``negative solutions'' and interprets them somehow. Negative energy in the matter makes the system unstable, because the matter can suck up more and more energy; that is why one does not want it. There is a whole list of conditions one could impose on an energy-momentum tensor: the \emph{energy conditions} (strong energy condition, dominant energy condition, and so on). Because classically there is only one type of matter, one tries to capture what any \emph{future} matter one might discover might have, not in detail but from its properties: but with just one example in the classical domain, ``it is a rather academic idea to be given an abstract rule for which you have only one example to test it'' \ts{14}{1}{52:23}\ts{14}{1}{52:44}\ts{14}{1}{53:05}\ts{14}{1}{53:25}\ts{14}{1}{53:46}.

\section{Phenomenology: perfect fluids, radiation and dust}\label{sec:c14-fluid}

In cosmology one often does not specify the fundamental equations of motion or action of some matter, but is satisfied to \emph{assume certain properties or forms of the energy-momentum tensor}. The prime example, to be used later, is a \emph{homogeneous and isotropic universe}: these are symmetry conditions, \emph{six Killing vector fields} (chapter~\ref{ch:c11}) ``eliminating the metric'', and a \textbf{perfect fluid}: the idea is that at large scales the galaxies or galaxy clusters form a perfect fluid that just flows; having a tangent vector field $U$ (a four-velocity, normalised, $g(U,U)=1$), a perfect fluid of pressure $P$ and density $\rho$ is modelled by
\[
  T_{ab}=(\rho+P)\,U_aU_b-P\,g_{ab}
\]
(minus is correct in the conventions of this course). ``I do not care what the fluid consists of'': there are arguments why it should take this form, but this is \emph{phenomenology}, not derived from an action (``I think it is rather difficult to get this from an action''): one fills spacetime with matter having this property because that is the idea of the contents of the universe at very large scale; one plays these games to make progress in solving the Einstein equations, not always starting from a fundamental matter action \ts{14}{1}{54:09}\ts{14}{1}{54:31}\ts{14}{1}{54:53}\ts{14}{1}{55:13}\ts{14}{1}{55:34}\ts{14}{1}{55:54}\ts{14}{1}{56:15}\ts{14}{1}{56:36}.

\begin{example}[Radiation fluid and dust]\label{ex:c14-eos}
What is a radiation fluid (a fluid of photons: ``a quantum-mechanical term, but you know what I mean'')? If one takes the Maxwell energy-momentum tensor and contracts its two indices with the metric one gets \emph{zero} (this is the effect of the factor $\tfrac14$: contracting $g_{ab}$ with $g^{ab}$ gives the dimension $4$ and the $\tfrac14$ cancels it against $F_{am}F^{am}$). A perfect fluid made of such stuff should satisfy the same, so contract the perfect-fluid tensor with $g^{ab}$ using the normalisation $g^{ab}U_aU_b=1$:
\[
  g^{ab}T_{ab}=(\rho+P)\cdot1-4P=\rho-3P=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P=\tfrac13\rho,
\]
the \textbf{equation of state} of a radiation fluid. The relation between $P$ and $\rho$ has to be submitted by hand: one must say what kind of fluid it is. Setting $P=0$ gives \textbf{dust}: it has a density but however dense it becomes it develops no pressure; this is the late universe, where everything is very far from each other with no interaction. The early universe, radiation dominated (to come), has pressure the higher the higher the density \ts{14}{1}{56:57}\ts{14}{1}{57:17}\ts{14}{1}{57:38}\ts{14}{1}{57:58}\ts{14}{1}{58:18}\ts{14}{1}{58:41}\ts{14}{1}{59:03}\ts{14}{1}{59:23}\ts{14}{1}{59:44}.
\end{example}
