\section{Geodesic deviation on a surface}\label{sec:c17-deviation}

``Welcome back. Let us continue the discussion on optical geometry.'' Before the break one saw that the Gaussian curvature of the equatorial plane of Schwarzschild in the optical geometry is negative everywhere. One now wants to know what that means for the \emph{geodesics} on the surface, i.e.\ for the spatial light rays. To study the connection between geodesics and curvature one looks at \textbf{geodesic deviation}, ``which I think you have seen before, at least briefly'', here in the context of surfaces \ts{17}{1}{0:00}\ts{17}{1}{0:20}\ts{17}{1}{0:41}.

\paragraph{Setting.} Take geodesics $\gamma$ with \emph{unit speed} with respect to the optical metric $\tilde g$: $\tilde g(\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma)=1$ (one can take $t$ as the parameter: ``in the optical geometry we can take time, of course, that is the $\mathrm dt$ of the line element''), parametrised as $x^a(t)$, satisfying $\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma=0$. To find out how geodesics vary from each other when one goes to a neighbouring geodesic, one introduces a vector field that measures this deviation, a \textbf{connecting vector field} $X$: it belongs to a \emph{congruence} of geodesics (a bunch of neighbouring geodesics defining a vector field), so that $X$ \emph{commutes} with $\dot\gamma$ ($[X,\dot\gamma]=0$: one can go around the little quadrilateral this way or that way, it closes); and $X$ is \emph{orthogonal} to the geodesic along which one looks at the deviation, $\tilde g(X,\dot\gamma)=0$ (the distance is measured orthogonally) \ts{17}{1}{1:03}\ts{17}{1}{1:23}\ts{17}{1}{1:43}\ts{17}{1}{2:04}\ts{17}{1}{2:25}\ts{17}{1}{2:46}\ts{17}{1}{3:10}. (A student asks which condition is for which; answer: the commutation is for the congruence, the orthogonality along the geodesic for which one looks at the deviation; ``I will draw a picture''.)

\begin{lemma}[Commuting vector fields]\label{lem:c17-commute}
If two vector fields $X$ and $Y$ commute, $[X,Y]=0$, then, for the Levi-Civita connection (symmetric in the lower indices),
\[
  \nabla_XY=\nabla_YX.
\]
\end{lemma}
\ts{17}{1}{3:10}\ts{17}{1}{3:31}
\begin{proof}
Take the $a$-th component of the commutator,
\[
  [X,Y]^a=X^b\partial_bY^a-Y^b\partial_bX^a=0,
\]
and add two terms that cancel (``why is this the same? These terms with the $\Gamma$'s are identical, because we can relabel $c$ and $b$, since we repeat indices''): $X^b\Gamma^a{}_{bc}Y^c-Y^b\Gamma^a{}_{bc}X^c=0$, which holds because the Levi-Civita connection is symmetric in its two lower indices. Then
\[\begin{aligned}
  [X,Y]^a&=X^b\bigl(\partial_bY^a+\Gamma^a{}_{bc}Y^c\bigr)-Y^b\bigl(\partial_bX^a+\Gamma^a{}_{bc}X^c\bigr)\\
  &=X^b\,Y^a{}_{;b}-Y^b\,X^a{}_{;b},
\end{aligned}\]
the $a$-th component of $\nabla_XY-\nabla_YX$. So $[X,Y]=0$ implies $\nabla_XY=\nabla_YX$. (The lecturer says the property was in an exam of the tutorials: ``you have seen it''; the caption ``torque'' is the torsion-freeness used here, symmetry of the lower indices.) \ts{17}{1}{3:31}\ts{17}{1}{3:51}\ts{17}{1}{4:14}\ts{17}{1}{4:50}\ts{17}{1}{5:11}\ts{17}{1}{5:31}\ts{17}{1}{5:52}\ts{17}{1}{6:13}\ts{17}{1}{6:33}
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The deviation equation.} ``Now let us look at geodesic deviation: plug in our vector fields into the Riemann tensor.'' By the definition used (the lecture's: $R(X,Y)Z=\nabla_X\nabla_YZ-\nabla_Y\nabla_XZ-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z$), with $Y=\dot\gamma$ and $Z=\dot\gamma$:
\[
  R(X,\dot\gamma)\dot\gamma=\nabla_X\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma-\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\nabla_X\dot\gamma-\nabla_{[X,\dot\gamma]}\dot\gamma.
\]
The last term vanishes because $X$ and $\dot\gamma$ commute; the first because $\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma=0$ (geodesic equation). What is left, using \cref{lem:c17-commute} ($\nabla_X\dot\gamma=\nabla_{\dot\gamma}X$, ``we can switch $X$ and $\dot\gamma$ in the covariant derivative'', the property named ``star''), is
\[
  \frac{\mathrm D^2X}{\mathrm dt^2}:=\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\nabla_{\dot\gamma}X=-R(X,\dot\gamma)\dot\gamma,
\]
the covariant derivative of the vector field along $\dot\gamma$, sometimes written $\mathrm D^2X/\mathrm dt^2$ for the parameter $t$ (the lecturer is asked what the notation $\mathrm D/\mathrm d\lambda$ means: ``it is just the covariant derivative along the curve'') \ts{17}{1}{6:33}\ts{17}{1}{7:04}\ts{17}{1}{7:25}\ts{17}{1}{7:46}\ts{17}{1}{8:08}\ts{17}{1}{8:34}\ts{17}{1}{8:57}\ts{17}{1}{9:17}.

Now in components on the surface, where the Riemann tensor is given in terms of the Gaussian curvature, \eqref{eq:c16-K} of chapter~\ref{ch:c16}: $R_{abcd}=K\bigl(g_{ad}g_{bc}-g_{ac}g_{bd}\bigr)$, and pulling up the first index, $R^a{}_{bcd}=K\bigl(\delta^a{}_dg_{bc}-\delta^a{}_cg_{bd}\bigr)$. The $a$-th component of the deviation is
\[
  \Bigl(\frac{\mathrm D^2X}{\mathrm dt^2}\Bigr)^a=R^a{}_{bcd}\,\dot\gamma^b\,X^c\,\dot\gamma^d
  =K\Bigl(\dot\gamma^a\,\tilde g(\dot\gamma,X)-X^a\,\tilde g(\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma)\Bigr)
\]
(in the sign convention of the lecture, which is the opposite one to the usual placement of the derivative terms, as in chapter~\ref{ch:c16}; ``sorry, it is tedious, but that is what we get''; the index contractions are shuffled several times on the board). By assumption $X$ is orthogonal to the geodesic, $\tilde g(\dot\gamma,X)=0$, and $\dot\gamma$ has unit length, $\tilde g(\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma)=1$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c17-deviation}
  \frac{\mathrm D^2X^a}{\mathrm dt^2}=-K\,X^a.
\end{equation}
\ts{17}{1}{9:17}\ts{17}{1}{9:40}\ts{17}{1}{10:01}\ts{17}{1}{10:22}\ts{17}{1}{10:42}\ts{17}{1}{11:03}\ts{17}{1}{11:24}\ts{17}{1}{11:45}\ts{17}{1}{12:07}\ts{17}{1}{12:27}\ts{17}{1}{12:50}\ts{17}{1}{13:12}

\begin{coursenote}[Student question: what is the physical picture?]
``The physical picture is just the same as before'': a surface with geodesics on it; the surface is curved; one wants the impact of the curvature on the geodesics. Take one geodesic, look at a neighbouring one, and ask whether the geodesics diverge, converge or stay parallel: this depends on the curvature, in exactly the way of \eqref{eq:c17-deviation}. The picture: the geodesic, neighbouring geodesics of the congruence, and the connecting vector field $X$ orthogonal to the geodesic: the components of $X$ measure the deviation, and \eqref{eq:c17-deviation} says how they change as one moves along $\dot\gamma$, parametrised by $t$; the left-hand side is the \emph{relative acceleration}, as measured in the parameter of the connecting vector field \ts{17}{1}{13:33}\ts{17}{1}{13:53}\ts{17}{1}{14:13}\ts{17}{1}{14:33}\ts{17}{1}{14:54}\ts{17}{1}{15:15}.
\end{coursenote}
So the \emph{local behaviour of neighbouring geodesics depends on the sign of the Gaussian curvature}: if $K>0$ the geodesics locally converge; if $K<0$ they diverge; if $K=0$ they stay parallel (``of course in three-dimensional space it would be different'') \ts{17}{1}{15:36}\ts{17}{1}{15:57}.
\begin{example}[Check on the sphere]\label{ex:c17-sphere}
For $K=+1$ \eqref{eq:c17-deviation} is a harmonic oscillator equation $\ddot X=-X$: on the unit sphere, sitting on the equator, the geodesics are great circles (the equator and the meridians), and the meridians get closer together as one moves away from the equator and meet at the pole. A student remarks that \eqref{eq:c17-deviation} looks like a harmonic oscillator: ``it does, does it not? That is exactly right: in some sense, if you are on the sphere, this is exactly what you get.'' Another student asks how one concludes from the equation that they converge: the equation concerns $X$ and not the geodesics, so one has to specify how the distance is measured; ``you would have a fixed parameter value times the component of $X$ that measures the distance, there are various ways of doing it'' \ts{17}{1}{16:58}\ts{17}{1}{17:20}\ts{17}{1}{17:41}\ts{17}{1}{18:01}.
\end{example}
Applied to the optical geometry of the equatorial plane of Schwarzschild, where $K<0$ everywhere (chapter~\ref{ch:c16}): the geodesics, which are the spatial light rays, \emph{diverge locally everywhere} \ts{17}{1}{15:57}\ts{17}{1}{16:17}\ts{17}{1}{16:37}.

\section{Why lensing is a global effect}\label{sec:c17-global}

But yesterday one saw pictures of gravitational lenses with \emph{multiple images} (the ``Cheshire cat lens'', multiple images of a background galaxy lensed by a cluster). The cluster has a complicated structure, with several galaxies; far from it, for these rays ``it is not entirely right to say this, but basically this is a Schwarzschild system'': far away from a compact mass distribution it looks like a point mass, and space is basically Schwarzschild. Then: \emph{if light rays diverge locally everywhere, how can they start at the light source, do something, and come back together at the observer in such a way that one sees multiple images?} The answer cannot be a local property: it must be a \emph{global} property: ``topology comes in''. To connect curvature and topology of a surface one needs the Gauss--Bonnet theorem \ts{17}{1}{18:24}\ts{17}{1}{18:45}\ts{17}{1}{19:05}\ts{17}{1}{19:26}\ts{17}{1}{19:46}\ts{17}{1}{20:06}\ts{17}{1}{20:31}.
\begin{coursenote}[Student question: did this confuse people, historically?]
A student remarks that intuitively one would say that squeezed into space this is a space of positive curvature, with local convergence, ``if you want to understand lensing''. Werner: ``I was certainly very surprised when I saw it first; I think people had not thought about this in these terms before.'' For lensing, he says, \emph{the first paper to do it is Gibbons and Werner, 2008} (as said in class, not checked); ``nobody looked at it before? not like this, no''. The reason: in astronomy people do not look at it from the general-relativity point of view; they start with a Euclidean space (the universe is spatially flat as far as one can tell) with very localised lenses, so one does not need all this machinery, and one can do a lot of lensing without it (the standard formalism is shown at the end of the lecture). The natural arena for lensing is null geodesics in spacetime, which is again more complicated: Capozziello, for example, did a lot of work on Fermat's principle in Lorentzian spacetimes (as said in class, not checked); the approach of this lecture is ``kind of in between'': general relativistic, but spatial, and more geometrical than the standard astronomers' formalism \ts{17}{1}{20:31}\ts{17}{1}{20:52}\ts{17}{1}{21:13}\ts{17}{1}{21:33}\ts{17}{1}{21:53}\ts{17}{1}{22:14}.
\end{coursenote}
So the answer is: \emph{lensing is intrinsically global}. ``It sounds rather wishy-washy, I suppose it is if I say it like that, but I will show you that the topological contribution for Schwarzschild is \emph{essential} for multiple images to occur'' \ts{17}{1}{22:34}\ts{17}{1}{22:55}\ts{17}{1}{23:24}.

\section{The Gauss--Bonnet theorem}\label{sec:c17-gb}

``Gauss--Bonnet: that is the last topic of these lectures, except that at the end I will show you a little bit about the framework in astrophysics as well'' \ts{17}{1}{23:24}\ts{17}{1}{23:44}.

\paragraph{Setting.} Consider the optical geometry of a \emph{static spherically symmetric} spacetime, not necessarily in vacuum (in vacuum it is Schwarzschild, by Birkhoff's theorem; outside vacuum one can consider, e.g., a fluid with these symmetries, and the field equations take a special form, the Tolman--Oppenheimer--Volkoff equations, ``in his paper if you are interested''; in these lectures Schwarzschild is used). By spherical symmetry one restricts to the equatorial plane (or any plane through the origin): a geodesically complete surface with metric $\tilde g$, in the standard spherical coordinates. In it consider a region $A$ whose boundary $\partial A$ is \emph{piecewise smooth} \ts{17}{1}{23:44}\ts{17}{1}{24:04}\ts{17}{1}{24:31}\ts{17}{1}{24:51}\ts{17}{1}{25:13}\ts{17}{1}{25:34}\ts{17}{1}{25:55}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the data of Gauss--Bonnet]\label{def:c17-gbdata}
The boundary $\partial A$ consists of smooth boundary \textbf{curves} $C$, parametrised by $t$ with unit speed, $\tilde g(\dot C,\dot C)=1$, and of \textbf{vertices} $V$ (finitely many; otherwise it is smooth). At a vertex the tangent vector jumps: the angle between the incoming and the outgoing tangent vectors is the \textbf{jump angle}; one uses the \textbf{exterior jump angle} $\varepsilon$, measured outside the region (the interior angle $\theta$ is related to it by $\varepsilon=\pi-\theta$). The region may have holes, and its topology is described by the \textbf{Euler characteristic} $\chi$ (``which you might be familiar with''): for a topological disc with $g$ holes (``a disc with holes'') $\chi=1-g$. The metric quantities are measured with the optical metric $\tilde g$ (``that is the only metric we have here''): the \textbf{Gaussian curvature} $K$ of the surface in $A$ (varying from point to point) and the \textbf{geodesic curvature} $\kappa$ of the smooth parts of the boundary, defined as the length of the covariant derivative of the boundary curve along its own tangent vector,
\[
  \kappa=\bigl\lVert\nabla_{\dot C}\dot C\bigr\rVert_{\tilde g}
\]
(``the acceleration''; one sees at once that the geodesic curvature of a geodesic is zero: $\kappa$ measures to what extent a curve is \emph{not} a geodesic; the name ``geodesic curvature'' as said in class).
\end{definition}
\ts{17}{1}{25:55}\ts{17}{1}{26:24}\ts{17}{1}{26:44}\ts{17}{1}{27:06}\ts{17}{1}{27:29}\ts{17}{1}{27:53}\ts{17}{1}{28:13}\ts{17}{1}{28:41}\ts{17}{1}{29:08}\ts{17}{1}{29:29}\ts{17}{1}{29:50}\ts{17}{1}{30:12}
\begin{coursenote}[Student question: what is all this, in easy words? A picture]
On the blackboard: the equatorial plane in the optical metric, a bounded region $A$ with boundary $\partial A$ consisting of two smooth bits $C_1$ and $C_2$ and two vertices $V_1$ and $V_2$; one moves round the boundary in a \emph{consistent orientation}, the tangent vector of $C_1$ at $V_2$ and the next tangent vector, of $C_2$ at the same vertex, start in a different direction: the angle between them is the (exterior) jump angle $\varepsilon_2$; similarly at $V_1$ there is the jump from $C_2$ to $C_1$ with $\varepsilon_1$. The surface in $A$ has a Gaussian curvature, the smooth part of the boundary has a geodesic curvature, and there may be holes: a hole needs its own boundary curve, which is also part of the boundary; ``only holes in the region count, not much on the hole''. The region lives \emph{on the surface}, in the embedding: ``it has a curvature, which might possibly vary from point to point''. All the metric quantities, including the angles, are measured with respect to $\tilde g$ \ts{17}{1}{30:32}\ts{17}{1}{30:53}\ts{17}{1}{31:14}\ts{17}{1}{31:34}\ts{17}{1}{31:54}\ts{17}{1}{32:15}\ts{17}{1}{32:37}\ts{17}{1}{32:58}\ts{17}{1}{33:19}\ts{17}{1}{33:39}\ts{17}{1}{34:00}.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{theorem}[Gauss--Bonnet]\label{thm:c17-gb}
For a compact region $A$ with piecewise smooth boundary on a surface,
\[
  \int_AK\,\mathrm dA+\int_{\partial A}\kappa\,\mathrm dt+\sum_{V}\varepsilon_V=2\pi\,\chi(A),
\]
the first integral over the region, the second over the smooth parts of the boundary, the sum over the exterior jump angles at the vertices.
\end{theorem}
\ts{17}{1}{34:00}\ts{17}{1}{34:23}\ts{17}{1}{34:44}\ts{17}{1}{35:06}
``It is a beautiful theorem which connects local properties of the surface, the Gaussian curvature, with global properties, in particular with the Euler characteristic, the number of holes; but of course we have to take care of the boundary as well.'' A student asks whether the set of vertices is assumed finite: yes, finitely many, ``otherwise it is smooth, so it is piecewise smooth'' \ts{17}{1}{34:23}\ts{17}{1}{35:06}.
\begin{coursenote}[Student question: is there an intuitive explanation? A check without boundary]
``It is a beautiful theorem; it is not easy to derive, I could not just cough up a derivation immediately''; no proof is given. To motivate it: for a surface \emph{without boundary}, e.g.\ $S^2$, the theorem says $\int_AK\,\mathrm dA=2\pi\chi$. For the unit sphere $K=1$ and the integral is $4\pi$, so $4\pi=2\pi\chi$ and $\chi=2$, indeed the Euler characteristic of a sphere. The formulas differ for a disc with boundary and a closed surface: for a topological disc with $g$ holes $\chi=1-g$ (it has a boundary curve); for a closed surface without boundary $\chi=2-2g$ with $g$ the number of holes (``handles''), which is $2$ for the sphere ($g=0$). If one deforms the sphere topologically, the Euler characteristic stays the same \ts{17}{1}{46:59}\ts{17}{1}{47:21}\ts{17}{1}{47:43}\ts{17}{1}{48:03}\ts{17}{1}{48:24}\ts{17}{1}{48:45}\ts{17}{1}{49:06}\ts{17}{1}{49:30}.
\end{coursenote}
\begin{coursenote}[Student question: what is the boundary of $S^2$?]
A student asks whether the boundary of $S^2$ is perhaps $S^2$ itself, since removing a point leaves parts of $S^2$ and ``not $S^2$'' all around it. Answer: ``in the standard sense of a surface, a manifold without boundary: you do not have a boundary curve that bounds $S^2$, of course; that is what we need'' \ts{17}{1}{49:51}\ts{17}{1}{50:11}.
\end{coursenote}

Two constructions follow: the first shows how the \emph{topology} impinges on the number of lens images; the second computes the \emph{deflection angle}, ``finally the thing that I have been rambling on about for the last two lectures'', from a very different point of view from the textbooks, which always work out the deflection angle in the same way \ts{17}{1}{35:33}\ts{17}{1}{35:53}.

\section{Topology and multiple images}\label{sec:c17-topology}

Let there be a light source $S$ and an observer $O$ and suppose there are two images: two light rays $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ connecting $S$ and $O$ (they are geodesics, hence $\kappa=0$ on them). As the Gauss--Bonnet region take the one \emph{between} them, with $S$ and $O$ as the two vertices: but for Schwarzschild the region has a \emph{hole}, the throat of chapter~\ref{ch:c16}, and one needs an inner boundary curve for it. It is convenient to take the \textbf{photon orbit} $r=3M$, the circular photon orbit that one has seen before: it is a geodesic too (the circular orbit; the one at the throat of the embedding diagram), so $\kappa=0$ along it, ``which makes computing the geodesic curvature in this case very easy''. Call it $\gamma_P$. The picture is ``looking into the throat from above'': the $z$ coordinate of the embedding of chapter~\ref{ch:c16} comes out of the blackboard, and the region $A$ encloses the throat. A consistent orientation is needed for Gauss--Bonnet: $\gamma_2$ is reversed \ts{17}{1}{35:53}\ts{17}{1}{36:14}\ts{17}{1}{36:36}\ts{17}{1}{36:58}\ts{17}{1}{37:20}\ts{17}{1}{37:40}\ts{17}{1}{38:00}\ts{17}{1}{38:23}\ts{17}{1}{38:43}\ts{17}{1}{39:04}.

Can such a setup exist? The two rays must \emph{converge} at $S$ and at $O$, as drawn: the interior angles $\theta_S$ and $\theta_O$ at the vertices (related to the exterior jump angles by $\varepsilon=\pi-\theta$) must be positive. Apply \cref{thm:c17-gb} to the region with $G$ holes ($\chi=1-G$; for Schwarzschild $G=1$), and $\kappa=0$ on all smooth parts of the boundary ($\gamma_1,\gamma_2$ light rays, $\gamma_P$ the photon orbit):
\[
  \int_AK\,\mathrm dA+\varepsilon_S+\varepsilon_O=2\pi(1-G).
\]
With $\varepsilon_S+\varepsilon_O=2\pi-(\theta_S+\theta_O)$ this gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c17-images}
  \theta_S+\theta_O=2\pi G+\int_AK\,\mathrm dA.
\end{equation}
(This is the content of the lecture's computation, which is written on the board in a rather suggestive and not fully legible form: the sum of the two interior angles is ``two half angles'', $2\pi=\pi+\pi$; one student reminds that the individual angles are $\pi-\varepsilon_S$ and $\pi-\varepsilon_O$. The relation as written here is reconstructed and agrees with the lecture's conclusion.) So the setup is possible only if
\[
  2\pi G+\int_AK\,\mathrm dA>0.
\]
\ts{17}{1}{39:04}\ts{17}{1}{39:26}\ts{17}{1}{39:47}\ts{17}{1}{40:11}\ts{17}{1}{40:33}\ts{17}{1}{40:54}\ts{17}{1}{41:14}\ts{17}{1}{41:36}\ts{17}{1}{41:59}\ts{17}{1}{42:21}\ts{17}{1}{42:44}\ts{17}{1}{43:04}\ts{17}{1}{43:25}
For Schwarzschild $K<0$ \emph{everywhere}, so $\int_AK\,\mathrm dA<0$, and the inequality can hold only if $G>0$: $G\geq1$ is an integer, one in the case of one Schwarzschild black hole. This is the \emph{topological contribution}: the two images in the Schwarzschild solution are possible only because the topology is non-trivial, because of the hole (``if the topology were trivial the inequality would fail''). So in this case \textbf{the topological contribution is essential for multiple lens images to occur}. Conversely, the topology of the throat was ``forced upon us'': the optical metric cannot be put on a space without a hole (a student asks: ``is that right?'' --- ``I think that is right'') \ts{17}{1}{43:25}\ts{17}{1}{43:45}\ts{17}{1}{44:07}\ts{17}{1}{44:28}\ts{17}{1}{44:55}.
\begin{coursenote}[Student question: and a massive body of the radius of the throat that is not a black hole?]
``I will come to this.'' In the Schwarzschild case it is essential because there is a photon orbit, a horizon; ``if we do not have a photon orbit, we do not have a horizon, the situation will change''. For a galaxy, a mass distribution that is static, spherically symmetric but not Schwarzschild, the embedding diagram does not have the throat: it just bulges out; and the Gaussian curvature can be positive somewhere, so the sign of $K$ changes and one can have other images because of that. In the more general case the topological contribution is \emph{not essential} but it comes in; ``this is the precise meaning of what I meant earlier: lensing is intrinsically a global effect'' \ts{17}{1}{45:16}\ts{17}{1}{45:36}\ts{17}{1}{45:57}\ts{17}{1}{46:17}\ts{17}{1}{46:39}.
\end{coursenote}
\begin{coursenote}[Student question: rays that stay far from the throat; windings]
A student remarks that two geodesics that stay far away from the throat still diverge and converge, so the earlier statement is just local. Answer: ``this is all relativistic, remember'': one could consider, in principle (it is not done here), geodesics wrapping around the throat and rotating several times before moving back out; ``I have considered the trivial case because that is actually what is going on in nature'': the light rays pass the lens galaxy at distances of thousands of parsecs. Even in the trivial example the topological contribution comes in. Windings have never been observed, as far as the lecturer knows; one would tell them apart by the different lensing properties, because to be observed the geodesics must start at an emission event and end at the observer, an additional geometrical constraint \ts{17}{1}{50:11}\ts{17}{1}{50:31}\ts{17}{1}{50:52}\ts{17}{1}{51:12}\ts{17}{1}{51:32}\ts{17}{1}{51:53}\ts{17}{1}{52:15}.
\end{coursenote}

\section{The deflection angle from Gauss--Bonnet}\label{sec:c17-deflection}

``Let us now use Gauss--Bonnet to actually work out the deflection angle, in the trivial case where the light ray passes the black hole at great distance'' \ts{17}{1}{52:15}.

\paragraph{Setting.} A light source $S$, a lens $L$ somewhere (it might have a photon sphere, but one is far from it) and an observer $O$; the actual ray $\gamma$ is slightly deflected. Take the \emph{undeflected} ray for reference, passing the lens at the coordinate distance $b$, the \textbf{impact parameter} (far from the black hole Schwarzschild coordinates can be used). The region $A_R$ is bounded by the light ray $\gamma$ and by a circular arc $C$ that connects $O$ back to $S$ (not a light ray). To make life easy: the distance from $S$ to $L$ equals the distance from $L$ to $O$, called $R$, and $C$ is the arc $r(t)=R$ (constant), with $\phi$ going from $-\alpha$ to $\pi$ (the polar axis along the asymptotic direction): this angle $\alpha$ will turn out to be the \emph{asymptotic deflection angle}. Eventually $S\to\infty$ in one direction and $O\to\infty$ in the other, so one needs $2M\ll b\ll R$, and in the limit the statement is exact. Then the optical metric tends to the Euclidean metric of the surface in polar coordinates, $\mathrm dt^2\to\mathrm dr^2+r^2\mathrm d\phi^2$ (``cosmology is ignored in this set-up: the lens is somewhere in the expanding universe, so it is really conformally Euclidean, and this can be changed easily; if one is pedantic one says $R$ is large but less than the Hubble scale'') \ts{17}{1}{52:35}\ts{17}{1}{52:55}\ts{17}{1}{53:17}\ts{17}{1}{53:38}\ts{17}{1}{53:59}\ts{17}{1}{54:20}\ts{17}{1}{54:40}\ts{17}{1}{55:01}\ts{17}{1}{55:22}\ts{17}{1}{55:42}\ts{17}{1}{56:04}\ts{17}{1}{56:27}\ts{17}{1}{56:48}.

\paragraph{Applying Gauss--Bonnet to $A_R$.} It has no hole (the throat is not inside the region: topologically a disc), so $\chi=1$, and the right-hand side is $2\pi$ \ts{17}{1}{56:48}\ts{17}{1}{57:09}\ts{17}{1}{57:39}.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Geodesic curvature of the light ray:} zero, it is a geodesic.
  \item \emph{Geodesic curvature of the arc $C$:} one has to work out the geodesic curvature of a circular arc in essentially flat Euclidean geometry; ``by the way, what are the non-vanishing connection components?'': for $\mathrm dt^2=\mathrm dr^2+r^2\mathrm d\phi^2$ the metric has the special form of chapter~\ref{ch:c16} with $f(r)=r$, so $\Gamma^r{}_{\phi\phi}=-f f'=-r$ and $\Gamma^\phi{}_{r\phi}=f'/f=1/r$. The curve is $r=R$, $\phi=t/R$ (unit speed): by circular symmetry the tangent vector has only a $\phi$ component, and the unit-speed condition $g_{\phi\phi}\dot C^\phi\dot C^\phi=1$ with $g_{\phi\phi}=R^2$ gives $\dot C^\phi=1/R$. Its components are constant, so in the covariant derivative the partial derivatives vanish and only the connection term remains: the only non-vanishing component of $\nabla_{\dot C}\dot C$ is the radial one,
    \[
      \bigl(\nabla_{\dot C}\dot C\bigr)^r=\Gamma^r{}_{\phi\phi}\,\dot C^\phi\dot C^\phi=-R\cdot\frac1{R^2}=-\frac1R,
    \]
    and since the metric is Euclidean in the radial direction its length is $\kappa=1/R$ (``perfectly obvious anyway: the geodesic curvature of a circle in Euclidean space is one over the radius; as the radius goes to infinity you get a straight line, which is a geodesic: $\kappa\to0$, as expected'') \ts{17}{1}{57:39}\ts{17}{1}{58:01}\ts{17}{1}{58:22}\ts{17}{1}{58:45}\ts{17}{1}{59:15}\ts{17}{1}{59:35}\ts{17}{1}{59:57}\ts{17}{1}{1:00:18}\ts{17}{1}{1:00:38}\ts{17}{1}{1:00:58}\ts{17}{1}{1:01:19}\ts{17}{1}{1:01:40}\ts{17}{1}{1:02:01}\ts{17}{1}{1:02:21}\ts{17}{1}{1:02:42}\ts{17}{1}{1:03:02}.
  \item \emph{The integral along $C$:} with $\mathrm dt=R\,\mathrm d\phi$ on $r=R$,
    \[
      \int_C\kappa\,\mathrm dt=\int_{-\alpha}^{\pi}\frac1R\,R\,\mathrm d\phi=\pi+\alpha.
    \]
  \item \emph{The jump angles:} the two vertices are $S$ and $O$, where a circular arc meets essentially radial arcs; as $R\to\infty$ they become orthogonal, so the two exterior jump angles tend to $\pi/2$ each and their sum to $\pi$. At the light ray itself there is \emph{no jump angle}: it is a smooth light ray (``it is just curved''); astronomers, who treat the light ray as straight lines in Euclidean space, have a kink there, but here, in a general-relativistic setting, it is a smooth ray, ``we just take it as it is'' \ts{17}{1}{1:03:23}\ts{17}{1}{1:03:46}\ts{17}{1}{1:04:06}\ts{17}{1}{1:04:27}\ts{17}{1}{1:04:47}\ts{17}{1}{1:05:07}\ts{17}{1}{1:05:27}\ts{17}{1}{1:05:48}\ts{17}{1}{1:06:08}.
\end{enumerate}
In the limit $R\to\infty$ the region becomes $A_\infty$, the region bounded by the (infinite) light ray, excluding the lens and extending to infinity, and Gauss--Bonnet reads
\[
  \int_{A_\infty}K\,\mathrm dA+(\pi+\alpha)+\pi=2\pi,
\]
that is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c17-alpha}
  \alpha=-\int_{A_\infty}K\,\mathrm dA.
\end{equation}
(A student asks about $\alpha\to0$ as one moves towards infinity; the region $A_\infty$ is ``the region bounded by the infinite light ray, excluding the lens, going all the way off to infinity''.) ``We have not made any approximation: \emph{this equation is exact for the deflection angle in the case of a static spherically symmetric lens}.'' \ts{17}{1}{1:06:08}\ts{17}{1}{1:06:29}\ts{17}{1}{1:06:50}

\begin{remark}[A surprising equation]\label{rem:c17-global}
Normally one says that a mass deflects light and thinks that the deflection depends on the mass that is \emph{enclosed} within the impact parameter: the more mass inside, the larger the deflection angle, and indeed it is proportional to $M$, as one saw yesterday. But \eqref{eq:c17-alpha} lets one look the other way: one works out a quantity integrated from the light ray off to infinity, \emph{excluding} the lens; one does not look at what is enclosed within the impact parameter but at what is outside. ``How can it be possible that you get the same thing? Again, it tells you it is a global effect.'' The deflection angle is determined by the integral of the Gaussian curvature of the optical metric outside the light ray; this underscores again that lensing is intrinsically global, ``in some sense saying the same thing'' as the topological result \ts{17}{1}{1:07:13}\ts{17}{1}{1:07:33}\ts{17}{1}{1:07:54}\ts{17}{1}{1:08:14}\ts{17}{1}{1:08:35}\ts{17}{1}{1:08:56}\ts{17}{1}{1:09:16}.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{How to compute it: iteratively.} The region $A_\infty$ already contains the answer, since it is bounded by the actual light ray. One computes iteratively: first take as boundary the \emph{undeflected} ray, get a value of $\alpha$ proportional to $M$; this gives a slightly better ray, and with it a better boundary, which gives third-order corrections and so on: the expansion in $M$ (``you can think of this as a Taylor expansion in $M$''). The mistake in approximating $A_\infty$ by the region bounded by the fiducial straight ray is small because the optical metric approaches the Euclidean one, so the infinite region does not contribute much. For the \emph{leading order} the first approximation suffices \ts{17}{1}{1:09:16}\ts{17}{1}{1:09:38}\ts{17}{1}{1:09:58}\ts{17}{1}{1:10:25}\ts{17}{1}{1:10:46}\ts{17}{1}{1:11:06}\ts{17}{1}{1:11:27}\ts{17}{1}{1:11:47}.

\begin{example}[The leading-order deflection angle $4M/b$]\label{ex:c17-leading}
Take as boundary the undeflected ray $r(\phi)=b/\sin\phi$ (straight line at distance $b$; $b\gg2M$, so one is in the Euclidean region). The Gaussian curvature of the Schwarzschild optical geometry is \eqref{eq:c16-KS}; for $r\gg M$ the leading term is $K\approx-2M/r^3$. The area element is $\mathrm dA=\sqrt{\det\tilde g}\,\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\phi\approx r\,\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\phi$, with $r$ from $b/\sin\phi$ to infinity and $\phi$ from $0$ to $\pi$. Then
\[\begin{aligned}
  \alpha&\approx-\int_0^\pi\!\!\int_{b/\sin\phi}^\infty\Bigl(-\frac{2M}{r^3}\Bigr)r\,\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\phi\\
  &=2M\int_0^\pi\frac{\sin\phi}{b}\,\mathrm d\phi=\frac{2M}{b}\bigl[-\cos\phi\bigr]_0^\pi=\frac{4M}{b}.
\end{aligned}\]
(The signs are sorted out on the board in a few steps: ``there is a minus here and that gives us minus minus one, which is one, so that gives us two'': the factor $2$ is the value of $\int_0^\pi\sin\phi\,\mathrm d\phi$.) In physical units, with $M$ standing for $GM/c^2$,
\[
  \alpha\approx\frac{4GM}{b\,c^2},
\]
the leading-order deflection angle of light in general relativity, ``what I have written down yesterday'': for Schwarzschild and indeed for any compact mass if one is sufficiently far away \ts{17}{1}{1:11:47}\ts{17}{1}{1:12:10}\ts{17}{1}{1:12:30}\ts{17}{1}{1:12:51}\ts{17}{1}{1:13:14}\ts{17}{1}{1:13:34}\ts{17}{1}{1:13:55}\ts{17}{1}{1:14:16}\ts{17}{1}{1:14:37}\ts{17}{1}{1:14:57}\ts{17}{1}{1:15:17}\ts{17}{1}{1:15:38}\ts{17}{1}{1:15:58}\ts{17}{1}{1:16:19}\ts{17}{1}{1:16:43}\ts{17}{1}{1:17:04}\ts{17}{1}{1:17:24}.
\end{example}
Is it the best way to compute it in practice? ``I agree it is maybe not the best way, because you actually have to go to curvature, to the second derivative of a metric, and solve it iteratively; but I think conceptually it is interesting, because it really brings out that lensing is a global effect'' \ts{17}{1}{1:17:24}\ts{17}{1}{1:17:45}.

\section{Beyond Schwarzschild: the singular isothermal sphere and the cosmic string}\label{sec:c17-sis}

In the last minutes (``sorry, I am overrunning already''), one example that is not Schwarzschild but a model for a galaxy that astronomers like to use: the \textbf{singular isothermal sphere} \ts{17}{1}{1:17:45}\ts{17}{1}{1:18:05}\ts{17}{1}{1:18:58}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: the singular isothermal sphere]\label{def:c17-sis}
A Newtonian mass model in which every massive particle has the same kinetic temperature (hence \emph{isothermal}), in a self-consistent equilibrium satisfying Poisson's equation: the spatial density is
\[
  \rho(r)=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\pi G\,r^2},
\]
where $\sigma^2$ is the velocity dispersion of the system of particles (hundreds of kilometres per second, so $\sigma\ll c$: ``this is not relativistic''). \emph{Singular} because the density blows up at the centre, ``but it does not matter too much, we can smooth it''. It describes a ball of massive point particles in thermodynamic equilibrium, a stellar-kinematics version of a galaxy, and is ``not a bad model for a galaxy''.
\end{definition}
\ts{17}{1}{1:18:58}\ts{17}{1}{1:19:18}\ts{17}{1}{1:19:39}\ts{17}{1}{1:19:59}\ts{17}{1}{1:20:20}
With the machinery of the lectures one could work out the energy-momentum tensor and the optical geometry; a student mentions Tolman--Oppenheimer--Volkoff, and the lecturer says there is a general-relativistic version of this model by Misner and Novikov [as said in class, not checked]; but one is looking at lensing in the Newtonian limit: one plugs in the Newtonian mass distribution and from general relativity one just inherits the factor of two in the deflection. The result: the optical geometry of the equatorial plane, embedded in $\mathbb R^3$ as before, is a \textbf{cone} (at large scales; the lecture gives $z\approx\sqrt{8\sigma^2}\,R$ [reading of the captions, not checked]; the mass may be cut off, in which case outside the cut-off the optical geometry looks like Schwarzschild, inside like the cone). Hence the Gaussian curvature is zero (a cone is flat). What does this say for the deflection angle? Not zero: the angle of the cone, the \textbf{deficit angle} (cut a sector out of a disc and glue the edges to get a cone: the angle removed is the deficit angle) \ts{17}{1}{1:20:40}\ts{17}{1}{1:21:01}\ts{17}{1}{1:21:21}\ts{17}{1}{1:21:42}\ts{17}{1}{1:22:04}\ts{17}{1}{1:22:30}\ts{17}{1}{1:22:50}\ts{17}{1}{1:23:16}.

The Gauss--Bonnet argument above was for a spacetime whose optical geometry approaches \emph{Euclidean} space at infinity; here it is flat but conic, so the geodesic curvature of the arc $C$ differs and $\alpha$ is not zero, and the formula \eqref{eq:c17-alpha} has to be modified. The leading-order result is
\[
  \alpha=4\pi\sigma^2\quad(\text{in units }c=1),
\]
which is \emph{half the deficit angle} of the cone (``on a cone the geodesics that correspond to light rays are straight lines, the Gaussian curvature is zero, except that there is this conic structure''). So the deflection angle for a singular isothermal sphere is \emph{constant}, independent of distance (the mass enclosed within the impact parameter is proportional to $r$, and the deflection angle is something like the enclosed mass over $r$: ``it is not surprising that it is constant'') \ts{17}{1}{1:23:16}\ts{17}{1}{1:23:37}\ts{17}{1}{1:23:57}\ts{17}{1}{1:24:18}\ts{17}{1}{1:24:38}\ts{17}{1}{1:24:59}\ts{17}{1}{1:25:19}.
\begin{coursenote}[A check of the factor]
(Not said in class.) A cone $z=aR$ with $a^2=8\sigma^2\ll1$ has deficit angle $2\pi\bigl(1-1/\sqrt{1+a^2}\bigr)\approx\pi a^2=8\pi\sigma^2$, whose half is $4\pi\sigma^2$, consistent with the value given.
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{The cosmic string.} Another spacetime, ``that you might have met before'' in the tutorials (a student is not sure): a \textbf{cosmic string} is a curve that carries mass in spacetime: an energy-momentum tensor with mass along some curve in space; the solution of Einstein's equations is a \emph{conic spacetime} with a deficit angle (here the deficit angle is in the spacetime, not just in space). The light deflection there is exactly the same relationship: half the deficit angle, again constant. So there is a formal similarity between a cosmic string and a singular isothermal sphere, two very different models. The main difference: for the \emph{cosmic string the spacetime is conic}, hence locally has no curvature (the curvature is zero) and the light rays are \emph{not magnified}; for the singular isothermal sphere only the \emph{optical geometry} is conic and the spacetime itself is curved, so there is \emph{magnification}. In the lens image the isothermal sphere gives a shifted image (by the constant $\alpha$) that is brighter, while the cosmic string gives a shifted image with exactly the same brightness \ts{17}{1}{1:25:19}\ts{17}{1}{1:25:40}\ts{17}{1}{1:26:00}\ts{17}{1}{1:26:21}\ts{17}{1}{1:26:42}\ts{17}{1}{1:27:03}\ts{17}{1}{1:27:24}\ts{17}{1}{1:27:45}\ts{17}{1}{1:28:06}.

\section{The odd-number theorem: Fermat potential and Morse theory}\label{sec:c17-morse}

The last question of the session, from a student: yesterday it was mentioned that there is always an \emph{odd number of images}; why? ``It would be another lecture, I am afraid; I can give you a very quick argument'' \ts{17}{1}{1:28:06}\ts{17}{1}{1:28:26}\ts{17}{1}{1:28:50}.

\paragraph{The standard lens-plane set-up.} In the standard approximation of lensing one has a light source, an observer and a lens; Euclidean space with a \emph{lens plane} at which the (otherwise straight) light rays get a kink, so they are piecewise straight. All possible curves from $S$ to $O$ are characterised by where they intersect the lens plane, with coordinates $x$; the source is in a source plane with coordinates $y$ (a fixed parameter). The actual rays are, by Fermat's principle (here in the standard optics sense: stationary travel time), the curves for which the \textbf{Fermat potential} $\Phi(x;y)$, the total travel time as a function of the lens-plane coordinate, is stationary,
\[
  \nabla_x\Phi=0.
\]
$\Phi$ is the sum of the \emph{geometric time delay}, which comes from the different lengths of the paths, and the \emph{gravitational time delay} (the Shapiro effect: ``the potential time delay, the Shapiro effect, eventually''), as said in class: the lecture describes $\Phi$ ``the deflection potential'' in words only \ts{17}{1}{1:28:50}\ts{17}{1}{1:29:11}\ts{17}{1}{1:29:32}\ts{17}{1}{1:29:53}\ts{17}{1}{1:30:14}\ts{17}{1}{1:30:34}\ts{17}{1}{1:30:57}.

Plot $\Phi$ as a function of the lens-plane coordinate: a surface. For an isolated lens all images, the stationary points, lie in some finite region, and at large distance the gravitational potential diverges at most logarithmically, so $\Phi$ is dominated by the geometric (quadratic) term: far out the surface looks like a \emph{paraboloid}. \textbf{Counting images is counting critical points} of this surface, of three types: minima, saddle points and maxima \ts{17}{1}{1:30:57}\ts{17}{1}{1:31:17}\ts{17}{1}{1:31:38}\ts{17}{1}{1:31:59}.

\begin{theorem}[Morse]\label{thm:c17-morse}
Let $\mathcal M$ be a closed manifold (compact, without boundary) of dimension $D$, and $f$ a function on it with only regular (non-degenerate) critical points. Let $n_\lambda$ be the number of critical points of \emph{Morse index} $\lambda$. Then
\[
  \sum_{\lambda=0}^{D}(-1)^\lambda\,n_\lambda=\chi(\mathcal M).
\]
\end{theorem}
\ts{17}{1}{1:32:19}\ts{17}{1}{1:32:39}\ts{17}{1}{1:33:00}\ts{17}{1}{1:33:20}
The Morse index of a minimum is $0$, of a saddle point $1$, of a maximum $2$ (in $D=2$; ``is that right? I think it is right; no, sorry, it is the other way round: $2,1,0$; no, sorry, it is $0,1,2$'': the lecturer hesitates, the correct assignment is the one given here, number of negative directions of the Hessian). No proof is given \ts{17}{1}{1:32:39}\ts{17}{1}{1:33:00}\ts{17}{1}{1:33:20}.

\begin{proposition}[The odd-number theorem of lensing]\label{prop:c17-odd}
For an isolated lens with a smooth finite mass distribution, the total number of images is odd.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Close the paraboloid-like surface of the Fermat potential, by adding a point at infinity, to a compact manifold without boundary, a topological sphere $S^2$ ($D=2$, $\chi=2$). By doing this one changes the problem ``up here, not down there'' and introduces exactly one additional critical point, a maximum at the top, which does not correspond to a lens image. Morse's theorem gives
\[
  n_{\min}-n_{\mathrm{sad}}+\bigl(n_{\max}+1\bigr)=2,
\]
where $n_{\min},n_{\mathrm{sad}},n_{\max}$ now count the minima, saddles, and maxima that are images, and the $+1$ is the additional maximum. Hence $n_{\min}+n_{\max}=n_{\mathrm{sad}}+1$ and the total number of images is
\[
  N=n_{\min}+n_{\mathrm{sad}}+n_{\max}=2\,n_{\mathrm{sad}}+1,
\]
an odd number. (``Magically, you get that the total number of images is twice the number of saddle points plus one.'') \ts{17}{1}{1:33:40}\ts{17}{1}{1:34:02}\ts{17}{1}{1:34:23}\ts{17}{1}{1:34:44}
\end{proof}
\begin{coursenote}[Student questions on the extra maximum and on the Euler characteristic]
A student asks why one has to add a $+1$ on the left, if two is the Morse index of a maximum: the $+1$ is not the index but the \emph{number} of critical points of that index (the number of maxima among the images, plus the additional maximum that is not an image). Another asks whether it is not the index plus one: ``no, it is two: we discussed this before: for a topological disc $\chi=1-g$; for a manifold without boundary like this, $\chi=2-2g$; no hole means $g=0$'' (the lecturer says ``$g$ equal to two'' and means zero). Check with a sphere and a height function: one minimum (south pole) and one maximum (north pole), $1\cdot(-1)^0+1\cdot(-1)^2=2=\chi(S^2)$ \ts{17}{1}{1:34:44}\ts{17}{1}{1:35:04}\ts{17}{1}{1:35:25}\ts{17}{1}{1:35:46}\ts{17}{1}{1:36:06}.
\end{coursenote}
This holds as long as the mass distribution is \emph{finite and smooth}, so that all images lie in a compact region. For a point mass (Schwarzschild) it does not work: there are two images, because of the singularity, the potential looks like a logarithm; ``that is not really what happens in nature: in nature you have a nice smooth mass distribution, which is finite''. In real life typically one of the images cannot be seen, either because the galaxy is in front of it or because it is strongly demagnified, so the odd-number theorem becomes an \emph{even-number} theorem: for example the \emph{Einstein cross}, with four images \ts{17}{1}{1:36:06}\ts{17}{1}{1:36:27}\ts{17}{1}{1:36:47}\ts{17}{1}{1:37:07}\ts{17}{1}{1:37:28}.
\begin{coursenote}[Student question: only one plane?]
A student remarks that everything has been a two-dimensional set-up, with everything in one plane. Right: one can consider more lenses in other positions and distances (a multi-lens-plane setting) and extend the argument, ``but it does not quite work like this'': one has to use Morse theory with boundary conditions $A$ and $B$ (a multi-dimensional version of the lens equation), still giving constraints on the number of images \ts{17}{1}{1:37:48}\ts{17}{1}{1:38:10}\ts{17}{1}{1:38:30}\ts{17}{1}{1:38:51}.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{keyconcepts}
\textbf{Retrieval questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Derive the geodesic deviation equation $\mathrm D^2X/\mathrm dt^2=-KX$ on a surface. What does the sign of $K$ say about neighbouring geodesics? What are the conditions on the connecting vector field?

  \item What are the optical geometries of the singular isothermal sphere and the cosmic string, and what is the deflection? Which one magnifies?
