\section{Why derive the Einstein action}\label{sec:c26-why}

``Welcome to the actually last central lecture of the winter school'' \ts{26}{1}{0:00}\ts{26}{1}{0:20}. Today the lecturer explains what was not explained: \emph{why the Einstein action has the form it has}. The whole structure of the theory was built from set theory over topology on, but the Einstein action, and hence the Einstein equations, were just presented \ts{26}{1}{0:40}.

The textbook view, often taken and ``absolutely correct'', is that the gravitational dynamics are postulated alongside the matter dynamics, such that the result describes nature. But if one postulates several things one must make sure that two of them are not contradictory; if one can be derived from the other, or is strongly constrained by it, ``then we should know'': to understand the theory better, to be sure there is no contradiction, and because such understanding usually points beyond what is known \ts{26}{1}{1:02}\ts{26}{1}{1:23}\ts{26}{1}{1:44}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: quantizable matter]\label{def:c26-quantizable}
The topic is the answer, due to a number of people, to the question how \textbf{quantizable matter} gravitates. It is \emph{not} quantum matter: the description of the matter is classical, but it must be a classical description \emph{that can be quantized}, i.e.\ that satisfies at least some necessary conditions for quantization. ``Today we require that all matter be quantizable'' (how to quantize, and whether this is a well-defined procedure at all, is a complicated question, asked of the audience who had a quantum theory course).
\end{definition}
\ts{26}{1}{2:04}\ts{26}{1}{2:24}\ts{26}{1}{2:45}

The claim: if one starts from a quantizable matter model, for instance Maxwell theory, then the theory \emph{already carries in itself the information how it gravitates}; for Maxwell matter, that gravity looks like Einstein gravity. This will be explained in a general context that also includes other matter producing other gravity theories \ts{26}{1}{3:06}\ts{26}{1}{3:27}. Today is less of the usual style with lots of indices to follow and more about the ideas behind it, though ``you are well prepared'' for the technical details. Gravity can be \emph{derived} from given matter dynamics and need not be postulated; this has a massive impact: if new matter is discovered in the universe, one may have to consider a different gravity theory, and the construction is \emph{constructive}, ``we know exactly what to do to get the new gravity'' \ts{26}{1}{3:48}\ts{26}{1}{4:09}.

\section{The standard picture, 1850 to 1915}\label{sec:c26-history}

The standard model of fundamental physics including gravity, as the school presented it \ts{26}{1}{4:29}. One starts with sets; for classical physics one wants a topology, a smooth atlas, and so on, until one has a smooth manifold, where tensors and tensor fields can be considered. At this stage of development \emph{tensor fields are the only matter one can put on a smooth manifold}; spinors (``who knows what a spinor is? spin one half and so on'') cannot be put on it at this stage, but soon will be \ts{26}{1}{4:50}\ts{26}{1}{5:11}\ts{26}{1}{5:32}.

Now jump back to 1850--1860 and Maxwell \ts{26}{1}{5:32}. There are observations: currents induce magnetic fields (``Ampere or something''), moving magnets induce currents (Faraday); Maxwell combined them and added a little term (the displacement current) \ts{26}{1}{5:53}\ts{26}{1}{6:14}. Of course Maxwell did not think of tensors, smooth atlases, topologies or sets, ``but from a modern perspective that is what he did'': he built on the building so far and wrote down his theory of electromagnetism on a manifold \ts{26}{1}{6:34}.

\begin{history}[Maxwell, Einstein, Minkowski]
As said in class (not checked): in 1905 Einstein stared at the Maxwell equations and realised, with a few years and the help of Minkowski and others, that if they are true (and they ought to be, since they describe all kinds of known phenomena) space carries a geometric structure all by itself, today called a Lorentzian metric, first thought of as a flat one. ``Einstein did not call it a Lorentzian metric, he wrote rather cumbersome equations, but it was a logical conclusion.'' Some 99 years and 11 months ago (the lecture was in 2015) Einstein realised that this metric should also be given dynamics, since its coefficients feature in the Maxwell action: ``you can brutally set them to zero, but you can also give them dynamics''. The dynamics of the background was chosen to capture gravity, so that the Einstein equations relate the Einstein curvature to the energy-momentum tensor of matter and thereby encode how matter gravitates. Dates 1850, 1905, 1915 as said in class.
\end{history}
\ts{26}{1}{6:56}\ts{26}{1}{7:16}\ts{26}{1}{7:37}\ts{26}{1}{7:57}\ts{26}{1}{8:17}\ts{26}{1}{8:38}

The further ingredient is \emph{spinorial matter}: only once there is a Lorentzian metric can one talk about spinors or fermions on spacetime. The famous spin one half is not a quantum mechanical thing: it comes from the representation theory of the isometry group of the flat Lorentzian metric, the Poincar\'e group, ``a geometric thing''; and under certain circumstances (there are obstructions) one can put spinors on a manifold. So the spinors come \emph{after} the Lorentzian metric. Tensorial matter and spinorial matter then describe what is needed: spinorial matter is always fermions (fermionic statistics), tensorial matter encodes bosons \ts{26}{1}{8:58}\ts{26}{1}{9:18}\ts{26}{1}{9:38}\ts{26}{1}{9:59}\ts{26}{1}{10:20}.

Everything that comes after the Lorentzian metric rests on what Einstein read in the Maxwell equations: ``the courageous step was that the Maxwell equations are taken so seriously that what follows from them is the foundation of all of physics''. He was ``very lucky that that is true''; it could have been that Maxwell captures only a little and there is much more out there \ts{26}{1}{10:40}\ts{26}{1}{11:01}. Today all matter is formulated in the standard model of particle physics, with the spinorial matter firmly resting on the Lorentzian metric; this together with gravity is the \textbf{standard model of fundamental physics}, extremely successful for a long time, and the logical path of this course, apart from the spinorial matter which was not done \ts{26}{1}{11:22}\ts{26}{1}{11:43}.

\section{The dark stuff and the plan: redo it for any matter}\label{sec:c26-plan}

Confronted with experiment, as computed in the cosmology lectures (chapters~\ref{ch:c20} and~\ref{ch:c21}) and heard in the lectures of Valeria Pettorino, standard model matter is only about 5\,\% of the matter and energy in the universe, a fact that has been corroborated by observations for some twenty years \ts{26}{1}{11:43}\ts{26}{1}{12:03}\ts{26}{1}{12:23}. In the nice textbook picture the standard model dynamics stand at the same level as Einstein gravity, both postulated and made to work together; but 95\,\% is ``dark stuff'' (``I call it disrespectfully dark stuff because we don't really know where it comes from''). It is dark because it interacts only gravitationally: it couples to the background metric, one assumes, but not to the other standard model matter. ``So most of what is out there is not of standard model type, and by my argument what is standard model type is actually Maxwell type'': one starts with Maxwell, bootstraps the whole standard model, ``because all the matter has been created in the image of Maxwell theory'' \ts{26}{1}{12:44}\ts{26}{1}{13:04}\ts{26}{1}{13:25}.

One logical possibility is that somebody writes the dark stuff down in standard model style, extending the standard model very conservatively, ``which would be preferable'', and the whole picture stays. But we \emph{do not know}, and if we take that seriously, which is the right thing to do, then we must delete from the textbook picture everything that depended on matter working like Maxwell matter: ``virtually all I taught you''. (One can always question more, for instance whether to have a smooth manifold or a topology at all: ``the game here is we only question what we are prompted to question, but what we are prompted to question we take seriously.'') \ts{26}{1}{13:45}\ts{26}{1}{14:06}\ts{26}{1}{14:27}.

So redo the whole thing for \emph{any} matter, new matter? One cannot write down theorems for any matter: there is not enough structure. Assumptions must be made. One could follow taste (strings, or physics done with complex numbers only), but the lecturer prefers a conservative approach: require of matter only what everybody agrees must be required \ts{26}{1}{14:49}\ts{26}{1}{15:09}\ts{26}{1}{15:29}\ts{26}{1}{15:49}.

\begin{definition}[Assumptions on matter: predictive and quantizable]\label{def:c26-assumptions}
A classical matter model is admitted if it is
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{predictive}: initial conditions are translated into later values of the field (``if you write down a theory for dark matter and it is not predictive it would be rejected outright by the physics community, justifiedly, because physics is about predicting the future''); and
  \item \textbf{quantizable}: ``all matter is quantum, at least so we believe''; a classical matter model should at least not obstruct quantization.
\end{enumerate}
How to make these two assumptions technical is explained below.
\end{definition}
\ts{26}{1}{16:09}\ts{26}{1}{16:30}

The announced result: these two assumptions already give rise \emph{uniquely} to a new tensorial geometry, ``not the one described by a Lorentzian metric'' but possibly by other tensors (not every tensor: a Riemannian metric would not do, a Lorentzian one does, and many others). That is ``1905 repeated''. This new geometry provides the coefficients of the matter equations, and one must give it dynamics: one gets new gravitational dynamics (the arrows in the lecture's picture mean ``we know how to do that''). Starting from Maxwell one arrives at Einstein gravity again, so the construction also \emph{explains} why Maxwell matter must end up with Einstein gravity \ts{26}{1}{16:50}\ts{26}{1}{17:10}\ts{26}{1}{17:31}\ts{26}{1}{17:52}\ts{26}{1}{18:12}.

\paragraph{The central result, in the announcement.} One is given predictive and quantizable matter equations in the form of an action, an observer's local matter model (``the bullet cluster'', say, for dark matter). The coefficients it couples to may not look like those of a Lorentzian metric. Example, ``unlikely to be observed directly, to illustrate the point'': if a light ray in vacuum were seen to split into two, general relativity with its Lorentzian metric would be dead. Such splitting is known in the laboratory: in a birefringent crystal a light ray splits into its polarization components; while vacuum is described by a metric, a linear medium is described by a \emph{fourth rank tensor}. If this were seen in the sky one would have to write a modified Maxwell theory with such a structure: ``it is not pure fantasy'' \ts{26}{1}{18:33}\ts{26}{1}{18:54}\ts{26}{1}{19:14}\ts{26}{1}{19:35}\ts{26}{1}{19:56}\ts{26}{1}{20:23}\ts{26}{1}{20:44}.

From the matter action one constructs a \emph{linear homogeneous system of partial differential equations} for the gravitational Lagrangian (``the slight disadvantage that it has infinitely many equations; but that doesn't shock us''): a straightforward algorithm, ``no brain power required''. These are not the equations of motion of the matter. Solving the system, which is trickier, gives the Lagrangian of gravity required as background such that the matter theory is both predictive and quantizable \ts{26}{1}{21:05}\ts{26}{1}{21:25}\ts{26}{1}{21:46}. For the standard model action, or just Maxwell, one gets such a system with particular coefficients; solving it, ``hooray'', the Einstein action comes out, \emph{with the undetermined gravitational constant and cosmological constant appearing as integration constants}. This is the reason, once explained, for the Einstein theory if matter is standard model matter: it need not be postulated separately \ts{26}{1}{22:06}\ts{26}{1}{22:27}\ts{26}{1}{22:47}. In general one puts in what one really sees; if this is the standard model and other stuff, then in total it is a new matter theory and the result is possibly a modification of general relativity \ts{26}{1}{23:08}.

The two steps: first understand the new matter and the spacetime structure that follows from it; then give that structure dynamics \ts{26}{1}{23:31}.

\section{The principal polynomial}\label{sec:c26-principal}

``The key to all of this is something called the principal polynomial of the matter field equations'' \ts{26}{1}{23:31}\ts{26}{1}{23:51}. Field equations like Maxwell's are partial differential equations, coming from an action. Varying the matter action with respect to the matter field $\phi$ gives, always, a PDE for $\phi$ of the form
\[
  Q^{\,}\phi+Q^{A_1}\partial_{A_1}\phi+Q^{A_1A_2}\partial_{A_1}\partial_{A_2}\phi+\dots+Q^{A_1\dots A_N}\partial_{A_1}\cdots\partial_{A_N}\phi=0,
\]
with coefficients $Q$ that may depend on the matter field as well (the equations need not be linear) \ts{26}{1}{24:12}. (This is the form after the correction made in class, see the coursenote.)

\begin{coursenote}[Student question: should the sum start at zero? and are these covariant derivatives?]
A student notices that the sum on the slide starts at one and asks about the zeroth order: ``I do, and this is a mistake that keeps in my slides to check the attention of the audience'': the sum starts at zero, there can be a term without derivatives (the lecturer first muddles it up, then settles that $A_i$ are indices running over the dimension of the manifold, not the order, and the order runs from $0$ to $N$). Another question people always ask: ``don't I have to use covariant derivatives?''; for a tensor field or even a scalar from the second derivative on one would. Answer: these are partial derivatives with coefficients, and the lower coefficients can provide for instance what we call the Christoffel symbols, ``but maybe in a more complicated geometry not coming from a metric one has to generalise the idea of a connection too''; one cannot assume any of that: the point is to \emph{find out} the geometry, not to know it already. The equations come from a matter action and are fully covariant; ``the coefficients carry all the non-triviality'' \ts{26}{1}{24:32}\ts{26}{1}{24:53}\ts{26}{1}{25:13}\ts{26}{1}{25:54}\ts{26}{1}{26:14}\ts{26}{1}{26:34}\ts{26}{1}{26:55}\ts{26}{1}{27:16}.
\end{coursenote}

The great thing that mathematicians found: to understand the causality of the equations (how can signals run, where do I put initial conditions) one does not study the whole equation but only the \emph{highest order coefficient} $Q^{A_1\dots A_N}$ \ts{26}{1}{25:33}\ts{26}{1}{25:54}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: principal polynomial]\label{def:c26-principal}
Insert a covector $k$ in all the indices of the highest order coefficient, $Q^{A_1\dots A_N}k_{A_1}\cdots k_{A_N}$. If $\phi$ is a tensor field with indices, the coefficient $Q$ carries twice the number of indices (one set connecting to $\phi$, one set from the variation), and the extra indices are removed by taking a \textbf{determinant} over them, as instructed by the mathematicians. The result is, at each point $x$ of the manifold, a polynomial $P(x,k)$ on the cotangent space $T_x^*M$: the \textbf{principal polynomial} of the field equations. (``Principal'' is the name for the highest order part, the principal part of the operator.) With a gauge symmetry the construction ``works a little differently, but that is not our concern''.
\end{definition}
\ts{26}{1}{27:16}\ts{26}{1}{27:37}\ts{26}{1}{27:58}\ts{26}{1}{28:18}\ts{26}{1}{28:40}

\begin{example}[Principal polynomial of Maxwell theory]\label{ex:c26-maxwell}
For the Maxwell equations
\[
  P(x,k)=g^{ab}(x)\,k_ak_b ,
\]
the inverse metric contracted twice with $k$ (the expression ``we see very often'').
\end{example}
\ts{26}{1}{29:00}\ts{26}{1}{29:21}

If one knows this polynomial ``one has understood almost everything about the causal structure of the theory''. Fix a point and draw the cotangent space there: the \textbf{null surface} $\{k: P(x,k)=0\}$ is the zero set of the principal polynomial. In Maxwell theory it is the familiar double cone of the $k$ with $g^{ab}k_ak_b=0$. A student asks whether $k$ is related to the generalised momentum of the Lagrangian: ``it is that one'': in the geometric optics limit of the field equations $k$ is the wave covector, i.e.\ the momentum, so to say, of the light ray \ts{26}{1}{29:43}\ts{26}{1}{30:04}\ts{26}{1}{30:24}. This is a key structure: in Maxwell theory it introduces the whole spacetime structure via the cones. For another matter theory the null surface can look more complicated, a pair of nested cones for instance, or any shape, as the zero set of a polynomial coming from your matter equations; then one needs a gravity theory with such a cone \ts{26}{1}{30:45}\ts{26}{1}{31:06}.

\begin{aside}[Superluminal neutrinos]
A while ago it was claimed in the press that neutrinos run faster than light. If this had been true there would be a cone from Maxwell theory (light still being there) and another cone from the neutrinos: ``it is not excluded that somebody could have written down equations'' with a principal polynomial of that structure. People gave interviews to the New York Times saying that then ``we are at absolute zero, we don't know how to do any physics any more''; ``it is not quite so bad''. (The result was wrong, ``because they didn't plug the socket right or something''.) \ts{26}{1}{31:26}\ts{26}{1}{31:47}\ts{26}{1}{32:08}
\end{aside}

\section{The dual polynomial: structures in tangent space}\label{sec:c26-dual}

The structure above lives in the cotangent space; what is wanted is the induced structure in the \emph{tangent} space. The null surface in $T_x^*M$ has gradients: the gradient of a surface in cotangent space is a vector (``in tangent space it would be a covector, that is what I always emphasise, but here we already have one star: we have the star star'': the dual of the cotangent space is the tangent space). Taking at every point $k$ of the surface the gradient and drawing it in the tangent space gives a new shape there. Mathematically this is the \textbf{fiber derivative} of $P$ (the derivative along the cotangent fiber) \ts{26}{1}{32:08}\ts{26}{1}{32:28}\ts{26}{1}{32:49}\ts{26}{1}{33:09}\ts{26}{1}{33:29}. In Maxwell theory the picture in tangent space ``boringly looks very similar'', in other cases more interesting. A student asks what the outside of the cone means (whether one cannot go there): ``we'll come there'' \ts{26}{1}{33:50}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: dual polynomial]\label{def:c26-dual}
These structures contain information about the causal structure of the theory. One can ask whether there is a polynomial $\hat P$ on the \emph{tangent} space whose zero set is this shape; if yes it is called the \textbf{dual polynomial} and is totally determined by $P$. Not always does it exist, but the predictivity condition below ensures that it does if $P$ underlies the theory. In Lorentzian spacetime, if the polynomial in the cotangent space is essentially the inverse metric, the one in the tangent space is essentially the metric; that these two are inverse is ``just an accident of linear algebra''. It should rather be said: the metric is the \emph{dual} of the inverse metric. One can go back and forth, but only with covectors on the surface (they map to vectors on the surface).
\end{definition}
\ts{26}{1}{34:10}\ts{26}{1}{34:31}\ts{26}{1}{34:51}\ts{26}{1}{35:12}\ts{26}{1}{35:33}\ts{26}{1}{35:54}\ts{26}{1}{36:14}

\section{Predictivity and quantizability: bihyperbolicity}\label{sec:c26-bihyp}

Somebody hands you a theory. First check: is it predictive, i.e.\ does it predict from data today the data tomorrow, ``not does it make the right predictions we observe''. ``It needs to go in that order'': first the power to predict, then whether it predicts the right thing \ts{26}{1}{36:14}\ts{26}{1}{36:35}\ts{26}{1}{36:56}. Second, matter is quantum matter. The key result, in words:

\begin{theorem}[Bihyperbolicity, in words]\label{thm:c26-bihyp}
If the matter field dynamics is predictive, the principal polynomial $P$ must be a \textbf{hyperbolic} polynomial in each cotangent space (long known, standard linear PDE theory: predictivity needs hyperbolicity of the principal polynomial). A new result: a necessary condition for \emph{quantizability} is that the dual polynomial $\hat P$ is hyperbolic too (it does by no means follow from the hyperbolicity of $P$). The two together are called \textbf{bihyperbolicity}. Similarly one can argue for point particle dynamics. If a proposed matter theory is not bihyperbolic ``you can immediately throw out the theory''.
\end{theorem}
\ts{26}{1}{37:16}\ts{26}{1}{37:36}\ts{26}{1}{37:56}\ts{26}{1}{38:17}\ts{26}{1}{38:39}\ts{26}{1}{38:59}\ts{26}{1}{49:27}

(The proofs are not given; ``we checked this, we have many theorems proven on that''.)

\begin{definition}[New concepts: hyperbolic polynomial and hyperbolicity cone]\label{def:c26-hyp}
Geometrically: a polynomial $P$ of degree $d$ (its zero set drawn in a vector space, with the origin) is \textbf{hyperbolic} if there exists a direction $h$ such that every line parallel to $h$ (anywhere in the space, ``also the one right over here'') intersects the zero surface exactly $d$ times, counted with \emph{algebraic} multiplicity (a line through the origin of a cone touches once geometrically but counts twice, ``two roots''). The light cone, from a second degree polynomial of Lorentzian signature, is the familiar case: a direction for which every parallel line intersects it exactly twice. For a fourth degree polynomial of two nested cones, the chosen direction must make every line meet it $4$ times; a direction in which some line misses the surface does not certify hyperbolicity. The set of all such directions has an interesting structure, the \textbf{hyperbolicity cone}: it does not contain zero, and it is a connected component of such directions (so for a double cone there are two, the future and the past one; ``later we will make a pick''). In the nested example it is only the set inside the inner cone.
\end{definition}
\ts{26}{1}{39:21}\ts{26}{1}{39:42}\ts{26}{1}{40:04}\ts{26}{1}{40:24}\ts{26}{1}{40:46}\ts{26}{1}{41:08}\ts{26}{1}{41:29}\ts{26}{1}{41:50}\ts{26}{1}{42:10}

\begin{theorem}[G{\aa}rding]\label{thm:c26-garding}
For a hyperbolic polynomial the hyperbolicity cone is always \emph{open} and \emph{convex}.
\end{theorem}
\ts{26}{1}{42:30}
``Whatever crazy polynomial you have, whatever matter theory you come from, it is an open and convex cone. That is extremely important''; the lecturer leaves as a question (``tell me later, in the tutorial'') why a non-convex cone would endanger the stability of photons: ``the stability of photons has a lot to do with the predictivity of the theory'' \ts{26}{1}{42:51}\ts{26}{1}{43:12}. (The proof is not given; the lecturer calls it a famous theorem by G{\aa}rding, ``Garding'' in the captions.)

\section{Raising and lowering indices: the Gauss and the Legendre map}\label{sec:c26-maps}

The null covectors correspond to null vectors: via the fiber derivative above one maps the $P$-null covectors to the $\hat P$-null vectors. But no other covector can be mapped over by this map, and no other vector back. ``By the way this is of course raising and lowering of indices, only that here in general it is nonlinear''. This is not good enough: one also needs to raise the index of covectors \emph{inside} the hyperbolicity cone \ts{26}{1}{43:12}\ts{26}{1}{43:32}\ts{26}{1}{43:52}\ts{26}{1}{44:13}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: Gauss map and Legendre map]\label{def:c26-maps}
The \textbf{Gauss map} (``for historical reasons'': an object from projective algebraic geometry, the study of zero surfaces of polynomials) is the fiber derivative of $P$ restricted to the null surface; it maps null covectors to null vectors. The \textbf{Legendre map} (an object from convex analysis) is also a fiber derivative, but of $\ln P$, defined on the hyperbolicity cone, whose image ``might lie in an ugly way'' in tangent space. In the Maxwell case the null vectors are mapped by one type of map and the vectors inside the cone by an entirely different one (a Legendre map); it is only by accident, because the structure is so simple, that the two maps numerically coincide, so in standard theory they are thought of as one and the same map, ``we always use the metric for that''. Even worse: one can extend this linear map to space-like covectors. As soon as the structure is a little more complicated the two are structurally different things.
\end{definition}
\ts{26}{1}{44:13}\ts{26}{1}{44:34}\ts{26}{1}{44:55}\ts{26}{1}{45:16}\ts{26}{1}{45:38}\ts{26}{1}{45:58}\ts{26}{1}{47:22}

\begin{theorem}[Invertibility of the Legendre map]\label{thm:c26-legendre}
If both $P$ and its dual $\hat P$ are hyperbolic (as a physicist: the matter theory is predictive and quantizable), then the Legendre map from the hyperbolicity cone of $P$ has an inverse, exists and is unique, defined on the hyperbolicity cone of the dual polynomial; it is the Legendre map of $\hat P$. So only for predictive and quantizable matter is there a theory of raising and lowering indices on the covectors and vectors inside these cones.
\end{theorem}
\ts{26}{1}{45:58}\ts{26}{1}{46:19}\ts{26}{1}{46:40}
(Statement as in the lecture; the proof is not given.)

What about vectors neither on the null surface nor inside the cone (space-like ones)? ``We don't know, it is probably not possible, and we don't care'': in standard theory too one never needs to raise the index of a space-like vector, it has no physical meaning; one can do it because the linear map extends, but there is no point in the theory where one needs it. One very often needs it on null vectors and on time-like (hyperbolic) covectors \ts{26}{1}{46:40}\ts{26}{1}{47:01}.

\begin{coursenote}[Student questions on the Legendre map and the domain]
(i) A student doubts that the Legendre map has an inverse: ``it is well-established theory, but in this context one needs to work a little to see that it can be applied''. For hyperbolic polynomials it is convex analysis: one can have a Legendre map for any convex function on a convex cone, with stricter conditions if the cone has a boundary. In classical mechanics (the Hamiltonian) and in statistical physics (thermodynamic potentials) the Legendre transformation acts on a whole vector space, which is a special case of a cone (closed under sums and positive multiples, ``like a vector subspace''), so ``some subtleties are not seen'' and one thinks one can always do it \ts{26}{1}{47:42}\ts{26}{1}{48:05}\ts{26}{1}{48:25}\ts{26}{1}{48:45}\ts{26}{1}{49:06}. (ii) A student asks about faster than light neutrinos: for a double cone, the hyperbolicity cone is the inner region; light could run on one cone and neutrinos on another one, ``there is no problem'' in principle: ``you might think which is the relevant cone: for what it's worth it is the hyperbolicity cone, but this stuff is also there; I will show you how to deal with this: that is the purpose of the whole talk'' \ts{26}{1}{49:27}\ts{26}{1}{49:48}\ts{26}{1}{50:09}.
\end{coursenote}

\section{Physical meaning of the structures}\label{sec:c26-meaning}

The physical significance (``you trust me that this is true, we checked this, many theorems'') is roughly as follows \ts{26}{1}{50:30}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item In the cotangent space the \textbf{null covectors} are the momenta of massless particles. This is the general idea of massless momenta from the \emph{geometric limit of optics}, and ``that is also the explanation why they lie on the null cone'' in the standard case: it was not explained before in the course \ts{26}{1}{50:51}\ts{26}{1}{51:12}.
  \item The covectors in the \textbf{hyperbolicity cone} are the momenta of \emph{massive} particles (``time-like covectors'', in the standard case) \ts{26}{1}{51:12}\ts{26}{1}{51:34}.
  \item Under the Gauss map the null covectors go to the tangent vectors (velocities) of massless particles; under the Legendre map the covectors of the hyperbolicity cone go to the tangent vectors of massive particles \ts{26}{1}{51:34}.
  \item Space-like covectors: ``of what should they be the momenta? Certainly not of matter; you don't need it'' \ts{26}{1}{51:56}.
\end{itemize}
In the tangent space the image of the hyperbolicity cone under the Legendre map, which does not coincide with the hyperbolicity cone of the dual polynomial and is in general bigger than the inner cone, gives the massive particle velocities. This has funny consequences: in theories not exactly standard one can have massive observers that run alongside some component of light, for instance in birefringent electrodynamics, with the two polarization components running in different directions. ``In matter this is reality; in vacuum we don't know'' (``if you are a material physicist you say yes, I know this stuff''). One can run with massive matter along one component of light, even faster than some component, but never faster than the fastest light; this gives effects like Cherenkov radiation \ts{26}{1}{52:16}\ts{26}{1}{52:36}\ts{26}{1}{52:57}\ts{26}{1}{53:17}\ts{26}{1}{53:38}. ``Who would have thought that in vacuum one could have lensing? Maybe the vacuum is as rich in structure as matter is'' \ts{26}{1}{53:59}.

The \textbf{hyperbolicity cone of the dual polynomial}, the one in the tangent space, is the set of \emph{velocities of possible observers}. In standard general relativity the observer runs along a time-like curve: the hyperbolicity cone of the dual. But in general not any matter can act as an observer: first of all they cannot, otherwise the whole picture becomes ill-defined, a constraint from the well-definition of the theory; and matter that runs faster than an observer ``will very quickly radiate away'': ``you can break the speed limit, the lower speed limit, but you pay a price: you radiate away'', losing energy to the electromagnetic field and falling back into the cone \ts{26}{1}{54:19}\ts{26}{1}{54:40}\ts{26}{1}{55:01}. This would have been a beautiful explanation, with the superluminal neutrinos, why nobody had seen them before: they would be very unstable (``so we were surprised why they would be so stable and therefore we didn't believe the result''). The lecturer stresses: ``I am not saying this is how nature is; if you write down new matter theories they can be perfectly predictive, perfectly quantizable and have this feature; maybe we will have to live with it''. ``Usually it is assumed that if a theory isn't like the standard one, it isn't predictive. That is just wrong: open any mathematics book'' \ts{26}{1}{55:21}\ts{26}{1}{55:42}\ts{26}{1}{56:03}.

\section{A refined definition of spacetime}\label{sec:c26-spacetime}

At this point a refined physical definition of spacetime, of which the one given at the beginning of the course is a special case that ``leaves out important aspects'' \ts{26}{1}{58:06}\ts{26}{1}{58:27}.

\begin{definition}[Spacetime, refined]\label{def:c26-spacetime}
A spacetime is a triple $(M,G,S_{\mathrm{matter}})$ of
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a smooth manifold $M$ (with all the structure of the first chapters; the lecture says: ``the 16-tuple''),
  \item a tensor field $G$, the \textbf{geometry} (it could have four indices, or $26$ indices, $10$ up and $16$ down, with crazy symmetries; there could be several; a Lorentzian metric is a special case), and
  \item a \textbf{test matter action} $S_{\mathrm{matter}}$ that probes it,
\end{enumerate}
such that the principal polynomial $P$ of the matter action, constructed from $G$, and its dual $\hat P$ are both hyperbolic (together with a time orientation, ``da da da'', which one can put on top). This is ``in a sense the Lorentzian condition'' in the metric case.
\end{definition}
\ts{26}{1}{58:47}\ts{26}{1}{59:08}\ts{26}{1}{59:28}\ts{26}{1}{59:48}\ts{26}{1}{1:01:10}\ts{26}{1}{1:01:31}

Why the matter must be part of the definition: ``I do not have the slightest clue how to interpret this structure if I do not use matter to probe it''. One could claim that in this room there is a Riemannian metric $(+,+,+,+)$ and have long philosophical discussions; but one has to \emph{check}, by using matter, for instance Mercury going around the sun (the precession of its perihelion) or light deflection: there must be a Lorentzian metric. ``Maxwell theory on a Riemannian metric is not predictive'': the hyperbolicity condition in the metric case \emph{means} Lorentzian signature. This is the explanation for the Lorentz signature (``somebody asked me that the other day''): if one does Maxwell theory on a metric background that is not Lorentzian, one cannot predict future data from initial data \ts{26}{1}{59:08}\ts{26}{1}{59:28}\ts{26}{1}{1:00:09}\ts{26}{1}{1:00:29}\ts{26}{1}{1:00:50}. In general there is nothing about signature, it is about bihyperbolicity.

So it makes no sense to ask whether spacetime has some structure; one asks whether it has this structure combined with such and such matter coupled to it. Whether the geometry is metric is a philosophical question (``how would I ever test this?''): matter and spacetime are not as separated as one thinks, not even in the basic interpretation. Historically this is what happened: Einstein stared at Maxwell and saw the relativistic structure, and the whole interpretation of observers and measurements was fixed by the Maxwell equations; ``we don't need a new Einstein, we just look at the mathematical structure of the theory with the mathematics we have'' \ts{26}{1}{1:01:52}\ts{26}{1}{1:02:12}\ts{26}{1}{1:02:33}\ts{26}{1}{1:02:53}.

\begin{remark}[The three exclamation marks]
``The physicality of the assumed spacetime geometry depends on the matter'': (1) it is remarkable that one needs to provide matter to understand what one is even talking about; (2) it explains many questions that were asked and others that could have been asked: why a metric (it underlies Maxwell theory); why a Lorentzian one (otherwise Maxwell theory is not predictive); why raise and lower indices like this (it is the duality between the spaces); why raise and lower null and time-like vectors in the same way (``that is an accident, that is not generic''); (3) the history of modifying gravity: many proposals, Einstein himself proposed a \emph{non-symmetric} metric tensor (more degrees of freedom, maybe a better theory), and then one started constructing inverses of it, ``and that is where the whole trouble starts''. ``Formal analogies mean very little: you need to understand why you did it in GR like this and then follow the why, not just copy something formal.'' One does not know how to go from covector to vector unless one has matter: it is the principal polynomial that tells how, and the principal polynomial is obtained from the structure $G$ only by looking at the matter whose polynomial it is \ts{26}{1}{1:03:13}\ts{26}{1}{1:03:34}\ts{26}{1}{1:03:55}\ts{26}{1}{1:04:15}\ts{26}{1}{1:04:35}\ts{26}{1}{1:04:55}.
\end{remark}

This also explains, for a point particle, why it has \emph{this} Lagrangian: the momentum is obtained by pulling an index on the velocity, but ``the velocity is a kinematical quantity and the momentum always depends on your Lagrangian, you know that from classical mechanics: you cannot calculate a momentum without knowing the dynamics''. So how would one associate momenta to velocities without knowing what matter one is talking about? ``A lot of what we do by raising and lowering indices, vectors going to covectors, we do as if written in stone on a Lorentzian manifold; it is only like this because we always substitute Maxwell theory as the one theory that tells us how things go'' \ts{26}{1}{1:05:17}\ts{26}{1}{1:05:38}\ts{26}{1}{1:05:58}\ts{26}{1}{1:06:20}.

It is ``a slight redefinition'' that only makes precise what was tacitly introduced before (``this is what Einstein told us''): maximally conservative, only studying what logically underlies the standard theory, but then applicable to any matter theory, ``the beauty of it, especially having so much matter around us that we do not understand''. One understands spacetime only if one understands the matter on it \ts{26}{1}{1:06:40}\ts{26}{1}{1:07:01}.

\section{Gravitational dynamics: God's game}\label{sec:c26-dynamics}

Everything so far is fine for a \emph{given} geometry. How to give the new structure dynamics? ``That has been undertaken quite a lot'': Einstein, Schr\"odinger, Eddington, Moffat and hundreds of others tried new gravitational dynamics for new geometric structures. ``I think in the past people followed too much the formal analogies and always ran against some wall'' \ts{26}{1}{1:07:21}\ts{26}{1}{1:07:42}\ts{26}{1}{1:08:02}. The lecturer goes close to what Giulini (guest lecturer, chapters~\ref{ch:c18} and~\ref{ch:c19}) said about the standard theory, but starts anew since that part is not examined: ``but you should (study it); it was a fantastic lecture'' \ts{26}{1}{1:08:23}.

\paragraph{The internal God.} (One dimension of spacetime is dropped in the picture.) An ``eternal God'' (the lecturer says ``internal''; the captions are not sure) views spacetime as a whole: ``tada'', he knows the history of the whole universe at once, a particle's world line, 2000 years ago to today. This is a bit the megalomaniac view of theoretical physicists: space is a four-dimensional manifold, the world line is there. In that sense nothing happens, it just is; and if we had this view we would not need physics, ``I can predict by looking'' \ts{26}{1}{1:08:44}\ts{26}{1}{1:09:04}\ts{26}{1}{1:09:25}\ts{26}{1}{1:09:45}.

But it is lonely up there, so God plays a game: in the spacetime he oversees he puts arbitrary \emph{space-like hypersurfaces} (``I don't know why God would be interested in space-like hypersurfaces, but let's assume he does''), for which the covector is the dual of a tangent vector to a world line. He knows the geometry (say a Lorentzian metric) on the whole of spacetime, hence the \emph{induced geometry} on each surface (``like me saying: let's put a little sphere here and study how the sphere works; why? I am bored''). Then he asks how the geometry on a surface changes if he slightly deforms the surface by a \textbf{lapse} $N$ (how much he goes up) and a \textbf{shift} $N^\alpha$ (how much he goes to the side), ``you remember that from Giulini's lecture''. He plays it in two ways: look at what is on the other surface, or take the data on one surface, say how he deforms it and calculate by derivatives how it looks: ``totally silly when God plays this game, a pedagogical game'' \ts{26}{1}{1:09:45}\ts{26}{1}{1:10:05}\ts{26}{1}{1:10:26}\ts{26}{1}{1:10:46}\ts{26}{1}{1:11:07}\ts{26}{1}{1:11:27}\ts{26}{1}{1:11:47}.

\paragraph{Homo sapiens.} Our access to spacetime is different: a short lifetime, or experiments that are switched on and off again, and the finite length of our arms: ``whatever your size and lifetime you experience something like a little piece of cake'' (the longest running experiment in history? ``Gerhard?'' nobody knows). ``Homo sapiens sapientissimus'' makes an abstraction: he linearises what happens away from now: instead of collecting data in the piece of cake he collects data on the plate on which the cake is served and linearly approximates the cake by giving initial velocities. Also for geometry: Giulini showed the geometric fields and the momentum fields (``a bit funny that geometry has a momentum, but if geometry is a field why wouldn't it''). Measuring a velocity needs a finite lifetime: ``if you live in one instant you never see velocities''. Position and velocity (initial geometry and its first order change) is the first step to competing with God, but then life is over \ts{26}{1}{1:11:47}\ts{26}{1}{1:12:09}\ts{26}{1}{1:12:30}\ts{26}{1}{1:12:53}\ts{26}{1}{1:13:13}\ts{26}{1}{1:13:34}\ts{26}{1}{1:13:55}\ts{26}{1}{1:14:15}\ts{26}{1}{1:14:36}\ts{26}{1}{1:14:57}.

What brings us closer to God is a \textbf{Hamiltonian} (or a Lagrangian, or the equations of motion): it eats the initial conditions and evolves them. So the question of the lecture is: \emph{what is the Hamiltonian of the new (or the old) gravity theory?} ``The Hamiltonian that makes you compete with God'' \ts{26}{1}{1:15:18}\ts{26}{1}{1:15:39}\ts{26}{1}{1:15:59}.

\paragraph{The deformation algebra.} Giulini mentioned that the \emph{constraint algebra} (the $H$'s and $D$'s) can be obtained from purely \emph{kinematical} deformation algebras: one does not need to know the dynamics first, only the geometry and how to deform one hypersurface into another \ts{26}{1}{1:16:20}\ts{26}{1}{1:16:40}. God's game is an embedding $X_t$ (Giulini's $E_t$) of the same three-manifold into spacetime again and again, space-like, with coordinates chosen on spacetime and on the abstract three-manifold; the induced geometry is the \emph{pullback} of the geometry along the embedding \ts{26}{1}{1:17:00}\ts{26}{1}{1:17:21}\ts{26}{1}{1:17:41}.

The game is expressed by \textbf{deformation operators}. The \emph{tangential} one, with the shift $N^\alpha$: a functional derivative acting on the geometry, which tells how the data change if one goes a bit to the side along the tangent vectors of the hypersurface, always given by the embedding \ts{26}{1}{1:17:41}\ts{26}{1}{1:18:02}\ts{26}{1}{1:18:22}. The \emph{normal} one, with the lapse $N$, is more interesting: one needs a normal \emph{vector}, but a hypersurface only gives a \emph{co-normal}, the gradient, which annihilates all tangent vectors; to go away from the hypersurface one has to convert the covector into a vector \ts{26}{1}{1:18:44}\ts{26}{1}{1:19:05}\ts{26}{1}{1:19:25}\ts{26}{1}{1:19:47}.

\begin{proposition}[Where the matter enters: the normal deformation]\label{prop:c26-normal}
An initial data surface must have a gradient covector that lies in the cotangent space \emph{inside the hyperbolicity cone}, and the direction in which one deforms is the \textbf{Legendre image} of that covector: the fiber derivative of $\ln P$, with $P$ the principal polynomial of the matter on the spacetime (the inverse map being that of the dual polynomial). This is the conversion from momentum to velocity for massive momenta, and ``it is the one and only step where physics goes into the derivation of gravity''. Gravity comes from matter at precisely this point.
\end{proposition}
\ts{26}{1}{1:19:47}\ts{26}{1}{1:20:08}\ts{26}{1}{1:20:28}\ts{26}{1}{1:20:49}\ts{26}{1}{1:21:10}\ts{26}{1}{1:21:31}\ts{26}{1}{1:21:51}\ts{26}{1}{1:22:12}

``Thank God we postulated matter in our definition of spacetime'': only if one understands the matter does one know what is meant by a normal deformation \ts{26}{1}{1:20:08}.

Now God plays his game with linear functional derivative operators, ``an easy game''. We must play a harder one: represent these operators not on the space of all geometries but as a \textbf{Poisson bracket action on initial data} (position and momentum, ``one can think of a Hamiltonian acting on positions: you get the $\dot q$''). The Hamiltonian of gravity is the sum of the two operators (``you can check in Giulini's lecture for the special case of Einstein gravity''): these have to be represented by Poisson bracket operators with one slot filled with representations of the two objects. But how are the two objects to be chosen? Calculate the algebra of the deformation operators and \emph{require that their representations satisfy the same algebra}: otherwise ``you are playing a different game'' \ts{26}{1}{1:22:33}\ts{26}{1}{1:22:53}\ts{26}{1}{1:23:14}\ts{26}{1}{1:23:34}\ts{26}{1}{1:23:54}.

\begin{theorem}[The deformation algebra, structure only]\label{thm:c26-algebra}
The commutator of two normal deformations is a tangential deformation whose vector field is built from the \emph{inverse spatial structure of the principal polynomial}: for matter of principal polynomial of degree $\deg P$ the shift vector field involves a tensor with $\deg P$ indices built from the principal polynomial, two of which are the ones displayed in the formula and the remaining ones are filled with the covector $k$ [?]. In Einstein gravity, $\deg P=2$ and this is the inverse of the spatial metric on the hypersurface. ``This is a calculation; it works in our fully general theory, and it must work like that for mathematical reasons.''
\end{theorem}
\ts{26}{1}{1:23:54}\ts{26}{1}{1:24:15}\ts{26}{1}{1:24:37}
(The formula was on the slide; the captions only give this description, hence the statement is a reconstruction and the exact index structure is marked [?].)

Imposing this algebra on the two representing objects determines them. This is a hard problem: the normal deformations appear \emph{quadratically} in the algebra ($HH$), and it is \emph{not a Lie algebra}: there are no structure constants but structure functions (Giulini also emphasised this), and the space is infinite dimensional \ts{26}{1}{1:24:37}\ts{26}{1}{1:24:57}\ts{26}{1}{1:25:17}. But once solved, one gets the two objects and the gravitational action. In summary: start with matter, calculate its principal polynomial, put it into the algebra (``this is always the same algebra, you don't even need to repeat the step''), find the representation, and one has the gravity Lagrangian: the Hamiltonian of the geometry \ts{26}{1}{1:25:17}\ts{26}{1}{1:25:37}\ts{26}{1}{1:25:59}.

\section{The linear homogeneous PDE system and its solution}\label{sec:c26-solving}

How to solve this very complicated representation problem? It is a quadratic one; it can be converted into a linear distribution-function differential equation, ``still too difficult'', and finally into a \emph{linear homogeneous system of partial differential equations}, for the price of being uncountably infinitely many equations \ts{26}{1}{1:25:59}\ts{26}{1}{1:26:19}\ts{26}{1}{1:26:39}.

\begin{theorem}[The Lagrangian of gravity from a PDE system, as announced]\label{thm:c26-pde}
Expand the gravitational Lagrangian in a series with unknown coefficients $C$ (with a varying number of indices). For these coefficients there are six equations and five sequences of equations, ``five times infinity'': infinitely many equations in total. The remaining coefficients (the ones the lecturer calls $U$, $S$, $T$, $V$, $M$, $Q$ [names as said, not checked]) are \emph{calculated from the principal polynomial} by a recipe (``one A4 page recipe, very boring, Max can tell you how boring it is''). The resulting equations always have the same form; only their coefficients change with the matter type. Solving them gives the $C$'s and the gravity theory.
\end{theorem}
\ts{26}{1}{1:26:39}\ts{26}{1}{1:27:00}\ts{26}{1}{1:27:21}\ts{26}{1}{1:27:41}
(The formulas were on slides and are not reproducible from the captions.)

\begin{example}[Maxwell matter gives Einstein gravity]\label{ex:c26-einstein}
Start with Maxwell theory: compute its principal polynomial $P=g^{ab}k_ak_b$, the coefficients, plug them in; ``you still have infinitely many equations, need to be very organised, and then you start solving them''. The result is that the Lagrangian expanded in the $C$'s is
\[
  L\;\propto\;\frac1G\,\sqrt{-g}\,\bigl(R+\Lambda\bigr)
\]
[the lecture says: the square root of minus $g$ times $R$ plus $\Lambda$, with an overall factor $1/G$; the exact normalisation of $\Lambda$ was not on the captions], \emph{up to the integration constants}, which stay undetermined because it is a system of differential equations. So: Einstein gravity is the gravity theory whose solutions are such that Maxwell theory can live on it and is predictive and quantizable.
\end{example}
\ts{26}{1}{1:27:41}\ts{26}{1}{1:28:02}\ts{26}{1}{1:28:22}\ts{26}{1}{1:28:43}

``So the problem of finding a gravity theory is not working in a patent office; it is actually solving these equations'' \ts{26}{1}{1:29:03}. Presented in Schuller and Witte, arXiv:1402.6548, chapter~\ref{ch:c30}, with the later developments in chapters~\ref{ch:c31} and~\ref{ch:c32}.

\section{Phenomenology, new matter and questions}\label{sec:c26-pheno}

What is a ``fundamental theory''? ``All physics is phenomenology.'' Ampere and Faraday: a current through a wire with magnetic needles around it arranging themselves, and the dynamo on a bike producing light; Maxwell adds the displacement current to get the continuity equation right (``minor stuff, peanuts''). ``It is actually these two experiments you do in high school that imply this, which implies the Big Bang: an amazing stretch of connection.'' Maybe today people looking at dark matter or dark energy will find a new Faraday and Ampere, and a new Maxwell to put them together: ``I am fantasizing, it could all be very simple''. What then? Calculate the principal polynomial, plug it into the equations above and solve them, ``however easy or hard that will be'' \ts{26}{1}{1:29:03}\ts{26}{1}{1:29:24}\ts{26}{1}{1:29:44}\ts{26}{1}{1:30:05}\ts{26}{1}{1:30:26}\ts{26}{1}{1:30:46}.

\begin{example}[A birefringent vacuum: linear electrodynamics with a general tensor]\label{ex:c26-biref}
NASA and others look for birefringence in vacuum and do not find it, so there are bounds; but ``how would you find it? You don't even have a theory that describes this properly.'' One does: take Maxwell theory, still linear (superposition), with the Lagrangian density
\[
  L\;\propto\;\sqrt{-g}\;G^{abcd}F_{ab}F_{cd}
\]
(prefactor and exact slide form not checked [?]) 
where instead of tying $F_{ab}F_{cd}$ together with two metrics (the usual $F^2$ action) one uses a general tensor $G^{abcd}$ with the induced symmetries (antisymmetric in each pair and an exchange symmetry). This is what explains linear birefringence in materials, and would also encode birefringence in the vacuum. One can compute its principal polynomial and ``you understand the drill''. One of the projects of Max and other students of the lecturer is to derive the gravity theory consistently underlying the assumption that there is something like this in spacetime. Then there is a prediction of \emph{where the effect will be strong}: if one just points the telescope somewhere and does not see birefringence that is not convincing, ``you need to know what you are looking for in order to discover the object'' (as the binary pulsar radiation computed ``yesterday'' in the school). One can calculate the gravity theory underlying this without waiting for dark energy to be put into equations in a hundred years: not a deviation parametrised by an extra term, but a well-rounded full theory of the matter \emph{and} the underlying gravity, which is not Einstein gravity \ts{26}{1}{1:31:06}\ts{26}{1}{1:31:27}\ts{26}{1}{1:31:48}\ts{26}{1}{1:32:09}\ts{26}{1}{1:32:29}\ts{26}{1}{1:32:50}\ts{26}{1}{1:33:11}.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Which matter gives non-Einstein gravity?]\label{rem:c26-which}
The answer: whenever the matter has a principal polynomial different from $g^{ab}k_ak_b$, i.e.\ whenever the cone of the principal polynomial looks more complicated than the double light cone; ``as simple as that''. Every matter, however complicated, with this principal polynomial is standard matter, with Einstein gravity \ts{26}{1}{1:33:11}\ts{26}{1}{1:33:31}.
\end{remark}

For instance the \textbf{Dirac algebra} $\{\gamma^a,\gamma^b\}=2g^{ab}$: why must the $\gamma$'s be like that? ``Because Dirac told us so'': in fact if one takes the (massless) Dirac equation and calculates its principal polynomial one gets $P(k)=g^{ab}k_ak_b$, the Maxwell polynomial. That is the reason for the form of the algebra (Dirac matter is standard matter there) \ts{26}{1}{1:33:31}\ts{26}{1}{1:33:53}\ts{26}{1}{1:34:14}.

There are no free parameters in the theory, ``it is not of the type you add some terms and then oh they are so highly suppressed but I still believe in it'': one gets the equations, solves them, and gets parameters only as integration constants of differential equations, ``undetermined stuff'' \ts{26}{1}{1:38:44}\ts{26}{1}{1:39:05}. \section*{Key concepts}
\begin{keyconcepts}
\textbf{Retrieval questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What does ``quantizable matter'' mean, and what are the two assumptions on matter (predictive, quantizable)?
  \item Define the principal polynomial of a system of field equations. What is it for Maxwell theory, and for the massless Dirac equation?
  \item Define a hyperbolic polynomial and its hyperbolicity cone. Which theorem makes the cone open and convex? What does one need for the dual polynomial?
  \item What is bihyperbolicity and which two physical requirements give it?
  \item Distinguish the Gauss map from the Legendre map. When is the Legendre map invertible, and why do they coincide in the standard theory?
  \item What are the physical meanings of the null covectors, the covectors in the hyperbolicity cone, and the hyperbolicity cone of the dual polynomial?
  \item State the refined definition of spacetime: why does it include a matter action? Why does Maxwell theory force the Lorentzian signature?
  \item In the deformation game, where does the matter enter? What is the structure of the algebra of the deformation operators, and why is it not a Lie algebra?
  \item Which system of equations gives the gravitational Lagrangian? What comes out for Maxwell matter, and which constants stay undetermined?
\end{enumerate}

\end{itemize}
