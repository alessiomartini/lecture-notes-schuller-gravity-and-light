\section{A new structure}\label{sec:c07-new}

Good morning, and welcome to lecture 7 \ts{7}{1}{0:00}. Today there is a new part of the course. Whatever was done so far (the underlying set, the topology, topological manifold, smooth manifold, the construction of the bundle) is structure that one has to provide \emph{by hand} before one can start doing physics, ``because we do not have equations that determine what happened so far'': these are assumptions that must be submitted before the physics can start.
Today one introduces yet another structure on a smooth manifold, a \textbf{connection}, also called a \textbf{covariant derivative} \ts{7}{1}{0:21}\ts{7}{1}{0:42}.

There is a difference between the two names, but ``for our purposes we will never talk about this difference; connection is the slightly more general notion''. In spacetime the connection will be determined by Einstein's equations; more precisely the equations will determine a \emph{metric}, an even more special structure, which in turn determines a connection. From now on everything will be an object that is the subject of Einstein's equations, determined by the matter in the universe. These are only allusions to physics to give some orientation; the lecture continues in the mathematical fashion
\ts{7}{1}{1:02}\ts{7}{1}{1:23}\ts{7}{1}{1:44}.

\section{Directional derivatives of tensor fields}\label{sec:c07-directional}

\paragraph{Notation: vector fields are $X$.} So far one wrote $\chi$ for a vector field and reserved $X$ for a vector; from now on only vector fields are considered unless said otherwise, and they are written with capital Latin letters (``the whole bundle construction of the last lecture was only necessary to speak about a \emph{smooth} vector field in a precise manner; for the intuition one only needs a vector at each point'').
A vector field $X$ gives a directional derivative: $Xf$ for a smooth function $f$; this was the whole idea of the tangent space, which contains all the directional derivative operators (called velocities)
\ts{7}{1}{2:07}\ts{7}{1}{2:28}\ts{7}{1}{2:49}\ts{7}{1}{3:12}\ts{7}{1}{3:32}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the symbol $\nabla_Xf$]\label{def:c07-nablaf}
For a smooth function $f$ and a vector field $X$ one introduces the new notation, to remind that this is a directional derivative,
\[
  \nabla_Xf:=Xf.
\]
It acts only on functions so far: ``we can only do directional derivatives of functions''.
\end{definition}
\ts{7}{1}{3:53}\ts{7}{1}{4:14}.

\paragraph{Why a third notation?} This seems notational overkill: one has $Xf$ and also $\mathrm df(X)$, which gives the same function (\cref{ex:c06-df}), so three notations for the same thing. The equal signs are right, but the objects differ in perspective: $X$ is a vector field eating a function and spitting out a function (a vector on a function gives a number, a vector \emph{field} on a function gives a function), whereas $\mathrm df$ eats a smooth vector field and only then spits out a function; and $\nabla_X$ is again $C^\infty(M)\to C^\infty(M)$, with the vector field put down in the index, which changes the perspective of what is argument and what is function. ``This is total notational overkill, but it is introduced for the following reason'' \ts{7}{1}{4:40}\ts{7}{1}{5:01}\ts{7}{1}{5:22}\ts{7}{1}{5:43}\ts{7}{1}{6:04}\ts{7}{1}{6:25}\ts{7}{1}{6:46}.
The symbol $\nabla_X$ can be \emph{generalised}: it should extend to eating an arbitrary $(p,q)$ tensor field and yielding again a $(p,q)$ tensor field, whereas $X$ can only eat functions. One wants to speak of the directional derivative of a vector field, for instance, and this is needed in physics. Had the extension come for free on any smooth manifold, one would not invent a new symbol; but \emph{it does not come automatically}: even requiring all the properties one wishes of a directional derivative, one still has to provide \emph{extra structure} on the manifold to get a directional derivative on non-functions, on tensors. The new symbol stands for exactly this additional structure. On functions it does not need the new structure and acts as above (hence \cref{def:c07-nablaf} is a definition only on functions)
\ts{7}{1}{7:07}\ts{7}{1}{7:29}\ts{7}{1}{7:50}\ts{7}{1}{8:10}\ts{7}{1}{8:30}\ts{7}{1}{8:51}\ts{7}{1}{9:12}.

\section{The wish list: the definition of a connection}\label{sec:c07-wish}

How does one get such directional derivatives and which structure is appropriate? ``Very simple: one first requires what one would like a directional derivative to satisfy, and then sees what structure that contains.'' So one formulates a wish list of properties which $\nabla_X$ acting on a tensor field should have, puts it in the form of a definition, and afterwards finds out how much freedom remains, since there may be many structures satisfying the wish list.
It will matter to identify the type and the amount of information to provide as extra structure in order to fix one such covariant derivative: the remaining freedom must be provided as additional structure beyond the smooth manifold one already has, and one will pick one on one occasion and another on another
\ts{7}{1}{9:36}\ts{7}{1}{9:58}\ts{7}{1}{10:21}\ts{7}{1}{10:46}\ts{7}{1}{11:07}\ts{7}{1}{11:28}\ts{7}{1}{11:49}\ts{7}{1}{12:10}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: connection, covariant derivative, manifold with connection]\label{def:c07-connection}
Let $(M,\mathcal O,\mathcal A)$ be a smooth manifold. A \textbf{connection} (also \textbf{linear connection}, \textbf{affine connection}, or, mostly in this course, \textbf{covariant derivative}) $\nabla$ on $M$ is a map which takes a pair consisting of a vector field $X$ and a $(p,q)$ tensor field $T$ and sends it to a $(p,q)$ tensor field denoted $\nabla_XT$,
\[
  (X,T)\longmapsto\nabla_XT,
\]
satisfying the following four properties:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Extension of the directional derivative}: for a $(0,0)$ tensor field, i.e.\ a function $f$,
        \[\nabla_Xf=Xf.\]
  \item \emph{Additivity in the tensor}: for $(p,q)$ tensor fields $T,S$ of the same type
        \[\nabla_X(T+S)=\nabla_XT+\nabla_XS.\]
  \item \emph{Leibniz rule}: for a $(1,1)$ tensor field $T$, a covector field $\omega$ and a vector field $Y$,
        \[\nabla_X\bigl(T(\omega,Y)\bigr)=(\nabla_XT)(\omega,Y)+T(\nabla_X\omega,Y)+T(\omega,\nabla_XY),\]
        and analogously for any $(p,q)$ tensor field, with one more term for each further entry (for a $(1,2)$ tensor field a term $T(\omega,Y,\nabla_XZ)$, and for another covector field a term $T(\nabla_X\omega',\dots)$).
  \item \emph{$C^\infty(M)$-linearity in the lower slot}: for a function $f$ and vector fields $X,Z$,
        \[\nabla_{fX+Z}T=f\,\nabla_XT+\nabla_ZT.\]
\end{enumerate}
A \textbf{manifold with connection} (an \textbf{affine manifold}) is a quadruple $(M,\mathcal O,\mathcal A,\nabla)$.
\end{definition}
\ts{7}{1}{12:30}\ts{7}{1}{12:51}\ts{7}{1}{13:11}\ts{7}{1}{13:35}\ts{7}{1}{14:17}\ts{7}{1}{14:40}\ts{7}{1}{15:01}\ts{7}{1}{16:02}\ts{7}{1}{17:03}\ts{7}{1}{17:45}\ts{7}{1}{19:33}\ts{7}{1}{20:14}\ts{7}{1}{20:35}\ts{7}{1}{21:38}.
\begin{coursenote}[Names]
Schuller lists the names: ``linear connection'' is more precise but non-linear ones are not considered; covariant derivative ``very much emphasizes the way in which we use it''; some authors call it an affine connection; he will mostly say covariant derivative. (As said in class; which author he means by ``some people like Schr\"odinger'' is not clear in the captions [?].)
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{Comments on the four properties.} Property 1 was already written down. Properties 2 to 4 are formulated for general $(p,q)$ tensor fields, and this includes $p=q=0$, so one must be consistent with property 1: in the problem sheet one checks that this works out, ``a very good exercise in seeing where an object is defined, where a multiplication is defined''. Property 2: one can add only tensors of the same type.
For property 3 one takes a $(1,1)$ tensor $T$ already evaluated on a covector field and a vector field: this is a smooth function, so its derivative $\nabla_X$ is determined by property 1; one \emph{wishes} that it equals the derivative of the tensor itself, evaluated on the two fields, plus the tensor with the covector field derived and the vector field kept, plus the tensor with the covector field kept and the vector field derived. In words: the derivative of the fully evaluated function, which one knows how to compute, can be represented by the action of $\nabla_X$ on everything that is inside, one after the other. This is the \textbf{Leibniz rule} (or product rule), and it is also called the \emph{extension rule} in the sense that $\nabla_X$ extends the action of $X$ on functions; property 2 is the additivity in the argument
\ts{7}{1}{16:23}\ts{7}{1}{16:43}\ts{7}{1}{17:24}\ts{7}{1}{17:45}\ts{7}{1}{18:06}\ts{7}{1}{18:26}\ts{7}{1}{18:47}\ts{7}{1}{19:08}\ts{7}{1}{19:53}.

Property 4 says that the whole object is $C^\infty(M)$-linear in the lower slot (which contains a vector field $X$): one may split $\nabla_{fX+Z}T=\nabla_{fX}T+\nabla_ZT$ and the function $f$ may jump in front. In the slot it acts on, $\nabla_XT$ is only additive, and if a function stands there it is treated like a tensor in a product, with the Leibniz rule
\ts{7}{1}{20:35}\ts{7}{1}{20:57}\ts{7}{1}{21:17}.

\begin{coursenote}[The Leibniz rule in terms of the tensor product]
Schuller leaves it as a homework (``it is important that you try that'') and then gives, in answer to a student's question, the equivalent formulation
\[
  \nabla_X(T\otimes S)=(\nabla_XT)\otimes S+T\otimes(\nabla_XS).
\]
Here, if $T$ is a $(p,q)$ tensor and $S$ an $(r,s)$ tensor, the \textbf{tensor product} $T\otimes S$ is the $(p+r,q+s)$ tensor field which eats $p+r$ covector fields and $q+s$ vector fields and is defined by multiplying: $T$ evaluated on the first $p$ covector fields and the first $q$ vector fields, times $S$ evaluated on the remaining $r$ covector fields and $s$ vector fields; the product is that of $\R$ or, for fields, of $C^\infty(M)$. In this form the rule ``reminds even more of the standard product rule''; the formulation with the evaluated tensor combined this definition and the rule, so the tensor product did not need to be introduced
\ts{7}{1}{36:50}\ts{7}{1}{37:11}\ts{7}{1}{37:31}\ts{7}{1}{37:52}\ts{7}{1}{38:13}\ts{7}{1}{38:34}.
(Schuller says ``the first $R$ vector fields'' where $q$ is meant: a slip at the board.)
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{How much choice is there?} Those are the axioms; a manifold with connection is the quadruple above. The question is whether there is a unique such object or an amount of choice; there is some remaining freedom, but it is fully under control \ts{7}{1}{21:38}\ts{7}{1}{22:06}\ts{7}{1}{22:27}\ts{7}{1}{22:47}.

\begin{coursenote}[Student question: only the lower entry must be a field]
A student asks whether the $f$ must be only in the entry below. Yes: ``it is only the entry down here'', and the entry on which $\nabla$ acts is different. It is meaningful to speak of the directional derivative of a field \emph{in the direction at a point}; other derivatives, like the Lie derivative, differ: the Lie derivative of a tensor field in the direction of a vector at one point cannot be asked, because it uses an entire direction \emph{field}.
Here, because of the $C^\infty$-linearity in the lower entry, one can scale the field $X$ at every point differently, but the result at a point $P$ depends only on the scaling chosen at $P$, not on how the field behaves in the neighbourhood: the values of $X$ near $P$ do not enter. So, for $\nabla_XT$, a vector at $P$ is enough (``you put in a field but you would not have needed a field''); this is the same secret as for tensor fields (\cref{def:c06-tensorfield}): even if one plugs in fields, one could evaluate by plugging in their values at the point. That is why the brackets in the definition (field versus vector at a point) ``could be read off with some experience'' from the $C^\infty$-linearity. Where $X$ is a vector at a point $p$, $\nabla_XT$ is only defined at $p$, like acting with a vector on a function giving a number.
\ts{7}{1}{22:47}\ts{7}{1}{23:09}\ts{7}{1}{23:31}\ts{7}{1}{23:52}\ts{7}{1}{24:13}\ts{7}{1}{24:35}\ts{7}{1}{24:56}\ts{7}{1}{25:16}.
Also on the field in the upper slot: only the field is allowed there, ``if you put here the field you need to put here the field, if you do not put here the field you cannot put here the field''
\ts{7}{1}{15:21}\ts{7}{1}{15:41}.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{aside}[Why not just linearity in the second slot?]
A student asks why one does not simply require $C^\infty(M)$-linearity (as usual) in the argument. It would not fit the instance of a function times a function: $X(fg)=(Xf)g+f(Xg)$ is a product rule, not a $C^\infty(M)$-linearity, which would have only one term and let $f$ jump out. A derivative is additive in the object on which it acts, but cannot be $C^\infty$-linear there, one needs the Leibniz rule
\ts{7}{1}{25:37}\ts{7}{1}{26:00}\ts{7}{1}{26:21}\ts{7}{1}{26:42}.
\end{aside}

\paragraph{On the symbol $\nabla$ without $X$.} If one leaves out the $X$ one can ask what the bare $\nabla$ is the extension of: it is the extension of $\mathrm d$ (``you see it here, the $\nabla$ alone is the extension of the $\mathrm d$; you need to feed the $\nabla$ and $X$ in some fashion before it can act on a function''). This is just notation, ``this has no depth'': one could have formulated everything as an extension of $\mathrm d$, and the structure comes with an $X$ only because this is more easily formulated; ``yet another level of obscurity which is not needed'' \ts{7}{1}{27:03}\ts{7}{1}{27:23}\ts{7}{1}{27:45}\ts{7}{1}{28:06}\ts{7}{1}{28:26}.

\section{The connection coefficient functions}\label{sec:c07-gamma}

The important question is how many such structures there are: what is the new structure required on $(M,\mathcal O,\mathcal A)$ to fix $\nabla$, i.e.\ how much freedom is left \ts{7}{1}{28:26}\ts{7}{1}{28:48}\ts{7}{1}{29:10}. Start with the simplest case beyond functions: $\nabla_XY$ for vector fields $X,Y$. To get numbers one goes to a chart.
Let $(U,x)\in\mathcal A$ and expand $X=X^i\,\partial/\partial x^i$ and $Y=Y^m\,\partial/\partial x^m$, with component \emph{functions} (no point is written, these are vector fields). Then, by the additivity and the $C^\infty$-linearity in the lower slot (pull the sum and the $X^i$ out), and by the Leibniz rule applied to $Y^m\otimes\partial/\partial x^m$ (property 1 turns $\nabla_{\partial/\partial x^i}Y^m$ into $\partial/\partial x^i(Y^m)$),
\[
  \nabla_XY=X^i\,\nabla_{\partial/\partial x^i}\Bigl(Y^m\frac{\partial}{\partial x^m}\Bigr)=X^i\,\frac{\partial Y^m}{\partial x^i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^m}+X^iY^m\,\nabla_{\partial/\partial x^i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^m},
\]
where $\partial Y^m/\partial x^i$ is shorthand for the vector field $\partial/\partial x^i$ acting on the function $Y^m$.
The first term is a vector field already expanded. The last factor $\nabla_{\partial/\partial x^i}\,\partial/\partial x^m$ cannot be reduced any further: it is a vector field (a derivative of a vector field is a vector field), whatever it is, so it can be expanded in the chart,
\[
  \nabla_{\partial/\partial x^i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^m}=\Gamma^q{}_{mi}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x^q}
\]
with some coefficient functions depending on the choice of $i$ and $m$ (and $q$ labels the expansion). ``These guys are very important and they have a specific name''
\ts{7}{1}{29:31}\ts{7}{1}{29:54}\ts{7}{1}{30:15}\ts{7}{1}{30:35}\ts{7}{1}{30:56}\ts{7}{1}{31:17}\ts{7}{1}{31:37}\ts{7}{1}{31:58}\ts{7}{1}{32:18}\ts{7}{1}{32:40}\ts{7}{1}{33:01}\ts{7}{1}{33:23}\ts{7}{1}{33:43}\ts{7}{1}{34:03}\ts{7}{1}{34:24}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: connection coefficient functions $\Gamma^i{}_{jk}$]\label{def:c07-gamma}
Let $(M,\mathcal O,\mathcal A,\nabla)$ be a manifold with connection and $(U,x)\in\mathcal A$ a chart. The \textbf{connection coefficient functions} of $\nabla$ with respect to the chart $(U,x)$ are the $(\dim M)^3$ functions $\Gamma^i{}_{jk}\colon U\to\R$,
\[
  \Gamma^i{}_{jk}:=\mathrm dx^i\Bigl(\nabla_{\partial/\partial x^k}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}\Bigr)
\]
(written $\Gamma_{(x)}{}^i{}_{jk}$ when the chart must be indicated). \textbf{Convention:} the \emph{last} index $k$ is the one of the object in the lower slot (the direction in which one derives), the middle index $j$ is the field that is differentiated, and the upper index $i$ extracts the component of the result by acting with $\mathrm dx^i$.
\end{definition}
\ts{7}{1}{34:45}\ts{7}{1}{35:06}\ts{7}{1}{35:26}\ts{7}{1}{39:46}\ts{7}{1}{40:07}\ts{7}{1}{40:28}\ts{7}{1}{40:52}\ts{7}{1}{41:13}.
The name: they are the \emph{coefficients} of the expansion of $\nabla_{\partial_k}\partial_j$ in the chart basis, and they are \emph{functions} (``they are functions on the manifold, chart-dependent''). One could view them as functions on the chart image, but it is preferred to think of them as functions on $U\subseteq M$, because $x$ and $x^{-1}$ are one-to-one and otherwise every formula would carry a composition with $x^{-1}$. The coordinate functions $x^1,\dots,x^d$ are another example of chart-dependent functions defined on the manifold
\ts{7}{1}{35:26}\ts{7}{1}{41:33}\ts{7}{1}{41:54}\ts{7}{1}{42:16}\ts{7}{1}{42:36}.
\begin{coursenote}[Student question: how can they be chart independent?]
A student asks how one can think of $\Gamma$ as a function when it has indices. Schuller: they are \emph{not} chart independent, the indices indicate the chart dependence and in another chart the same object takes other values; but a chart-dependent object is nevertheless a function, a collection of functions $U\to\R$. He also adds a little $x$ to the symbol on the board, $\Gamma_{(x)}$, as the student wants: ``it is a good pedagogical principle: first put it there until we get tired of it and then we drop it'', mandatory when different charts are compared
\ts{7}{1}{35:47}\ts{7}{1}{36:08}\ts{7}{1}{39:05}\ts{7}{1}{39:25}.
\end{coursenote}

So the information needed to fix $\nabla_XY$ in a chart is $(\dim M)^3$ functions (``it is a lot of information, but this is all the information you need''), and this is the freedom that is left; how to go globally comes later \ts{7}{1}{44:41}\ts{7}{1}{45:02}\ts{7}{1}{45:22}.

\section{The action on vectors, covectors and tensors}\label{sec:c07-action}

With the $\Gamma$'s, $\nabla_XY$ is a vector field and can be expanded; its $i$-th component is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c07-nablaV}
  (\nabla_XY)^i=X\bigl(Y^i\bigr)+\Gamma^i{}_{jk}\,Y^jX^k,
\end{equation}
that is, $X^k\,\partial Y^i/\partial x^k+\Gamma^i{}_{jk}Y^jX^k$. It is important whether the $i$ is inside the bracket (the $i$-th component function of $Y$, a member of a family of functions, on which the vector field $X$ acts as on a function) or outside (then one would act with $X$ on a vector, which is impossible). All the products here are pointwise products of functions (in $C^\infty(M)$), the sum too: at a point one would have a multiplication of numbers
\ts{7}{1}{42:57}\ts{7}{1}{43:17}\ts{7}{1}{43:38}\ts{7}{1}{43:58}\ts{7}{1}{44:20}\ts{7}{1}{44:41}.
\begin{coursenote}[Index order on the board]
On the board (at 43:58) Schuller writes the coefficient as $\Gamma^i{}_{mn}$ with ``$m$ goes with the $X^m$ and $n$ is the $Y^n$'', which contradicts his own convention of \cref{def:c07-gamma} that the last index goes with the direction; a few minutes later (54:30) he states the convention again and says that ``the last entry is always for the direction in which you go''. The formulas here use the convention of the definition: $\Gamma^i{}_{jk}Y^jX^k$.
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{Does one need more coefficients for covector and tensor fields?} The fear is that the higher the rank, the more coefficients are needed. This is not the case: the same $(\dim M)^3$ functions fix the action on any tensor field, used in a slightly different way (``you need to take careful attention at the position of indices that are contracted and at the signs in front'').

\begin{proposition}[The $\Gamma$'s fix $\nabla$ on every tensor field of a chart domain]\label{prop:c07-fix}
On a chart domain $U$, the choice of the $\Gamma^i{}_{jk}$ fixes the action of $\nabla$ on vector fields (\cref{eq:c07-nablaV}) and also on every tensor field. In particular, for covector fields
\[
  \nabla_{\partial/\partial x^k}\,\mathrm dx^i=-\Gamma^i{}_{jk}\,\mathrm dx^j.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For a covector field one does the same calculation as above up to the elementary question of what $\nabla_{\partial/\partial x^m}\,\mathrm dx^i$ is; this is a covector field, so one can expand it, and one could introduce new coefficients $\Sigma$ for it. The question is whether these are already expressed through the $\Gamma$'s. Look at the function $\mathrm dx^i(\partial/\partial x^j)=\delta^i_j$: it is constant, so the action of $\partial/\partial x^m$ on it is zero. On the other hand the Leibniz rule (applied to the function $\mathrm dx^i(\partial/\partial x^j)$, a $(1,1)$ tensor $\mathrm dx^i\otimes\partial/\partial x^j$ fully evaluated) gives
\begin{align*}
  0&=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^m}\Bigl(\mathrm dx^i\bigl(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x^j}\bigr)\Bigr)\\ &=\Bigl(\nabla_{\partial/\partial x^m}\mathrm dx^i\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}\Bigr)+\mathrm dx^i\Bigl(\nabla_{\partial/\partial x^m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}\Bigr).
\end{align*}
The second term is $\Gamma^i{}_{jm}$ by definition. Hence $(\nabla_{\partial/\partial x^m}\mathrm dx^i)(\partial/\partial x^j)=-\Gamma^i{}_{jm}$, and since the $j$-th component of a covector field is its action on $\partial/\partial x^j$, one gets $\nabla_{\partial/\partial x^m}\mathrm dx^i=-\Gamma^i{}_{jm}\,\mathrm dx^j$, i.e.\ the $\Sigma$'s are minus the $\Gamma$'s (``a fat minus sign because of taking this to the other side''). For higher-rank tensors one proceeds the same way, applying the Leibniz rule further.
\ts{7}{1}{45:43}\ts{7}{1}{46:04}\ts{7}{1}{46:24}\ts{7}{1}{47:31}\ts{7}{1}{47:52}\ts{7}{1}{48:14}\ts{7}{1}{48:35}\ts{7}{1}{48:57}\ts{7}{1}{49:18}\ts{7}{1}{49:39}\ts{7}{1}{49:59}\ts{7}{1}{50:19}\ts{7}{1}{50:39}\ts{7}{1}{51:01}\ts{7}{1}{51:22}\ts{7}{1}{51:43}\ts{7}{1}{52:03}\ts{7}{1}{52:24}\ts{7}{1}{52:45}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The rules to commit to memory.} ``This is not mathematics, this is just memory, but you need to commit this to memory'': wherever one has the term for vectors it comes with a \emph{plus} sign, for covectors with a \emph{minus} sign. In components, with $X^k$ always attached to the \emph{last} index of $\Gamma$ (the correction is linear in the direction):
\begin{align}
  (\nabla_XY)^i&=X\bigl(Y^i\bigr)+\Gamma^i{}_{jk}\,Y^jX^k,\label{eq:c07-V}\\
  (\nabla_X\omega)_i&=X\bigl(\omega_i\bigr)-\Gamma^j{}_{ik}\,\omega_jX^k.\label{eq:c07-CoV}
\end{align}
In \cref{eq:c07-V} the first term is ``engineering mathematics'': derive a vector field by deriving its components; but this is not all, there is a $\Gamma$ with three entries: the last for the direction, the first free index $i$ up, and a remaining summation index carrying the components of the field. For covectors one also applies $X$ naively to the components, but then a minus sign and the indices are distributed differently: the free index $i$ must stay down, and the dummy index is now up on $\Gamma$ and down on $\omega$ ($\omega_j$ has the index down)

\begin{coursenote}[Student question: in Euclidean space the $\Gamma$'s all vanish?]

\section{Change of chart: the $\Gamma$'s are not a tensor}\label{sec:c07-transf}

The remaining question is that the $\Gamma$'s were defined on one chart. If another chart overlaps, one cannot choose them here like this and there like that: one would get different covariant derivatives in the overlap region, depending on which chart one is on. So one needs the change of the $\Gamma$'s under a change of chart, a compatibility condition \ts{7}{1}{1:08:51}\ts{7}{1}{1:09:11}\ts{7}{1}{1:09:33}.

\begin{theorem}[Transformation of the connection coefficient functions]\label{thm:c07-transf}
Let $(U,x)$ and $(V,y)$ be charts of the smooth atlas with $U\cap V\neq\emptyset$. On $U\cap V$
\[
  \Gamma_{(y)}{}^i{}_{jk}=\frac{\partial y^i}{\partial x^q}\,\frac{\partial x^s}{\partial y^j}\,\frac{\partial x^p}{\partial y^k}\;\Gamma_{(x)}{}^q{}_{sp}\;+\;\frac{\partial y^i}{\partial x^s}\,\frac{\partial^2x^s}{\partial y^j\,\partial y^k}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By definition $\Gamma_{(y)}{}^i{}_{jk}=\mathrm dy^i\bigl(\nabla_{\partial/\partial y^k}\,\partial/\partial y^j\bigr)$ (in the $y$ chart: ``every $x$ that was before an $x$ is now a $y$''). The $y^i$ are functions, so the covector field $\mathrm dy^i$ can be expanded in the $x$ chart like a gradient, $\mathrm dy^i=\dfrac{\partial y^i}{\partial x^q}\,\mathrm dx^q$ (not evaluated at a point: as a field). The vector field $\partial/\partial y^k$ is expanded as $\dfrac{\partial x^p}{\partial y^k}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x^p}$ (the chain rule for the symbols, which reduces directly to the chain rule of undergraduate analysis). Wherever there was a $y$ one wants to get an $x$ into the game. So
\begin{align*}
  \Gamma_{(y)}{}^i{}_{jk}&=\frac{\partial y^i}{\partial x^q}\,\mathrm dx^q\Bigl(\frac{\partial x^p}{\partial y^k}\,\nabla_{\partial/\partial x^p}\\ &\qquad\Bigl(\frac{\partial x^s}{\partial y^j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^s}\Bigr)\Bigr),
\end{align*}
where the function $\partial x^p/\partial y^k$ jumped in front of $\nabla$ by the $C^\infty$-linearity in the lower slot, and $\mathrm dy^i$ is a covector field whose coefficients, being functions, can be taken out of $\mathrm dx^q(\cdot)$. Now the function $\partial x^s/\partial y^j$ multiplies the vector field $\partial/\partial x^s$ and one uses the Leibniz rule:
\begin{align*}
  \nabla_{\partial/\partial x^p}\Bigl(\frac{\partial x^s}{\partial y^j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^s}\Bigr)&=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^p}\Bigl(\frac{\partial x^s}{\partial y^j}\Bigr)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^s}\\ &\quad+\frac{\partial x^s}{\partial y^j}\,\nabla_{\partial/\partial x^p}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^s}.
\end{align*}
In the first term the vector field acts on a function (hence is the derivative of a function, a second derivative that is \emph{not} in the same variable), and $\mathrm dx^q(\partial/\partial x^s)=\delta^q_s$; in the second term $\mathrm dx^q\bigl(\nabla_{\partial/\partial x^p}\partial/\partial x^s\bigr)=\Gamma_{(x)}{}^q{}_{sp}$ by definition. Hence
\begin{align*}
  \Gamma_{(y)}{}^i{}_{jk}&=\frac{\partial y^i}{\partial x^q}\frac{\partial x^p}{\partial y^k}\frac{\partial x^s}{\partial y^j}\Gamma_{(x)}{}^q{}_{sp}\\ &\quad+\frac{\partial y^i}{\partial x^s}\,\frac{\partial x^p}{\partial y^k}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^p}\Bigl(\frac{\partial x^s}{\partial y^j}\Bigr).
\end{align*}
In the last term there is a summation $\frac{\partial x^p}{\partial y^k}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^p}$, which by the chain rule is $\partial/\partial y^k$: the term is $\dfrac{\partial y^i}{\partial x^s}\dfrac{\partial^2x^s}{\partial y^k\,\partial y^j}$, a second derivative with respect to \emph{the same set of coordinates} $y$ twice.
\ts{7}{1}{1:09:54}\ts{7}{1}{1:10:15}\ts{7}{1}{1:10:36}\ts{7}{1}{1:10:56}\ts{7}{1}{1:11:17}\ts{7}{1}{1:11:38}\ts{7}{1}{1:11:58}\ts{7}{1}{1:12:18}\ts{7}{1}{1:12:39}\ts{7}{1}{1:12:59}\ts{7}{1}{1:13:20}\ts{7}{1}{1:13:42}\ts{7}{1}{1:14:02}\ts{7}{1}{1:14:22}\ts{7}{1}{1:14:43}\ts{7}{1}{1:15:03}\ts{7}{1}{1:15:24}\ts{7}{1}{1:15:45}\ts{7}{1}{1:16:06}\ts{7}{1}{1:16:30}\ts{7}{1}{1:16:52}\ts{7}{1}{1:17:14}\ts{7}{1}{1:17:35}.
\end{proof}

\begin{coursenote}[Which symmetry of the second derivative?]
``Be very careful'': one might think the second derivative can be exchanged by Schwarz's rule. The symbol is not the partial derivative in the first place (one has to go down to the chart), and in $\partial^2x^s/\partial y^j\partial y^k$ both derivatives are with respect to the \emph{same} coordinates $y$, so the Schwarz rule is inherited by the symbol and one can exchange $j$ and $k$; in $\partial^2x^s/\partial x^p\partial y^j$ (mixed coordinate sets) one cannot: ``very important, very common mistake''
\ts{7}{1}{1:17:56}\ts{7}{1}{1:18:16}\ts{7}{1}{1:18:36}.
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{Why this formula matters.} The first term is exactly the transformation behaviour of the components of a $(1,2)$ tensor (compare \cref{thm:c05-changeV} and its covector analogue), but there is a second, \emph{inhomogeneous} term in which no $\Gamma$ appears:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the $\Gamma$'s do \textbf{not} transform as components of a tensor;
  \item even if all the $\Gamma$'s vanish in some chart $x$, the second term makes non-vanishing $\Gamma$'s out of nothing in another chart $y$, for chart transitions with a non-trivial second derivative. For a \emph{linear} change of chart (rotations, i.e.\ the Cartesian-to-Cartesian transformations) one does not feel it, because the second derivative drops out; this is one of the reasons why in special relativity one is happy to look only at Lorentz transformations in position space (in the tangent spaces this is fully understood), but ``why would I not introduce polar coordinates?'', which make this term non-zero;
  \item for a tensor, if the components vanish in one frame they vanish in any frame, because the new components depend homogeneously on the old ones. The $\Gamma$'s can vanish in one chart, even across the entire manifold, and still not vanish in others;
  \item in an overlap, the choice of the $\Gamma_{(x)}$ on $U$ and of the $\Gamma_{(y)}$ on $V$ cannot be independent: they must obey this relation, which can be read as a \emph{compatibility condition}.
\end{itemize}
``Whatever way you read it this is a very important formula, it should also be committed to memory'': the change of the connection coefficient functions under a chart change from $(U\cap V,x)$ to $(U\cap V,y)$

\section{Normal coordinates}\label{sec:c07-normal}

Because a question was asked, five more minutes to explain normal coordinates and how one can and cannot get them \ts{7}{1}{1:22:27}\ts{7}{1}{1:22:48}.

Let $P$ be a point of a manifold with connection (some connection $\nabla$ is assumed given). Is there a coordinate system that makes the $\Gamma$'s vanish, since the formulas would become simpler? The result is rather sobering: one can construct a chart $(U,x)$ containing $P$ in which the $\Gamma$'s vanish \emph{at the point $P$}, but not necessarily in any neighbourhood. If the manifold has a certain property, \emph{flatness} (to be defined next lecture), the $\Gamma$'s can be made to vanish in an extended region; if it is not flat, like the hyperbolic space ($\R^2$ as a smooth manifold, with the shape given by the covariant derivative: ``trust me, it has to do with the shape, the covariant derivative gives the space shape''), one can choose the entire coordinate system in the neighbourhood of the point such that the $\Gamma$'s are zero at the point, but never everywhere in the smallest neighbourhood
\ts{7}{1}{1:23:11}\ts{7}{1}{1:23:32}\ts{7}{1}{1:23:53}\ts{7}{1}{1:24:16}\ts{7}{1}{1:24:38}\ts{7}{1}{1:25:02}\ts{7}{1}{1:25:23}\ts{7}{1}{1:25:44}.

\begin{theorem}[Normal coordinates at a point]\label{thm:c07-normal}
Let $(M,\mathcal O,\mathcal A,\nabla)$ be a manifold with connection and $P\in M$. There is a chart $(U,x)$ with $P\in U$ such that the symmetric part of the connection coefficient functions vanishes at $P$:
\[
  \Gamma_{(x)}{}^i{}_{(jk)}(P)=0,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad\Gamma_{(x)}{}^i{}_{jk}(P)=\Gamma_{(x)}{}^i{}_{[jk]}(P)=\tfrac12\bigl(\Gamma_{(x)}{}^i{}_{jk}-\Gamma_{(x)}{}^i{}_{kj}\bigr)(P).
\]
Such a chart is called a \textbf{normal coordinate chart} of $\nabla$ at $P$ (``Cartesian'' would be the name at the metric level, where angles exist; here one is on the lower level of structure and there is no angle).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Start with any chart $(V,y)$ with $P\in V$; generically the $\Gamma_{(y)}{}^i{}_{jk}$ will not vanish. Without loss of generality the chart is \emph{centred}, $y(P)=0$ (``if not I can move the chart''). Look at the transformation law of \cref{thm:c07-transf}: one wants the second term to cancel what comes from the first, for which the second derivative of the chart transition map $x\circ y^{-1}$ must be controlled. So define the new chart $(U,x)$ by the chart transition map $x\circ y^{-1}\colon\R^d\supseteq y(V)\to\R^d$,
\[
  \bigl(x\circ y^{-1}\bigr)^i(\alpha):=\alpha^i+\tfrac12\,\Gamma^i_{(y)\,jk}(P)\,\alpha^j\alpha^k
\]
(restricted to a neighbourhood of $0$ on which it is invertible, which exists by the inverse function theorem [not said in class]). ``On the chart you can do whatever you wish, this is just $\R^d$ to $\R^d$''; the numbers $\Gamma_{(y)}{}^i{}_{jk}(P)$ are the values at the fixed point $P$, hence constants. The product $\alpha^j\alpha^k$ is symmetric in $j,k$, so only the symmetric part of $\Gamma_{(y)}(P)$ enters automatically.
Compute the first and second derivative of the chart transition map at $\alpha=0$, i.e.\ at the point $P$:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\partial x^i}{\partial y^b}\Big|_P&=\delta^i_b+\Gamma_{(y)}{}^i{}_{(bk)}(P)\,\alpha^k\big|_{\alpha=0}\ &=\delta^i_b,\\
  \frac{\partial^2x^i}{\partial y^j\,\partial y^k}\Big|_P&=\tfrac12\bigl(\Gamma_{(y)}{}^i{}_{jk}+\Gamma_{(y)}{}^i{}_{kj}\bigr)(P)\\ &=\Gamma_{(y)}{}^i{}_{(jk)}(P),
\end{align*}
(the factor $2$ from the product rule cancels the $\tfrac12$). The first derivative is the identity matrix at $P$, so its inverse, $\partial y^i/\partial x^j|_P$, is also $\delta^i_j$ (the matrix of the inverse chart transition, ``you check on the last problem sheet''). Now use \cref{thm:c07-transf} in the form that expresses $\Gamma_{(y)}$ through $\Gamma_{(x)}$, evaluated at $P$, where all Jacobians are Kronecker deltas:
\begin{align*}
  \Gamma_{(y)}{}^i{}_{jk}(P)&=\Gamma_{(x)}{}^i{}_{jk}(P)+\frac{\partial^2x^i}{\partial y^j\partial y^k}\Big|_P\\ &=\Gamma_{(x)}{}^i{}_{jk}(P)+\Gamma_{(y)}{}^i{}_{(jk)}(P).
\end{align*}
Hence $\Gamma_{(x)}{}^i{}_{jk}(P)=\Gamma_{(y)}{}^i{}_{jk}(P)-\Gamma_{(y)}{}^i{}_{(jk)}(P)=\Gamma_{(y)}{}^i{}_{[jk]}(P)$. [On the board the sign of the quadratic term is left open (``plus or minus, I will decide in a second'') and ends up as a minus; with the transformation law of \cref{thm:c07-transf} as derived above the consistent sign for $x\circ y^{-1}$ is the plus used here, and the result is the same: the symmetric part is subtracted [?].]
\ts{7}{1}{1:26:05}\ts{7}{1}{1:26:27}\ts{7}{1}{1:26:48}\ts{7}{1}{1:27:10}\ts{7}{1}{1:27:33}\ts{7}{1}{1:27:55}\ts{7}{1}{1:28:16}\ts{7}{1}{1:28:36}\ts{7}{1}{1:28:57}\ts{7}{1}{1:29:17}\ts{7}{1}{1:29:37}\ts{7}{1}{1:29:58}\ts{7}{1}{1:30:18}\ts{7}{1}{1:30:38}\ts{7}{1}{1:31:00}\ts{7}{1}{1:31:20}\ts{7}{1}{1:31:41}\ts{7}{1}{1:32:01}\ts{7}{1}{1:32:21}\ts{7}{1}{1:32:43}\ts{7}{1}{1:33:03}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Torsion.} The antisymmetric part $\Gamma^i{}_{jk}-\Gamma^i{}_{kj}$ is \emph{a tensor}: the inhomogeneous term of \cref{thm:c07-transf} is symmetric in $(j,k)$, so the antisymmetric part does not feel it and transforms like the components of a $(1,2)$ tensor field (``of course not, because if it is a tensor it must transform like this, it must not feel this''). It is called the \textbf{torsion} (defined in the next lecture). \subsection*{Exercise 1: statements about connections}

\paragraph{(A) The covariant derivative operator $\nabla_X$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\nabla_X$ is $C^\infty$-linear in $X$: \textbf{true}, as shown in the lecture (axiom 4) \ts{7}{2}{0:41}.
  \item $\nabla_X$ cannot be applied to a $(0,0)$ tensor field: \textbf{false}; $(0,0)$ tensor fields are functions and $\nabla_Xf=Xf$ \ts{7}{2}{1:01}\ts{7}{2}{1:24}.
  \item $\nabla_X$ acts simply as the partial derivative if the manifold is flat: \textbf{false}. A flat manifold is one where the curvature vanishes everywhere, which is equivalent to the existence of charts of the atlas covering the whole manifold in which the connection coefficients vanish; but this does not mean that they vanish in \emph{every} chart: even on a flat manifold there are charts with non-vanishing coefficient functions, and there $\nabla$ is not the partial derivative \ts{7}{2}{1:45}\ts{7}{2}{2:07}\ts{7}{2}{2:27}.
  \item $\nabla_X$ applied to a smooth function is the gradient of the function: \textbf{false}; it is the gradient $\mathrm df$ \emph{applied to the vector field} $X$, since the gradient itself is an element of the cotangent space \ts{7}{2}{2:50}\ts{7}{2}{3:10}.
  \item $\nabla$ is uniquely fixed by choosing $(\dim M)^3$ many connection coefficient functions of an atlas: \textbf{true}; they carry three indices $a,b,c$ running from $1$ to $\dim M$, and once they are all chosen the action is fixed uniquely \ts{7}{2}{3:31}\ts{7}{2}{3:52}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{(B) A connection $\nabla$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A connection maps a vector field and a $(p,q)$ tensor field to the real numbers: \textbf{false}; to a $(p,q)$ tensor field again \ts{7}{2}{4:17}\ts{7}{2}{4:37}.
  \item A connection satisfies the Leibniz rule: \textbf{true}, one of the axioms of the lecture \ts{7}{2}{4:37}.
  \item Connection coefficients vanishing in a chart vanish in the overlap with another chart: \textbf{false}. The transformation looks at first like that of a tensor, but there is the additional term with the second derivative $\partial^2x^k/\partial y^b\partial y^c\;\partial y^a/\partial x^k$, which destroys the tensor transformation behaviour; even if the coefficients vanish in the chart $x$, one gets non-vanishing coefficients in the overlap with $y$ \ts{7}{2}{4:57}\ts{7}{2}{5:19}\ts{7}{2}{5:40}\ts{7}{2}{6:00}.
  \item The coefficient functions may be defined by $\nabla$ and the chart-induced basis fields: \textbf{true}, $\nabla_{\partial/\partial x^b}\,\partial/\partial x^c=\Gamma^a{}_{bc}\,\partial/\partial x^a$ as the tutor writes it, with the direction first (in his exercise 2 he uses the lecture's order, direction last; the symmetric/antisymmetric statements do not depend on it), which defines them purely by the action of $\nabla$ on the chart-induced basis fields \ts{7}{2}{6:21}\ts{7}{2}{6:43}\ts{7}{2}{7:03}.
  \item A torsion-free connection has coefficient functions with vanishing antisymmetric part $\Gamma^k{}_{[ab]}$ in all charts: \textbf{true}; the torsion carries the indices $a$ up and $b,c$ down and is $\Gamma^a{}_{bc}-\Gamma^a{}_{cb}$, that is twice the antisymmetrised part, so if the torsion vanishes so does the antisymmetric part of the coefficients \ts{7}{2}{7:03}\ts{7}{2}{7:24}\ts{7}{2}{7:46}\ts{7}{2}{8:07}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Exercise 2: practical rules for the covariant derivative}

Examples of $\nabla$ acting on all kinds of tensor fields, to get familiar with the rules \ts{7}{2}{8:34}\ts{7}{2}{8:55}. (In the tutor's index convention $\nabla_m:=\nabla_{\partial/\partial x^m}$ and $\Gamma^a{}_{bc}$ has the direction index last, as in the lecture.)
\begin{enumerate}
  \item On a function: $\nabla_Xf=Xf=X^i\,\partial f/\partial x^i$ \ts{7}{2}{8:55}\ts{7}{2}{9:16}.
  \item On a vector field $Y$, the $a$-th component: $(\nabla_XY)^a=X(Y^a)+\Gamma^a{}_{bc}\,Y^bX^c=X^i\,\partial Y^a/\partial x^i+\Gamma^a{}_{bc}Y^bX^c$ \ts{7}{2}{9:16}\ts{7}{2}{9:37}\ts{7}{2}{9:57}\ts{7}{2}{10:18}.
  \item On a covector field $\omega$, the $a$-th component: $(\nabla_X\omega)_a=X(\omega_a)-\Gamma^m{}_{ab}\,\omega_mX^b$ (``a minus sign, and the index $m$ is up on $\Gamma$ and down on $\omega$'') \ts{7}{2}{10:39}\ts{7}{2}{11:00}\ts{7}{2}{11:22}\ts{7}{2}{11:43}.
  \item On a $(1,2)$ tensor field, with $\nabla_m$ the covariant derivative in the direction $\partial/\partial x^m$:
        \[
          (\nabla_mT)^a{}_{bc}=\frac{\partial T^a{}_{bc}}{\partial x^m}+\Gamma^a{}_{nm}\,T^n{}_{bc}-\Gamma^n{}_{bm}\,T^a{}_{nc}-\Gamma^n{}_{cm}\,T^a{}_{bn}:
        \]
        a plus term for the upper index and a minus term for each of the two lower ones, always with the direction $m$ as the last index of $\Gamma$ \ts{7}{2}{12:07}\ts{7}{2}{12:28}\ts{7}{2}{12:50}\ts{7}{2}{13:12}\ts{7}{2}{13:32}\ts{7}{2}{13:54}\ts{7}{2}{14:16}.
  \item On a covector field with antisymmetrisation, $\nabla_{[m}A_{n]}=\tfrac12\bigl(\nabla_mA_n-\nabla_nA_m\bigr)$. Expanding, $\nabla_mA_n=\partial_mA_n-\Gamma^r{}_{nm}A_r$ and $\nabla_nA_m=\partial_nA_m-\Gamma^r{}_{mn}A_r$, so
        \[
          \nabla_{[m}A_{n]}=\tfrac12\bigl(\partial_mA_n-\partial_nA_m\bigr)-\Gamma^r{}_{[nm]}A_r,
        \]
        and for a \textbf{torsion-free} connection the antisymmetric part of $\Gamma$ vanishes (exercise 1) so one is left with $\tfrac12(\partial_mA_n-\partial_nA_m)$, which one recognises as a physical quantity: one half of the electromagnetic field strength tensor $F_{mn}$ for $A$ the vector potential \ts{7}{2}{14:37}\ts{7}{2}{14:58}\ts{7}{2}{15:20}\ts{7}{2}{15:40}\ts{7}{2}{16:02}\ts{7}{2}{16:23}\ts{7}{2}{16:44}\ts{7}{2}{17:05}\ts{7}{2}{17:27}\ts{7}{2}{17:49}\ts{7}{2}{18:09}\ts{7}{2}{18:30}.
  \item On a $(0,3)$ tensor field with three antisymmetrised indices, $\nabla_{[m}\Omega_{nr]}$: expand the antisymmetrisation into the $3!=6$ terms (a factor $\tfrac1{3!}$, each exchange of two indices gives a minus sign) and write each $\nabla_a\Omega_{bc}=\partial_a\Omega_{bc}-\Gamma^s{}_{ba}\Omega_{sc}-\Gamma^s{}_{ca}\Omega_{bs}$. Using that $\Omega$ is itself antisymmetric and that $\Gamma$ is torsion free ($\Gamma^s{}_{ba}=\Gamma^s{}_{ab}$), all the terms with $\Gamma$ cancel in pairs (the tutor goes through the six terms one by one: two terms differing by the exchange of the lower indices of $\Gamma$ come with different signs and the same $\Omega$ and cancel), and only
        \[
          \nabla_{[m}\Omega_{nr]}=\partial_{[m}\Omega_{nr]}
        \]
        is left, i.e.\ with a torsion-free connection the fully antisymmetrised covariant derivative of any $(0,q)$ tensor field with antisymmetric entries involves no connection coefficients at all (this is the exterior derivative, [not said in the tutorial]) \ts{7}{2}{18:52}\ts{7}{2}{19:13}\ts{7}{2}{19:35}\ts{7}{2}{19:56}\ts{7}{2}{20:17}\ts{7}{2}{20:42}\ts{7}{2}{21:04}\ts{7}{2}{21:26}\ts{7}{2}{21:47}\ts{7}{2}{22:08}\ts{7}{2}{22:32}\ts{7}{2}{22:53}\ts{7}{2}{23:14}\ts{7}{2}{23:35}\ts{7}{2}{23:56}\ts{7}{2}{24:16}\ts{7}{2}{24:37}\ts{7}{2}{25:00}\ts{7}{2}{25:21}\ts{7}{2}{25:42}\ts{7}{2}{26:03}\ts{7}{2}{26:25}\ts{7}{2}{26:45}\ts{7}{2}{27:06}\ts{7}{2}{27:26}\ts{7}{2}{27:47}.
\end{enumerate}
The tutor stresses that this holds for the anti-symmetrised part of the result on \emph{any} such tensor field, only for a torsion-free covariant derivative \ts{7}{2}{27:26}\ts{7}{2}{27:47}.

\subsection*{Exercise 3: connection coefficients}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{How are the $\Gamma$'s of the chart $(U\cap V,y)$ related to those of $(U\cap V,x)$?} This is \cref{thm:c07-transf}:
        \[
          \Gamma_{(y)}{}^a{}_{bc}=\frac{\partial y^a}{\partial x^m}\frac{\partial x^n}{\partial y^b}\frac{\partial x^r}{\partial y^c}\,\Gamma_{(x)}{}^m{}_{nr}+\frac{\partial^2x^k}{\partial y^b\,\partial y^c}\frac{\partial y^a}{\partial x^k}.
        \]
        They do not transform like a tensor: only the first part does, and they pick up the second derivative of the chart transition function \ts{7}{2}{28:11}\ts{7}{2}{28:31}\ts{7}{2}{28:52}\ts{7}{2}{29:12}\ts{7}{2}{29:33}.
  \item \emph{Which class of chart transition maps make them transform as if they were a tensor?} The second term must vanish, i.e.\ the second derivative of the chart transition map must vanish: the transition map must be an \textbf{affine map}, $y\circ x^{-1}\colon\alpha^j\mapsto A^i{}_j\alpha^j+B^i$ with real numbers $A^i{}_j$ and $B^i$, whose second derivative with respect to any entries is zero. This is a very special choice of transitions: for arbitrary chart transition maps the term does not vanish \ts{7}{2}{29:53}\ts{7}{2}{30:14}\ts{7}{2}{30:35}\ts{7}{2}{30:56}\ts{7}{2}{31:16}\ts{7}{2}{31:37}\ts{7}{2}{31:58}\ts{7}{2}{32:19}.
  \item \emph{An example: the flat plane.} Consider the upper half plane $\{(a,b)\in\R^2\mid b>0\}$ with two charts: $x$ the Cartesian coordinates and $y$ the polar coordinates, with $x\circ y^{-1}(r,\varphi)=(r\cos\varphi,\,r\sin\varphi)$, $r\in\R^+$, $\varphi\in(0,\pi)$. Assume that the $\Gamma_{(x)}$ vanish in the Cartesian chart and compute the polar ones. Then only the second term of the transformation law survives,
        \[
          \Gamma_{(y)}{}^a{}_{bc}=\frac{\partial^2x^k}{\partial y^b\,\partial y^c}\,\frac{\partial y^a}{\partial x^k},
        \]
        and one needs the matrix of $\partial x^m/\partial y^n=\partial_n(x^m\circ y^{-1})$ evaluated at $(r,\varphi)$, with $m$ labelling the rows,
        \[
          \frac{\partial x^m}{\partial y^n}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\varphi&-r\sin\varphi\\ \sin\varphi&r\cos\varphi\end{pmatrix},
        \]
        of determinant $J=r\cos^2\varphi+r\sin^2\varphi=r$. For $\partial y^m/\partial x^n$ one does not differentiate the more complicated map $y\circ x^{-1}$: it is the \emph{inverse matrix} of the previous one (insert $x^{-1}\circ x\circ y^{-1}$, evaluate at $y(p)$): divide by the determinant, exchange the diagonal entries and flip the sign of the off-diagonal entries,
        \[
          \frac{\partial y^m}{\partial x^n}=\frac1r\begin{pmatrix}r\cos\varphi&r\sin\varphi\\ -\sin\varphi&\cos\varphi\end{pmatrix},
        \]
        and one checks that the product is the identity. Take $\Gamma_{(y)}{}^1{}_{22}$: sum over $k=1,2$,
        \[
          \Gamma_{(y)}{}^1{}_{22}=\frac{\partial^2x^1}{\partial y^2\partial y^2}\frac{\partial y^1}{\partial x^1}+\frac{\partial^2x^2}{\partial y^2\partial y^2}\frac{\partial y^1}{\partial x^2}=(-r\cos\varphi)(\cos\varphi)+(-r\sin\varphi)(\sin\varphi)=-r.
        \]
        There are more coefficients; ``you can do this for yourself'' \ts{7}{2}{32:39}\ts{7}{2}{32:59}\ts{7}{2}{33:20}\ts{7}{2}{33:42}\ts{7}{2}{34:03}\ts{7}{2}{34:23}\ts{7}{2}{34:44}\ts{7}{2}{35:05}\ts{7}{2}{35:27}\ts{7}{2}{35:52}\ts{7}{2}{36:13}\ts{7}{2}{36:36}\ts{7}{2}{36:57}\ts{7}{2}{37:44}\ts{7}{2}{38:04}\ts{7}{2}{38:25}\ts{7}{2}{38:49}\ts{7}{2}{39:12}\ts{7}{2}{39:33}\ts{7}{2}{39:55}\ts{7}{2}{40:16}\ts{7}{2}{40:37}\ts{7}{2}{40:57}\ts{7}{2}{41:18}\ts{7}{2}{41:39}\ts{7}{2}{41:59}\ts{7}{2}{42:20}\ts{7}{2}{42:41}\ts{7}{2}{43:02}\ts{7}{2}{43:23}\ts{7}{2}{43:43}\ts{7}{2}{44:05}\ts{7}{2}{44:27}\ts{7}{2}{44:47}\ts{7}{2}{45:10}\ts{7}{2}{45:47}\ts{7}{2}{46:07}\ts{7}{2}{46:28}\ts{7}{2}{46:49}\ts{7}{2}{47:10}\ts{7}{2}{47:30}.

        (Not said in the tutorial.) The same computation gives $\Gamma_{(y)}{}^2{}_{12}=\Gamma_{(y)}{}^2{}_{21}=1/r$ and all the others zero, the familiar polar-coordinate corrections.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exercise 4: on functions $\nabla_X$ reduces to $X$}

Complete the sketch of the proof that $\nabla_Xh:=Xh=\mathrm dh(X)$ satisfies the axioms, for the scaling part of the $C^\infty$-linearity, by indicating over each equal sign on which space the multiplication or addition is defined and justifying each step \ts{7}{2}{47:50}\ts{7}{2}{48:12}\ts{7}{2}{48:35}.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Scaling in the lower slot:} $\nabla_{gX}h=(gX)h\overset{!}{=}g\,(Xh)=g\,\nabla_Xh$. The first step is the definition (the product $gX$ is that of $\Gamma(TM)$, a function times a vector field); the second is the critical one and is checked pointwise: $\bigl((gX)h\bigr)(p)=g(p)\,(Xh)(p)$, because the product $gX$ on $\Gamma(TM)$ is defined pointwise as $g(p)X(p)$; the product $g(p)\cdot(Xh)(p)$ lives on $\R$, and pulling the point out again one gets the product in $C^\infty(M)$ of $g$ and the function $Xh$. The last step is the definition of $\nabla_X$ on a function \ts{7}{2}{48:56}\ts{7}{2}{49:16}\ts{7}{2}{49:39}\ts{7}{2}{50:00}\ts{7}{2}{50:25}\ts{7}{2}{50:46}\ts{7}{2}{51:07}\ts{7}{2}{51:27}.
  \item \emph{Additivity in the lower slot:} $\nabla_{X+Y}h=(X+Y)h=Xh+Yh=\nabla_Xh+\nabla_Yh$. The sum $X+Y$ lives on $\Gamma(TM)$; the middle equality is again pointwise (the sum of two vector fields is pointwise the sum of the vectors), then the sum lives on $C^\infty(M)$ \ts{7}{2}{51:48}\ts{7}{2}{52:11}\ts{7}{2}{52:32}\ts{7}{2}{52:53}\ts{7}{2}{53:14}\ts{7}{2}{53:38}.
  \item \emph{Additivity in the argument:} $\nabla_X(h+g)=X(h+g)=Xh+Xg$, the sum on $C^\infty(M)$ in all places, the equality in the middle holding by the pointwise evaluation \ts{7}{2}{53:58}\ts{7}{2}{54:19}\ts{7}{2}{54:40}\ts{7}{2}{55:03}.
  \item \emph{Leibniz rule:} $\nabla_X(fg)=X(fg)=X^i\,\partial_i(fg)=X^i\bigl(\partial_if\,g+f\,\partial_ig\bigr)=(Xf)g+f(Xg)$: expand into components and use the product rule from the previous tutorial; the plus lives on $C^\infty(M)$, the multiplications on $\R$ pointwise (or on $C^\infty(M)$) \ts{7}{2}{55:24}\ts{7}{2}{55:50}\ts{7}{2}{56:14}\ts{7}{2}{56:34}\ts{7}{2}{56:58}.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Key concepts}
\begin{keyconcepts}
\textbf{Retrieval questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Why is the directional derivative of a tensor field not available on a smooth manifold alone? Which extra structure is needed, and what is the wish list (the four axioms)?
  \item Why is $\nabla_X$ $C^\infty(M)$-linear in the lower slot but obeys a Leibniz rule in the upper one? Why does a vector at a point suffice in the lower slot?
  \item Define the connection coefficient functions $\Gamma^i{}_{jk}$ of a chart and say which index goes with the direction. How many functions fix $\nabla$?
  \item Write $(\nabla_XY)^i$, $(\nabla_X\omega)_i$ and $(\nabla_XT)^i{}_{jk}$ for a $(1,2)$ tensor and explain the sign rule. Derive $\nabla_{\partial_k}\mathrm dx^i=-\Gamma^i{}_{jk}\mathrm dx^j$.
  \item State and derive the transformation law of the $\Gamma$'s under a change of chart. Why are they not a tensor, and what does that imply for charts in which they vanish?
  \item What is a normal coordinate chart at a point? What can and what cannot be achieved, and what is the obstruction?
  \item What is the divergence of a vector field on a manifold with connection, and how does it contain the formulas for polar coordinates?
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Concepts.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Connection / covariant derivative with its four axioms (\cref{def:c07-connection}); $\nabla_Xf=Xf$ (\cref{def:c07-nablaf}).
  \item Connection coefficient functions $\Gamma^i{}_{jk}=\mathrm dx^i(\nabla_{\partial_k}\partial_j)$ (\cref{def:c07-gamma}); the same $(\dim M)^3$ functions fix $\nabla$ on every tensor field (\cref{prop:c07-fix}): plus for each upper index, minus for each lower index.
  \item The inhomogeneous transformation law of the $\Gamma$'s (\cref{thm:c07-transf}): a flat connection can have non-vanishing $\Gamma$'s in a curvilinear chart (polar coordinates: $\Gamma^1{}_{22}=-r$).
  \item Divergence (\cref{def:c07-div}); torsion as the antisymmetric part of $\Gamma$ (a tensor); normal coordinates at a point (\cref{thm:c07-normal}).
\end{itemize}
\end{keyconcepts}
