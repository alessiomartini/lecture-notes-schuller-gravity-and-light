\section{Fields and bundles}\label{sec:c06-bundles}

Welcome to the sixth lecture, whose title is \emph{Fields}, in the sense of the physicists \ts{6}{1}{0:00}. The notion of field was introduced into physics by Faraday, ``who did not know any mathematics by modern standards, and in fact he invented the fields to deal with that fact''. (As said in class, not checked.)
Today one draws many pictures, to pay tribute to the fact that all this can be understood by pictures as Faraday did; but to really work with it one needs the mathematics that underlies the pictures \emph{in a one-to-one manner}, and that is what will be introduced \ts{6}{1}{0:20}\ts{6}{1}{0:40}.

So far one focused on a single tangent space and a vector in it (or a covector); a chart gives a coordinate-induced basis there, $\partial/\partial x^i$ or $\mathrm dx^i$. But always at one point. As physicists one is interested in vector fields: at every point there is a vector (a few drawn on the board).
How to treat this technically? One can say ``just imagine at every point a vector'', but imagining is dangerous because you may imagine something different from me: one needs a proper way of saying what imagining is, and the proper way is the theory of \emph{bundles} \ts{6}{1}{1:02}\ts{6}{1}{1:22}\ts{6}{1}{1:42}\ts{6}{1}{2:02}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: bundle, total space, base space, projection]\label{def:c06-bundle}
A \textbf{bundle} (a smooth bundle, but only these are considered) is a triple $(E,\pi,M)$, where
\begin{itemize}
  \item $E$ is a smooth manifold, the \textbf{total space} (``I do not want to say $E$ all the time'');
  \item $M$ is a smooth manifold, the \textbf{base space};
  \item $\pi\colon E\to M$ is a smooth surjective map, the \textbf{projection map} of the bundle.
\end{itemize}
Any three pieces of data like this are called a bundle.
\end{definition}
\ts{6}{1}{2:29}\ts{6}{1}{2:49}\ts{6}{1}{3:11}\ts{6}{1}{3:32}.

\begin{example}[A cylinder over a circle]\label{ex:c06-cylinder}
Take $E$ a two-dimensional cylinder (continued arbitrarily), $M$ a circle (both with a topology and a smooth atlas), and $\pi$ any smooth map $E\to M$ which hits all points of $M$; the picture is for intuition only, ``it is not mathematics''. If one is not very imaginative, one sends all the points that lie on a vertical line of the cylinder to the point of the circle below it, and so on for the lines in the back;
but that is just one choice of the map $\pi$. Any choice of manifold $E$, of manifold $M$ and of a smooth surjection $\pi$ is a bundle: there are ``enormously many'' bundles \ts{6}{1}{3:32}\ts{6}{1}{3:53}\ts{6}{1}{4:14}\ts{6}{1}{4:35}\ts{6}{1}{4:55}\ts{6}{1}{5:15}\ts{6}{1}{5:37}\ts{6}{1}{5:57}.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: fibre over a point]\label{def:c06-fibre}
Let $(E,\pi,M)$ be a bundle and $p\in M$. The \textbf{fibre over $p$} is the preimage of the one-element set $\{p\}$ under the projection,
\[
  \pi^{-1}(\{p\})\subseteq E.
\]
\end{definition}
\ts{6}{1}{5:57}\ts{6}{1}{6:17}\ts{6}{1}{6:39}. In the cylinder example the preimage of a point of the circle is the whole vertical line over it. The fibres are not the primarily given thing: the bundle projection is, and the fibres are derived objects \ts{6}{1}{6:39}\ts{6}{1}{6:59}.

Now the basic idea of a field, ``so in a sense Faraday comes in very quickly'', though vector fields and so on will only come after some more work:

\begin{definition}[New concepts: section]\label{def:c06-section}
A \textbf{section} of a bundle $(E,\pi,M)$ is a map $\sigma\colon M\to E$ going the other way, from the base space to the total space, such that
\[
  \pi\circ\sigma=\mathrm{id}_M.
\]
(A \emph{smooth} section is a smooth such map.)
\end{definition}
\ts{6}{1}{7:20}\ts{6}{1}{7:41}\ts{6}{1}{8:02}\ts{6}{1}{8:23}\ts{6}{1}{8:44}. Without the condition $\sigma$ could send a base point to a point in a different fibre than the fibre over it; one does not want that. Going up with $\sigma$ and then projecting down lands at the same point. A section requires all the structure the bundle has to give: $E$ and $M$ as target and domain, and $\pi$ through this compatibility condition.
If the fibre over a point consisted of all the tangent vectors at the point, a section would go to a point and pick an element \emph{of the fibre over that point}, i.e.\ a vector in the tangent space at that point; ``you do not want a vector field where you say the vector at this point is a vector over here''. For the circle this is even true as a picture: the tangent spaces glued together are a cylinder
(as a set, ``you grind it, you put these all up''), and this is the so-called tangent bundle of the circle (to be made precise) \ts{6}{1}{9:04}\ts{6}{1}{9:25}\ts{6}{1}{9:45}\ts{6}{1}{10:06}\ts{6}{1}{10:26}.

\paragraph{Sections are the fields.} What type of field depends on the fibre. The base space is the manifold; for a \emph{vector field} the fibres are the tangent vector spaces, for a \emph{covector field} one takes a section of the cotangent bundle (fibres built from the cotangent spaces), for a \emph{tensor field} one builds over the base space fibres that are tensor spaces (higher dimensional, one cannot draw them any more).
``It is very simple what a field is: the intuition is right, at the point, in the tangent space, you have an object of that type'' \ts{6}{1}{10:26}\ts{6}{1}{10:48}\ts{6}{1}{11:08}.

\begin{aside}[Wave functions are sections]
Side remark on quantum mechanics: one talks about a wave function on position space and says that wave functions are functions. ``They are not'': they are sections of a $\C$-line bundle over the base space (to a point one associates a point of the fibre, a complex line), which is not quite the same as a function, because on such bundles one can define covariant derivatives (to be introduced later).
If one wants to do quantum mechanics in curved coordinates one runs into a wall with thinking the wave function is a function. ``Fields are always sections, and there are subtle differences'': on a function one acts with a covariant derivative in a trivial manner, on a section of a $\C$-bundle in a non-trivial manner, ``and that makes all the difference'', so even the name ``wave function'' is a misnomer
\ts{6}{1}{11:31}\ts{6}{1}{11:52}\ts{6}{1}{12:13}\ts{6}{1}{12:33}.
\end{aside}
\begin{coursenote}[Student question on the wave function]
A student asks whether $\psi$ is not rather the \emph{components} and whether tensors and components are being mixed up. Schuller: with the $\C$-bundle ``it is a little degenerate''; locally one can always represent such a section by a function, but the whole point is the global question and the question what the momentum operator is: a partial derivative, or a covariant derivative with the appropriate connection?
If $\psi$ were a function the covariant derivative would always equal the partial derivative, but this does not transform right in a curved space, e.g.\ for quantum mechanics on a sphere. ``It is just my answer to this question, it is really just an aside: the field concept even in quantum mechanics you have too'' \ts{6}{1}{12:54}\ts{6}{1}{13:14}\ts{6}{1}{13:34}\ts{6}{1}{13:55}\ts{6}{1}{14:15}.
\end{coursenote}

A student asks whether the definition of a section \emph{is} a field. Schuller: a section is a field, and what type of field depends on the choice of total space; the primary example for general relativity follows and makes the question clear \ts{6}{1}{14:35}\ts{6}{1}{14:56}\ts{6}{1}{15:17}.

\section{The tangent bundle}\label{sec:c06-tangent}

``We need this because we want to understand vector fields, and these are sections of the tangent bundle''; the construction is done in detail ``because it is fantastic practice of all the concepts we have'' \ts{6}{1}{14:35}\ts{6}{1}{14:56}. Let $(M,\mathcal O,\mathcal A)$ be a smooth manifold, which will be the base space of the bundle.

\paragraph{A. The set.} As a set only, define the \textbf{tangent bundle}
\[
  TM:=\bigsqcup_{p\in M}T_pM,
\]
the \emph{disjoint} union of all the tangent spaces: all the tangent spaces ``thrown into a big bag''. Why disjoint? A vector in $T_pM$ is quite a different thing from a vector in $T_qM$; if one throws them into one bag one still knows that this is a different object than that one. (Disjoint union is a union where one emphasises that there are no elements to be identified between the different sets; a student asks whether it is a special case of a union.)
Schuller's image: ``you all have a nose, and your noses constitute a vector field in this room; if I cut all your noses off and collect them in a bag, I can roll it around, I have this set, but it does not mean that your nose is all of a sudden Kurt's nose: they are still different noses''
\ts{6}{1}{15:17}\ts{6}{1}{15:40}\ts{6}{1}{16:01}\ts{6}{1}{16:22}\ts{6}{1}{16:43}\ts{6}{1}{17:03}\ts{6}{1}{17:24}\ts{6}{1}{17:45}.
This set is the total space of the bundle under construction; one calls it $TM$ because it is constructed from $M$.

\paragraph{B. The projection.} Define $\pi\colon TM\to M$, $\pi(X):=p$, where $p$ is the unique point of $M$ with $X\in T_pM$ (``the unique point $p$ such that the nose I took from the bag is the nose of Mr.\ $p$''). It is unique because the union is disjoint (if the noses were the same one would not know whose it is), and it is surjective because all tangent spaces were taken.
At this point one has a smooth manifold $M$, a mere \emph{set} $TM$ (far from a smooth manifold) and a surjective map $\pi$, of which one cannot say whether it is smooth, since one first needs a smooth structure on $TM$ \ts{6}{1}{17:45}\ts{6}{1}{18:06}\ts{6}{1}{18:28}\ts{6}{1}{18:52}\ts{6}{1}{19:14}\ts{6}{1}{19:37}.

\paragraph{C. A smooth manifold structure on $TM$.} A smooth manifold is first of all a topological manifold; first equip the set with a topology. Schuller proposes the \emph{coarsest topology such that $\pi$ is continuous}, the \textbf{initial topology} with respect to $\pi$ (where one knows the topology on the target and a map, and gives the domain the coarsest topology that makes the map continuous):
\[
  \mathcal O_{TM}:=\bigl\{\pi^{-1}(U)\mid U\in\mathcal O\bigr\}.
\]
Why the coarsest? One could take the discrete topology, in which $\pi$ is trivially continuous, but then the data one already has in $\pi$ is not used; it is clever to take not the finest topology but the coarsest that does not yet destroy the continuity of $\pi$. The preimages of open sets must be open for $\pi$ to be continuous, so these have to be included; the question is whether one has to include more,
and the answer is no: one shows (in the tutorial) that $\mathcal O_{TM}$ is a topology: it contains $\emptyset$ and $TM$, and is closed under finite intersections and arbitrary unions \ts{6}{1}{19:57}\ts{6}{1}{20:17}\ts{6}{1}{20:37}\ts{6}{1}{21:01}\ts{6}{1}{21:21}\ts{6}{1}{21:42}\ts{6}{1}{22:02}\ts{6}{1}{22:23}\ts{6}{1}{22:44}.
A student remarks that this is the definition of the coarsest topology; yes. For the \emph{final} topology (the other way around: the topology is known on the target and one wants one on the domain) one needs the concept of a topological basis, which is trickier; ``we are lucky here that I did not need to introduce that'' \ts{6}{1}{23:04}\ts{6}{1}{23:25}.
\begin{coursenote}[A flaw in this topology]
The proof that $\mathcal O_{TM}$ is a topology is a one-liner: $\pi^{-1}$ commutes with unions and intersections, $\pi^{-1}(\emptyset)=\emptyset$ and $\pi^{-1}(M)=TM$. But these open sets are unions of \emph{whole fibres}, so no open set can separate two vectors of the same fibre, and $TM$ with this topology could not be locally homeomorphic to $\R^{2d}$ (the chart maps $\xi_x$ below would not be continuous with respect to it: the open set $\xi_x^{-1}(\text{ball})$ is not of the form $\pi^{-1}(U)$). The topology that makes the charts below homeomorphisms is the one \emph{induced by the atlas}
(equivalently the coarsest topology in which $\pi$ and all the component maps $X\mapsto\mathrm dx^i_{\pi(X)}(X)$ are continuous). This is the usual construction; the text of the lecture is the one above, which is kept here as said. (Not said in class: a lecture error to be corrected; checked by the note-taker, not verified in the Geometric Anatomy notes [?].)
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{D. A smooth atlas on $TM$ from the atlas of $M$.} The question is whether $TM$ is a topological manifold and whether one can choose charts with smooth transition maps: construct an atlas, using only data one already has, namely the atlas $\mathcal A$ of $M$ \ts{6}{1}{23:25}\ts{6}{1}{23:46}\ts{6}{1}{24:06}\ts{6}{1}{24:27}.
Picture: $M=S^1$, a point $p$ with the fibre over it, i.e.\ $T_pM$, and the bundle as the collection of all fibres. On $M$ one has a chart $(U,x)$ mapping an open piece to $\R^1$. Take the disjoint union $TU$ of all the fibres over points of $U$ (the part of the bundle over $U$) and map it by a chart $\xi_x$ to a two-dimensional space: in general to $\R^{2d}$, twice the dimension of the base manifold
(``not so surprising: each tangent space has dimension of the manifold, so putting all the tangent spaces over each of the points one has a $2d$-dimensional manifold, if one has a manifold at all'') \ts{6}{1}{24:47}\ts{6}{1}{25:08}\ts{6}{1}{25:29}\ts{6}{1}{25:49}\ts{6}{1}{26:10}\ts{6}{1}{26:32}\ts{6}{1}{26:52}\ts{6}{1}{27:13}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the chart $\xi_x$ on $TU$]\label{def:c06-xi}
For $(U,x)\in\mathcal A$ define $TU:=\pi^{-1}(U)=\bigsqcup_{p\in U}T_pM$ and
\[
  \xi_x\colon TU\to\R^{2d},\qquad\xi_x(X):=\Bigl(x^1(\pi(X)),\dots,x^d(\pi(X)),\;\mathrm dx^1_{\pi(X)}(X),\dots,\mathrm dx^d_{\pi(X)}(X)\Bigr).
\]
The first $d$ entries are the coordinates of the base point of $X$ (``I stored in the chart the information of the coordinates of the base point''), the last $d$ are the \emph{components} of $X\in T_{\pi(X)}M$ with respect to the chart-induced basis: indeed $X=X^i\,\partial/\partial x^i|_{\pi(X)}$ and
$\mathrm dx^j_{\pi(X)}(X)=X^i\,\mathrm dx^j(\partial/\partial x^i)=X^j$ by the dual basis property (\cref{thm:c05-dualbasis}), a linear map pulling the numbers out.
\end{definition}
\ts{6}{1}{27:33}\ts{6}{1}{28:35}\ts{6}{1}{29:37}\ts{6}{1}{30:40}.

\paragraph{The inverse chart.} $\xi_x^{-1}\colon\xi_x(TU)\subseteq\R^{2d}\to TU$ must reconstruct, from a collection of numbers $(\alpha^1,\dots,\alpha^d,\beta^1,\dots,\beta^d)$, a vector in the appropriate tangent space. The numbers $\beta^i$ are the components of the vector with respect to the basis $\partial/\partial x^i$ at the base point, and the base point is $x^{-1}(\alpha^1,\dots,\alpha^d)=\pi(X)$:
\[
  \xi_x^{-1}(\alpha,\beta)=\beta^i\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\Big|_{x^{-1}(\alpha)}.
\]
So ``the nose'' is fully reconstructed, whichever nose was picked from the bag \ts{6}{1}{31:43}\ts{6}{1}{32:07}\ts{6}{1}{32:28}\ts{6}{1}{32:48}\ts{6}{1}{33:08}.

\paragraph{The transition maps are smooth.} What matters is whether the atlas is smooth. For another chart $(V,y)\in\mathcal A$ with $U\cap V\neq\emptyset$, compute $\xi_y\circ\xi_x^{-1}$ on $\xi_x(T(U\cap V))$: it eats $(\alpha,\beta)\in\R^{2d}$ and one has to check that it is smooth. Applying $\xi_y$ to $\xi_x^{-1}(\alpha,\beta)=\beta^m\,\partial/\partial x^m|_{x^{-1}(\alpha)}$, whose base point is $x^{-1}(\alpha)$:
the first $d$ components (the $i$-th) are $y^i\bigl(x^{-1}(\alpha)\bigr)=(y\circ x^{-1})^i(\alpha)$, the old acquaintance, \emph{the chart transition map on the base space}; the next $d$ (the $i$-th) are
\[
  \mathrm dy^i_{x^{-1}(\alpha)}\Bigl(\beta^m\frac{\partial}{\partial x^m}\Big|_{x^{-1}(\alpha)}\Bigr)=\beta^m\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x^m}\Big|_{x^{-1}(\alpha)}(y^i)=\beta^m\,\partial_m\bigl(y^i\circ x^{-1}\bigr)(\alpha),
\]
pulling the numbers $\beta^m$ out by linearity of the covector and using that a vector acting on a coordinate function is the gradient of that function (the definition of $\mathrm dy^i$). In summary
\[
  \xi_y\circ\xi_x^{-1}(\alpha,\beta)=\Bigl((y\circ x^{-1})(\alpha),\;\beta^m\,\partial_m(y\circ x^{-1})(\alpha)\Bigr),
\]
the transformation behaviour of the components of a vector under a chart transition (\cref{thm:c05-changeV}). Is it smooth as a map $\R^{2d}\to\R^{2d}$ (undergraduate analysis)? The first block is the transition map of the smooth atlas of $M$, smooth. The second block is \emph{linear in $\beta$}, certainly smooth, and in $\alpha$ it is the derivative of the transition maps, which are $C^\infty$, so deriving them once still keeps them smooth (the $x$ goes away: it is the symbol $\partial_m(y^i\circ x^{-1})$ evaluated at $\alpha$).
Hooray, everything is smooth \ts{6}{1}{33:29}\ts{6}{1}{34:32}\ts{6}{1}{35:33}\ts{6}{1}{36:35}\ts{6}{1}{37:37}\ts{6}{1}{38:40}\ts{6}{1}{39:41}\ts{6}{1}{40:45}\ts{6}{1}{41:47}.
\begin{coursenote}[Slips on the board]
Schuller first writes $\xi_x$ where $\xi_y$ is needed and has a student point out that there were ``too many $i$'s'': the summation index of the components is renamed $m$, and the missing $y$ in $\xi_y$ and in the $\mathrm dy^i$ is added (``I am sorry, I need a $y$ here and you need a $y$ there''); a student asks where the $y$ comes from: from the atlas, the other chart $(V,y)$ with $U\cap V\neq\emptyset$ and the transition map between the images of the intersection, as always. A student also asks whether two-dimensional vectors and covectors are meant: no, this is a vector to which a gradient is applied, selecting a component, which is a number; the components of a vector with respect to the chart-induced basis are obtained by acting with the chart-induced dual basis
\ts{6}{1}{36:35}\ts{6}{1}{36:55}\ts{6}{1}{37:17}\ts{6}{1}{39:41}\ts{6}{1}{40:03}\ts{6}{1}{40:23}.
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{Upshot.} $(TM,\mathcal O_{TM},\mathcal A_{TM})$ with $\mathcal A_{TM}=\{(TU,\xi_x)\mid(U,x)\in\mathcal A\}$ is a smooth manifold (of dimension $2d$); $\pi$ is smooth, since in the chart representatives it is $(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto\alpha$, a projection to the first components. Hence $(TM,\pi,M)$ is a bundle in the sense of \cref{def:c06-bundle}, called the \textbf{tangent bundle} (from \cref{ex:c06-cylinder}: for $M=S^1$ the cylinder).
That was a lot of work to make a very intuitive picture precise, but it works for just any smooth manifold, and one even gets an atlas on $TM$ \emph{from the data below}, so one can work in an atlas exactly as before \ts{6}{1}{41:47}\ts{6}{1}{42:08}\ts{6}{1}{42:30}\ts{6}{1}{42:50}\ts{6}{1}{43:10}.

\section{Vector fields}\label{sec:c06-vectorfields}

The reason for the effort of making the total space a smooth manifold is now clear \ts{6}{1}{43:10}\ts{6}{1}{43:32}. One could look at vector fields that are just sections, without $TM$ being a manifold, but for a notion of \emph{smooth} vector field one needs smooth manifolds on both sides.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: smooth vector field]\label{def:c06-vf}
A \textbf{smooth vector field} $\chi$ on $M$ is a smooth section of the tangent bundle, i.e.\ a smooth map $\chi\colon M\to TM$ with $\pi\circ\chi=\mathrm{id}_M$.
\end{definition}
\ts{6}{1}{43:32}\ts{6}{1}{43:52}\ts{6}{1}{44:13}\ts{6}{1}{44:33}\ts{6}{1}{44:55}. (Schuller's blackboard letter for it is ``$\chi$'', heard as ``shy''.) In the beginning the intuition was right: a vector field is an association of a vector to each point. But how does one say that this association is \emph{smooth}? One can say ``it just goes like this'', but that is not technically good; now one has the technical way
\ts{6}{1}{45:16}\ts{6}{1}{45:36}.

\paragraph{The picture on the circle.} Look at $TS^1\to S^1$, drawn as a cylinder. On the circle a vector field (here ``your world'', the circle): a vector pointing one way, then smaller, then zero, then turned around, then zero again, then growing a little. As a section: for every point of the circle one picks the point of the fibre over it that represents the vector, the zero vector at the points where it vanishes, a point in the mathematically negative direction elsewhere, and so on;
the section is a curve on the cylinder that goes once around, and ``associates all these points, and that corresponds to this vector field'' \ts{6}{1}{45:58}\ts{6}{1}{46:18}\ts{6}{1}{46:38}\ts{6}{1}{46:59}\ts{6}{1}{47:19}\ts{6}{1}{47:40}.
(Schuller says ``the smooth vector field on a cotangent bundle'' by mistake: the tangent bundle is meant.)

\subsection{The $C^\infty(M)$-module of vector fields}

Now ``a very very very important point, and it is a point that makes you understand stuff that otherwise you wouldn't'', with the awe-inspiring headline \emph{the $C^\infty(M)$-module $\Gamma(TM)$} \ts{6}{1}{47:40}\ts{6}{1}{48:02}\ts{6}{1}{48:25}.

\paragraph{The ring $C^\infty(M)$.} The set of smooth functions $C^\infty(M)$ is already a real vector space (\cref{def:c05-velocity}). But one can also multiply two smooth functions pointwise and get a smooth function: it is a vector space with a ``fat multiplication'' that takes two elements to an element. Addition is commutative and associative, with neutral element the function that vanishes everywhere, and with inverse $-f$;
multiplication is commutative and associative, with neutral element the constant function $1$. Is there an inverse for every non-zero function? No: a function that is not the zero function can still vanish at some points. So the structure satisfies all the axioms of a field (as for the real numbers or $\mathbb Q$) \emph{except} the existence of multiplicative inverses for all non-zero elements (the distributive law holds): it is not a field but a \textbf{ring}
\ts{6}{1}{48:25}\ts{6}{1}{48:46}\ts{6}{1}{49:06}\ts{6}{1}{49:27}\ts{6}{1}{49:47}\ts{6}{1}{50:08}\ts{6}{1}{50:30}.

\paragraph{The module $\Gamma(TM)$.} $\Gamma(TM)$ denotes the set of all smooth sections of $TM$, i.e.\ of all smooth vector fields (for a general bundle $\Gamma(E)$ is the set of all sections). It carries an addition. A vector field $\chi$ acts on a smooth function $f$ giving again a function: $(\chi f)(p):=\chi(p)(f)$, where $\chi(p)\in T_pM$ is the vector at $p$ and acts on $f$ as in \cref{def:c05-velocity}
(the evaluation point ``jumps in between''; a vector at a point gives a number, a vector field at every point gives a smooth function; one is writing ``$\chi f$'' for this new type of action). Define
\[
  (\chi+\tilde\chi)f:=\chi f+\tilde\chi f,\qquad(g\chi)f:=g\cdot(\chi f)\quad(g\in C^\infty(M)),
\]
i.e.\ pointwise $\bigl(\chi+\tilde\chi\bigr)(p)=\chi(p)+\tilde\chi(p)$ and $(g\chi)(p)=g(p)\chi(p)$ [the formulas as written on the board: addition through the action on $f$, a tilde on the second field was missed first and corrected after a student's remark]. One can of course multiply a vector field by a real number and make it a real vector space, but one can do more: take a whole \emph{function} $g$, so that at each point there is a different number, and scale the vector field differently at each point; this still gives a vector field.
Allowing this, $\Gamma(TM)$ satisfies all the vector space axioms \emph{but} the scalars by which one multiplies are only the elements of the ring $C^\infty(M)$. Mathematicians do not call this a vector space over a ring but a \textbf{module}: ``it deserves an extra name because modules do not have all the properties that vector spaces have, if you start multiplying by ring elements instead of field elements you no longer have all the nice theorems for vector spaces''
\ts{6}{1}{50:30}\ts{6}{1}{51:32}\ts{6}{1}{52:34}\ts{6}{1}{53:35}\ts{6}{1}{54:37}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: module over a ring]\label{def:c06-module}
The set $\Gamma(TM)$ of smooth vector fields, with the addition and the multiplication by smooth functions above, is a \textbf{$C^\infty(M)$-module}. (A module is a structure satisfying the vector space axioms (\cref{def:c03-vs}) with scalars in a ring instead of a field. The name: the German/Latin \emph{modulus}, a measure [not said in class, to be checked].)
\end{definition}
\ts{6}{1}{54:57}\ts{6}{1}{55:18}.

\paragraph{No basis in general.} This sounds like mathematical pedantry but it is not. Depending on the choice of set theory (one never talked about the axioms of set theory: in the beginning ``we all know what a set is''), the statement ``every vector space has a basis'' requires the axiom of choice, so ``very deep down this goes very high up''. But \emph{no such result exists for modules}: if the objects by which one multiplies do not form a field, one can no longer prove that a basis exists.
That is a shame, because it means that for $\Gamma(TM)$ one cannot in general choose $d$ vector fields $\chi_1,\dots,\chi_d\in\Gamma(TM)$ once and for all such that every vector field is $\chi=f^i\chi_i$ with component \emph{functions} $f^i\in C^\infty(M)$ (scaling with a function at each point). The question is whether there exist global vector fields on the whole manifold which can be chosen once and for all and in which one can expand every vector field globally. If $\Gamma(TM)$ were a vector space, or if the coefficients always had multiplicative inverses when not the zero function, then this would need to be possible, with the set theory chosen implicitly all the time.
For some manifolds it works anyway, but for interesting ones it does not \ts{6}{1}{55:18}\ts{6}{1}{56:19}\ts{6}{1}{57:21}\ts{6}{1}{58:24}\ts{6}{1}{59:26}.

\begin{example}[The sphere cannot be combed]\label{ex:c06-hairy}
The simple counterexample is the sphere $S^2$. Try to find a \emph{single} smooth vector field that can be an element of a basis. ``Imagine the sphere has hair, everywhere: can you comb it?'' One can put the hair somehow like this, with the root of the hair at the point and the tip the vector, but if the field is smooth then at the top one gets in a problem: it has to vanish there, and Schuller says that Morse theory can tell this abstractly: one can prove that every smooth vector field on the sphere must vanish somewhere, and still be smooth.
If now one takes a second vector field, which one can let go down at a place where the first one vanishes, it has to vanish somewhere else, coming from behind. At a point where one of the fields vanishes, a vector field with a component in that direction cannot be reproduced. So there are smooth vector fields on the sphere, but \emph{none that does not vanish somewhere}, and wherever it vanishes it loses its power as a basis element at that point. ``You cannot comb a sphere'':
this is related to $\Gamma(TM)$ only being a $C^\infty(M)$-module and not a vector space, and not having a basis
\ts{6}{1}{59:46}\ts{6}{1}{1:00:07}\ts{6}{1}{1:00:28}\ts{6}{1}{1:00:48}\ts{6}{1}{1:01:09}\ts{6}{1}{1:01:30}\ts{6}{1}{1:01:51}\ts{6}{1}{1:02:11}.
\end{example}
\begin{coursenote}[Hairy ball theorem]
That every continuous (hence every smooth) tangent vector field on the even-dimensional sphere $S^2$ has a zero is the \emph{hairy ball theorem}; Schuller attributes the abstract proof to Morse theory (strictly it follows from the Poincar\'e--Hopf theorem, since $\chi(S^2)=2\neq0$). A single nowhere vanishing field is what a basis element would have to be. (Not said in class.)
\end{coursenote}

So what does one do? One always expands a vector field in terms of components in a basis only on a domain where this is possible. That can be done: the basis can come from a chart, and one can still say ``I can always go into a chart, who cares about the real world''; but as soon as it comes to vector fields one \emph{no longer necessarily can, globally}, though locally still. ``This is yet another blow to the idea, let us just do it in charts.'' ``This is not philosophy, it is just mathematics: locally you can do this'' \ts{6}{1}{1:02:11}\ts{6}{1}{1:02:32}\ts{6}{1}{1:02:52}\ts{6}{1}{1:03:13}\ts{6}{1}{1:03:34}.

\paragraph{Chart-induced vector fields.} One need not despair. Since $\partial/\partial x^i$ had always a $p$, one can now define the symbol without the $p$ as a map $M\to TM$ (on the chart domain $U$), smooth, assigning to $p$ in a smooth way $\partial/\partial x^i|_p$: a \textbf{chart-induced vector field}. This is the basis of the tangent space at each point of $U$, the vector fields of the chart distributed over the chart domain; this is not in contradiction with the above, ``because it is local, it is only on the chart domain''. In fact charts fail at similar points: if one tries to write just any vector field as a linear combination of them, at a point where one of the chart fields vanishes one cannot reproduce a component in that direction;
``it fails only at a point, it is very subtle, but it fails'' \ts{6}{1}{1:03:34}\ts{6}{1}{1:03:55}\ts{6}{1}{1:04:16}\ts{6}{1}{1:04:37}\ts{6}{1}{1:04:57}.

\paragraph{Student question: can I choose a basis on a local area?} A student: one can choose a basis for a local area, but one must make sure not to leave the area. Schuller: yes, but the area could be arbitrary; for instance the manifold $\R^2$: its vector fields are still a $C^\infty(M)$-module, however a module that \emph{happens to have} a basis. ``I do not say it cannot have a basis, I say it is not guaranteed to have a basis.'' One could think this is a remote mathematical possibility; but already the sphere does not work. That is the logical status, and ``it does not prevent us from doing anything; it just is another blow to the idea of going to charts and being done with it''
\ts{6}{1}{1:05:18}\ts{6}{1}{1:05:39}\ts{6}{1}{1:05:59}\ts{6}{1}{1:06:20}.

\section{Tensor fields}\label{sec:c06-tensorfields}

With ten minutes left one can introduce the final object: tensor fields. ``Almost everything in general relativity that you put on the manifold, matter, but also the geometry itself, is given by a tensor field'' \ts{6}{1}{1:06:20}\ts{6}{1}{1:06:40}. So far one constructed the sections over the tangent bundle, in vulgar language the vector fields. In exactly the same way, which Schuller asks to do in detail in the tutorial, one constructs the \textbf{cotangent bundle} $T^*M=\bigsqcup_pT^*_pM$ with $\pi\colon T^*M\to M$ and the charts that map a covector to the coordinates of its base point and the components of the covector with respect to the $\mathrm dx^i$ basis, makes it a smooth manifold, and considers its sections, the \emph{covector fields}.
Again one can scale covector fields by functions and the set $\Gamma(T^*M)$ of covector fields is also a $C^\infty(M)$-module \ts{6}{1}{1:07:01}\ts{6}{1}{1:07:21}\ts{6}{1}{1:07:42}\ts{6}{1}{1:08:04}\ts{6}{1}{1:08:25}.
\begin{coursenote}[The cotangent chart]
By analogy: for $(U,x)$ in the atlas, $\xi^*_x\colon T^*U\to\R^{2d}$, $\xi^*_x(\omega)=\bigl(x^i(\pi(\omega)),\,\omega(\partial/\partial x^i|_{\pi(\omega)})\bigr)$; the transition maps are then $(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto\bigl((y\circ x^{-1})(\alpha),\,\beta_m\,\partial_i(x^m\circ y^{-1})((y\circ x^{-1})(\alpha))\bigr)$ and are smooth for the same reason. (Not said in class: announced as a tutorial exercise.)
\end{coursenote}

Just as $T_pM$ and $T^*_pM$ were the building blocks to understand every tensor, these two $C^\infty(M)$-modules are the building blocks of every tensor field:

\begin{definition}[New concepts: $(r,s)$ tensor field]\label{def:c06-tensorfield}
An \textbf{$(r,s)$ tensor field} $T$ on $M$ is a $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear map
\[
  T\colon\underbrace{\Gamma(T^*M)\times\dots\times\Gamma(T^*M)}_{r}\times\underbrace{\Gamma(TM)\times\dots\times\Gamma(TM)}_{s}\longrightarrow C^\infty(M),
\]
i.e.\ it eats $r$ covector fields and $s$ vector fields and returns a smooth \emph{function} (not a real number: ``it is a real number point by point and everything is supposed to be smooth''), and it is multilinear with respect to addition and with respect to multiplication by \emph{functions} in each entry: a multilinear map between $C^\infty(M)$-modules ($C^\infty(M)$ is trivially a module over itself, as $\R$ is a vector space over $\R$).
\end{definition}
\ts{6}{1}{1:08:25}\ts{6}{1}{1:08:46}\ts{6}{1}{1:09:06}\ts{6}{1}{1:09:29}\ts{6}{1}{1:09:49}\ts{6}{1}{1:10:10}.
Of course it is also $\R$-multilinear, but that does not capture the whole aspect: the natural requirement is $C^\infty(M)$-multilinearity. This is an incredibly practical requirement: later one will define the Riemann tensor and the torsion tensor with respect to additional structure, and to check that such an object is a tensor field one has to check multilinearity in each entry \emph{with respect to functions, not with respect to scaling by real numbers}.
Many objects one can write down are $\R$-multilinear maps of vector fields but not $C^\infty(M)$-linear; only the second type qualifies as a tensor field \ts{6}{1}{1:10:30}\ts{6}{1}{1:10:50}\ts{6}{1}{1:11:11}.

\begin{example}[The gradient $\mathrm df$ as a covector field]\label{ex:c06-df}
For a smooth function $f$ one defines $\mathrm df\colon\Gamma(TM)\to C^\infty(M)$ by $\mathrm df(\chi):=\chi f$, i.e.\ $\bigl(\mathrm df(\chi)\bigr)(p):=\mathrm df_p\bigl(\chi(p)\bigr)=\chi(p)(f)$ (\cref{def:c05-cotangent}). It has exactly the structure of a $(0,1)$ tensor field, with $r=0$ and $s=1$. As the action of a vector field on a function it uses the new application rule above: $\chi f$ is a function, evaluated at $p$ as $\chi(p)(f)$, ``the evaluation point jumps in between'', standard notation for a vector field acting on a function.
One can check (``maybe you want to do it at home'') that $\mathrm df$ is $C^\infty(M)$-linear in $\chi$: if one scales $\chi$ by a function $g$ one gets $g$ times the result, since $((g\chi)f)(p)=g(p)\,\chi(p)(f)$. So it is a $C^\infty(M)$-linear map and hence a tensor field: ``one can make of the gradient at a point a gradient vector field; there you see, I said it: a gradient \emph{covector} field''
\ts{6}{1}{1:11:11}\ts{6}{1}{1:12:13}\ts{6}{1}{1:13:15}\ts{6}{1}{1:14:18}.
\end{example}
Schuller also mentions: in the lecture the first idea is to define $\mathrm df$ at a point, then to extend to a field; the first attempt is ``no, hang on, I want it differently'', and ends with the version above. Further examples will follow in later lectures.
