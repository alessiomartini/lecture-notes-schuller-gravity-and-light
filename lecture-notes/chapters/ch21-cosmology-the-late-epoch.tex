\section{Several matter types: what we recall}\label{sec:c21-recall}

``We studied cosmology at earlier times; now we look at later times'' \ts{21}{1}{0:00}\ts{21}{1}{0:21}. The new feature: \emph{several matter types at the same time}, as some of the audience asked at the break: ``you cannot say, let us assume the universe is made of photons: it is not, there is lots of stuff in the universe'' \ts{21}{1}{0:46}\ts{21}{1}{1:12}.

\begin{coursenote}[Why these simple models]
The purpose of the lectures is ``to give you a taste and the basic ideas''; whatever assumption one is unhappy with, one generalises it, and people did. If one drops some of the Killing symmetries one gets non-isotropic universes, for instance the Bianchi models; here the homogeneous and isotropic Friedmann--Robertson--Walker (FRW) model is used. Some modelling is always needed, since the local structure of the universe is extremely rich and including every detail the Einstein equations cannot be solved. The honest way is to \emph{make the assumptions explicit}: ``there is nothing dishonourable about making such assumptions, it is an assumption nevertheless, you know the game'' \ts{21}{1}{1:32}\ts{21}{1}{1:53}\ts{21}{1}{2:14}\ts{21}{1}{2:34}\ts{21}{1}{2:55}.
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{What is recalled.} Each matter type $i=1,\dots,I$ is a perfect fluid with a \emph{linear equation of state}
\[
  P_i=\omega_i\,\rho_i,\qquad \omega_i=\text{const},
\]
with a different constant $\omega_i$ for each type, hence different pressures and densities \ts{21}{1}{2:55}\ts{21}{1}{3:16}. From the earlier lectures (the early epoch, chapter~\ref{ch:c20}) one has: independent of the matter type, $H^2$ scales like the density, which scales like $a^{-n(\omega)}$ with $n(\omega)=3(1+\omega)$ \ts{21}{1}{3:36}\ts{21}{1}{3:57}. The lecturer rebuilds the table, and asks that it is not erased (the board is erased from the top, ``the erasing procedure is not homogeneous'' \ts{21}{1}{4:20}):

(``instead of remembering the $\omega$ we could remember the $n$'': $\omega=\tfrac13$ gives $3\cdot\tfrac43=4$.) The cosmological constant is special, it evolves exponentially (for a constant Hubble function); the others follow the power law $a\propto t^{2/n}$. For all matter types except the cosmological constant the density goes like $\rho\propto t^{-2}$ \ts{21}{1}{7:07}\ts{21}{1}{7:28}. The spatial curvature is \emph{not} listed as a matter type: the acceleration equation is rewritten below with it separate \ts{21}{1}{5:04}. (Radiation is called ``$\gamma$'', like a photon; dust is called ``matter'', $m$, in the cosmological jargon, see below.)

\section{The Hubble function and density parameters}\label{sec:c21-hubble}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the Hubble function]\label{def:c21-hubble}
The \textbf{Hubble function} is
\[
  H(t):=\frac{\dot a}{a}.
\]
(Named after Edwin Hubble, who observed the expansion of the universe [not said in class, not checked].) If there is only a cosmological constant it is a constant, the \emph{Hubble constant}; otherwise a function of time.
\end{definition}
\ts{21}{1}{7:28}\ts{21}{1}{7:48}
The Hubble function is related to the \textbf{age of the universe}: as $\rho\propto t^{-2}$ and $H^2\propto\rho$ one gets $H\propto t^{-1}$ (``that we will show in the tutorials''; the derivation is in \cref{sec:c21-t15}), and $t=0$ is the big bang. So, roughly speaking, \emph{$H^{-1}$ is the age of the universe}, and $H$ can be measured, being the expansion rate. ``It is a rather remarkable number: the universe has an age, because it has a birthday, the big bang, let us hope we do not celebrate it ever'' (a joke in class). ``Einstein did not set out to calculate the age of the universe, it is a rather preposterous aim, but nevertheless it drops out'' \ts{21}{1}{8:08}\ts{21}{1}{8:30}\ts{21}{1}{8:50}\ts{21}{1}{9:12}\ts{21}{1}{9:33}.

\subsection{Density parameters}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: density parameters]\label{def:c21-omega}
For each matter type $i$, with density $\rho_i$, the \textbf{density parameter} is
\[
  \Omega_i:=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\,\frac{\rho_i}{H^2},\qquad i=1,\dots,I
\]
(``$8\pi G/3$ is a bunch of constants, they are put there because the equations become perfectly simple if they are absorbed''). The density enters \emph{linearly}, hence the name; it is normalised with the Hubble function squared. The lecture calls them ``parameters'' rather than functions because, for linear equations of state, they turn out to be constants in a universe with a single matter type; with several types they vary with time (see \cref{sec:c21-dominant}). ``In this jargon you should never ever deduce anything from what it is called, you only look at the definition.''
\end{definition}
\ts{21}{1}{9:33}\ts{21}{1}{9:55}\ts{21}{1}{10:16}\ts{21}{1}{10:37}\ts{21}{1}{10:58}\ts{21}{1}{11:18}\ts{21}{1}{11:39}

\begin{coursenote}[Constant or not]
Written out: since $H^2\propto\rho$ only for a single matter type, $\Omega_i=\frac{8\pi G}3\rho_i/H^2$ is a constant only in a universe with one type; with several types $\rho_i\propto a^{-n_i}$ while $H^2$ is a combination of all of them, so $\Omega_i$ varies with time (this is exactly the content of \cref{sec:c21-dominant}). (Not said in class: the general statement; the lecture speaks of constants for linear evolution laws.)
\end{coursenote}

This works for matter. The spatial curvature $\kappa\in\{-1,0,1\}$ (the three possible homogeneous and isotropic three-geometries, the scale being absorbed in $a$) and the cosmological constant $\Lambda$ are not matter in the same sense, but one can treat them like it: ``fake matter'' \ts{21}{1}{11:59}\ts{21}{1}{12:20}\ts{21}{1}{12:40}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: fake density parameters]\label{def:c21-fake}
The two further parameters are
\[
  \Omega_\kappa:=-\frac{\kappa}{H^2a^2},\qquad \Omega_\Lambda:=\frac{\Lambda}{3H^2}.
\]
$\Omega_\kappa$ is a \emph{fake} density parameter (``my personal terminology, you might equally drop it''); $\Omega_\Lambda$ is a ``pseudo-fake'' one (``it could also be called a density parameter''). The cosmological constant is treated as a fluid with $\omega=-1$; the curvature is not counted among the $I$ matter types: it is an extra one.
\end{definition}
\ts{21}{1}{18:33}\ts{21}{1}{18:58}\ts{21}{1}{19:18}\ts{21}{1}{19:39}\ts{21}{1}{19:59}\ts{21}{1}{20:20}\ts{21}{1}{20:40}
(The point is: ``ignore the terminology; on the right of these definitions are quantities with a physical meaning, on the left a combination of them, and it is nicer to work with these combinations''.)

\begin{coursenote}[Student questions: is $\Lambda$ matter? is curvature matter?]
Students ask what is strange about moving a term to the other side of the equation, and what the physical meaning of the fake matter is. The answers: (1) \emph{Cosmological constant.} In the Einstein equations $\Lambda$ comes in at the level of the action as an addition to the Lagrangian density $\sqrt{-g}\,(\dots+\text{matter Lagrangian})$; ``it is the associativity of addition'' that allows one to count it as matter or as geometry: it is an artificial distinction to say it belongs to the geometry and not to the matter, for historical reasons it was added on the geometric side. Its energy-momentum tensor is $\Lambda$ times the metric (up to dressing factors), the fluid has negative pressure: ``it is a funny matter because it is everywhere, of negative pressure; but a rubber band that you stretch also has negative pressure, it pulls you in''. (2) \emph{It is not a force.} A student asks whether pulling things together or apart brings back a gravitational force; no: $\Lambda$ contributes to the energy-momentum tensor, hence to the curvature of spacetime, ``it just contributes to the curvature via the Einstein equation''; all particle trajectories are modified, but there is no force. (3) \emph{Curvature $\kappa$} on the other hand does not appear anywhere in the Lagrangian: it comes from a totally different point, a symmetry assumption (three choices of ansatz), so to call it matter is ``a little strange''; formally one can, and then treat all contributions on the same footing \ts{21}{1}{12:40}\ts{21}{1}{13:01}\ts{21}{1}{13:22}\ts{21}{1}{13:43}\ts{21}{1}{14:03}\ts{21}{1}{14:23}\ts{21}{1}{14:45}\ts{21}{1}{15:06}\ts{21}{1}{15:26}\ts{21}{1}{15:47}\ts{21}{1}{16:07}\ts{21}{1}{16:28}\ts{21}{1}{16:49}\ts{21}{1}{17:10}\ts{21}{1}{17:30}\ts{21}{1}{17:51}\ts{21}{1}{18:12}.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{theorem}[The Friedmann and acceleration equations in density parameters]\label{thm:c21-friedmann}
With the matter types $i=1,\dots,I$ (not including $\Lambda$ and $\kappa$)
\begin{align}
  \sum_{i=1}^{I}\Omega_i+\Omega_\Lambda+\Omega_\kappa&=1,\label{eq:c21-friedmann}\\
  \frac{1}{H^2}\frac{\ddot a}{a}&=-\frac12\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{I}(1+3\omega_i)\,\Omega_i-2\,\Omega_\Lambda\Bigr)=-\frac12\sum_{i\in\text{matter}\,+\,\Lambda}(1+3\omega_i)\,\Omega_i,\label{eq:c21-accel}
\end{align}
where in the last sum $\Lambda$ is counted as a type with $\omega=-1$, and \emph{not} the curvature.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(Not done on the board, ``you can check''; the verification is added, not said in class.) The Friedmann equation is $H^2=\frac{8\pi G}3\sum_i\rho_i-\frac{\kappa}{a^2}+\frac\Lambda3$; dividing by $H^2$ gives \eqref{eq:c21-friedmann}. The acceleration equation is $\frac{\ddot a}a=-\frac{4\pi G}{3}\sum_i(\rho_i+3P_i)+\frac\Lambda3$; with $P_i=\omega_i\rho_i$ and dividing by $H^2$ this is $-\frac12\sum_i(1+3\omega_i)\Omega_i+\Omega_\Lambda$, and $\Lambda$ as a type with $\omega=-1$ contributes $-\frac12(1-3)\Omega_\Lambda=+\Omega_\Lambda$.
\end{proof}
\ts{21}{1}{20:40}\ts{21}{1}{21:04}\ts{21}{1}{21:25}\ts{21}{1}{21:45}\ts{21}{1}{22:06}\ts{21}{1}{22:27}\ts{21}{1}{22:47}\ts{21}{1}{23:09}\ts{21}{1}{23:30}\ts{21}{1}{23:51}\ts{21}{1}{24:11}
In the lecture, the first equation is the sum over the non-fake matter (``including the cosmological constant, from $1$ to $I$'') plus the fake one; the second contains only the non-fake types, and ``the $\omega$ from the linear equation of state makes a reappearance''. This is ``more memorable than the original Friedmann equation''; cosmologists like to put all contributions, the curvature (which may be chosen to be absent, $\kappa=0$) included, at the same level in the first equation \ts{21}{1}{23:30}\ts{21}{1}{23:51}\ts{21}{1}{24:11}\ts{21}{1}{24:31}\ts{21}{1}{24:54}.

\section{Dominant matter at various epochs}\label{sec:c21-dominant}

Which matter type dominates in the early universe and which later? ``You do not need physics intuition, you can calculate'' \ts{21}{1}{24:54}\ts{21}{1}{25:16}\ts{21}{1}{25:37}. One knows $\rho_i\propto a^{-n(\omega_i)}$ \ts{21}{1}{25:58}, and the values of $n$:
\[
  n_\gamma=4\ \text{(radiation)},\quad n_m=3\ \text{(dust, ``matter'')},\quad n_\kappa=2,\quad n_\Lambda=0 .
\]
The $n=2$ is the one formally associated with the spatial curvature (a ``fluid'' with $\omega=-\tfrac13$: $n=3(1-\tfrac13)=2$; the lecturer first writes a wrong sign and corrects: ``that is minus one third, the sign comes from the $\Omega$ in a second''), and the $n=0$ with $\Lambda$ ($\omega=-1$). This is true for every matter type, the cosmological constant included, whereas some of the earlier formulas hold only for $\Lambda$ excluded (``see the tutorial today where you will derive this, a very short derivation'') \ts{21}{1}{26:18}\ts{21}{1}{26:39}\ts{21}{1}{27:01}\ts{21}{1}{27:21}\ts{21}{1}{27:42}\ts{21}{1}{28:05}\ts{21}{1}{28:26}.

\begin{theorem}[Relative scaling of the density parameters]\label{thm:c21-scaling}
With the definitions above, up to constant factors,
\[
  \Omega_\Lambda\;\propto\;a^{2}\,\Omega_\kappa\;\propto\;a^{3}\,\Omega_m\;\propto\;a^{4}\,\Omega_\gamma .
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
``If you plug in these $n$ and check how the density parameter has to go, it is very obvious.'' All $\Omega$ have the same factor $H^{-2}$, which drops out of ratios. The numerators are $\Lambda/3$ (constant, $a^0$), $-\kappa/a^2$, $\frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho_m\propto a^{-3}$, $\frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho_\gamma\propto a^{-4}$. Hence $\Omega_\Lambda/\Omega_\kappa\propto a^2$, $\Omega_\Lambda/\Omega_m\propto a^3$, $\Omega_\Lambda/\Omega_\gamma\propto a^4$, and in the same way for the other ratios (for instance $\Omega_m/\Omega_\gamma\propto a$).
\end{proof}
\ts{21}{1}{28:46}\ts{21}{1}{29:07}\ts{21}{1}{29:28}\ts{21}{1}{29:48}

Now an observation that tells a lot. An \textbf{expanding universe} is one where $H>0$ at all times: $a$ increases, so $t_1<t_2\iff a(t_1)<a(t_2)$ (``very simple''). Divide the relation $\Omega_m\propto a\,\Omega_\gamma$ (matter against radiation): as time goes on $a$ grows, so $\Omega_m/\Omega_\gamma$ grows: the types \emph{to the left in the chain} become ever more important. In the early universe, where the scale factor is small, radiation $\gamma$ dominates and the others ``may be there but are suppressed''; then matter takes over; then the spatial curvature (if it was chosen to be present; it may or may not dominate); and at very late times only the cosmological constant is the dominant type \ts{21}{1}{30:20}\ts{21}{1}{30:42}\ts{21}{1}{31:03}\ts{21}{1}{31:23}\ts{21}{1}{31:44}\ts{21}{1}{32:06}\ts{21}{1}{32:28}\ts{21}{1}{32:48}. ``So I did not have to speculate about the role of a cosmological constant in the early universe: there is none, it is highly suppressed. It would play a role in the early universe only if all the other matter types were not there: it is a political solution, if you eliminate all your competitors you stay dominant everywhere.'' This is a result of the theory, not a modelling assumption; one has better control over the epochs, and when a cosmologist says ``$\Lambda$ dominates at late times'' it is not mysterious, it is this chain of proportionalities \ts{21}{1}{33:08}\ts{21}{1}{33:28}\ts{21}{1}{33:49}\ts{21}{1}{34:09}.

\begin{coursenote}[Student questions on the epochs]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Are we at ``late time''?} Yes, ``we are at late time; if we were not, maybe we could not have observed this yet'': the other stuff might still be dominating \ts{21}{1}{34:45}\ts{21}{1}{35:06}.
  \item \emph{Is conversion of one type into another included?} No, not in this model; people look at it (nucleosynthesis, for which a deceleration period is needed, and so on); ``there are many results, all based on the model''; this model is simple-minded \ts{21}{1}{35:26}\ts{21}{1}{35:46}.
  \item \emph{Curvature is not caused by mass.} Right: ``we just assume we live in a sphere, a saddle or a flat space''. If the spatial universe is $S^3$ (closed, finite volume), then curvature does not play a big role early, and not a dominant role late, but somewhere \emph{in between}. When did the stars develop, structure formation, the cosmic microwave background imprinted, fluctuations: one tries to figure out what happened when, using more physics, and this ``first-order ordering principle'' is the zeroth-order approximation of a subject that needs quantum field theory, plasma physics and more \ts{21}{1}{36:06}\ts{21}{1}{36:27}\ts{21}{1}{36:47}\ts{21}{1}{37:07}\ts{21}{1}{37:29}.
  \item \emph{Does it mean matter has no effect today?} (Several questions.) The ratios only give proportions, not absolute values. Matter does evolve according to gravity (a galaxy cluster does its things), but ``I no longer have the resolution to see a galaxy cluster, I think of a fluid from very far away'' (like Boyle not thinking of atoms in the gas laws); matter has little impact on the evolution of the universe as a whole at our time. Analogy of the lecture: in a room, what contributes more to the curvature, your bodies or the oxygen? The bodies; in a room filled with dense oxygen it would be the oxygen: the dominance changes with the proportions \ts{21}{1}{37:53}\ts{21}{1}{38:13}\ts{21}{1}{38:34}\ts{21}{1}{38:55}\ts{21}{1}{39:16}\ts{21}{1}{39:36}\ts{21}{1}{39:56}\ts{21}{1}{40:17}\ts{21}{1}{40:38}\ts{21}{1}{40:59}.
  \item \emph{Why does radiation fade faster than dust?} It depends on statistical mechanics: if one makes space twice as big the density of radiation changes differently from that of massive bodies, the scale dependence is not the same: massive and massless particles have different phase spaces. ``This is in perfect accordance with statistical considerations'' \ts{21}{1}{41:20}\ts{21}{1}{41:40}.
\end{itemize}
\end{coursenote}

\section{Dark energy, and what $\Lambda$ is}\label{sec:c21-dark}

``The larger the universe grows, the less important matter becomes''; but what is the interpretation of $\Lambda$? Some people call it \textbf{dark energy}, to help the imagination (``I like to think of photons as little balls that do not interact, and of curvature, but this guy I cannot imagine''): it is an \emph{energy} density, and \emph{dark} because $\Lambda$ was added to the energy-momentum tensor and none of the other matter has the $\Lambda$ in its action (``where is $\Lambda$ in the Maxwell action in Jackson? It is not there''): light does not couple to it, so one cannot detect it through other matter (``I can detect your head by throwing a ball if it is reflected; if it does not interact, I have a problem detecting your head''). We see it only through its gravitational effect, via the Einstein curvature. ``That is the name, I try to avoid the terminology'' \ts{21}{1}{41:40}\ts{21}{1}{42:00}\ts{21}{1}{42:21}\ts{21}{1}{42:41}\ts{21}{1}{43:02}\ts{21}{1}{43:23}\ts{21}{1}{43:43}\ts{21}{1}{44:04}.

\begin{coursenote}[Student questions on $\Lambda$]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{If $\Lambda$ is a constant in the equation, is it perfectly homogeneously distributed?} Yes, ``it is like the ether'' (``that is polemics''). A follow-up on background independence: as Giulini explained in his evening talk [captions read ``Julian'', ?], one has to be very specific about what background independence means, since any matter put in is extra structure if one wishes; ``it depends on what you call a background, at this stage of technical definitions it is a rather ill-defined question'' \ts{21}{1}{44:24}\ts{21}{1}{44:45}\ts{21}{1}{45:05}\ts{21}{1}{45:25}.
  \item \emph{Can one see a gravitational effect of $\Lambda$ on small scales, e.g.\ near the sun?} In the model with only $\Lambda$ the evolution is $e^{Ht}$ and the universe blows up; light deflection sees it, since light follows null geodesics of the changing metric; but in a spatial foliation one sees nothing, since experiments use massive particles. ``In the model everything is uniform; in real life matter and radiation have texture, pockets with more radiation''; whether dark energy could also have pockets: some people say so, ``let me continue, I want to give answers that depend on an experimental result I am going to show'' \ts{21}{1}{45:46}\ts{21}{1}{46:08}\ts{21}{1}{46:28}\ts{21}{1}{46:49}\ts{21}{1}{47:09}.
\end{itemize}
\end{coursenote}

\section{A more realistic late universe: the $(\Omega_m,\Omega_\Lambda)$ plane}\label{sec:c21-plane}

``Let us look at a more realistic late universe; these are all toy theories and they can be slightly refined'': matter (dust), spatial curvature and a cosmological constant \ts{21}{1}{47:09}\ts{21}{1}{47:37}\ts{21}{1}{48:04}. Three density parameters, but the Friedmann equation \eqref{eq:c21-friedmann} says that they sum to one,
\[
  \Omega_\kappa=1-\Omega_m-\Omega_\Lambda,
\]
so only two are needed: the parameter space is the $(\Omega_m,\Omega_\Lambda)$ plane (the lecture draws $\Omega_\Lambda$ vertically, from $-1$ to $1$, and $\Omega_m$ horizontally, from $0$ to $2$). A point in it is a (sufficiently precise) observation: experiments extract $\Omega_m$ and $\Omega_\Lambda$ from the data \emph{on the basis of the model}, ``very important caveat''; then $\Omega_\kappa$ follows. Radiation could be added as a third axis: ``then draw a three-dimensional diagram''; but for the late universe $\Omega_\gamma$ is no longer interesting, ``we leave it out in the model, why would we bother'' \ts{21}{1}{48:24}\ts{21}{1}{48:44}\ts{21}{1}{49:06}\ts{21}{1}{49:26}\ts{21}{1}{49:47}\ts{21}{1}{50:07}\ts{21}{1}{50:28}\ts{21}{1}{50:49}\ts{21}{1}{51:10}\ts{21}{1}{51:30}\ts{21}{1}{51:50}\ts{21}{1}{52:11}.
One classifies the regions of the plane.

\paragraph{Spatial curvature.} $\kappa=0\iff\Omega_\kappa=0$ (since $\Omega_\kappa\propto\kappa$), so $0=1-\Omega_m-\Omega_\Lambda$, i.e.
\[
  \Omega_\Lambda=1-\Omega_m\qquad(\text{flat universe}),
\]
a straight line (``in a suitably curved blackboard plane''): the first slip (``$\Omega_m-1$'') is corrected on the board. ``These are all the observational data points that would give $\kappa=0$.'' On one side $\kappa>0$, on the other $\kappa<0$, found by the same argument with the other sign: since $\Omega_\kappa=-\kappa/(H^2a^2)$, $\kappa>0\iff\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda>1$ (above the line) and $\kappa<0\iff\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda<1$ (below it) [the direction is not audible in the captions, this is the result of the computation] \ts{21}{1}{52:34}\ts{21}{1}{52:55}\ts{21}{1}{53:16}\ts{21}{1}{53:36}\ts{21}{1}{53:57}\ts{21}{1}{54:22}\ts{21}{1}{54:45}.

\paragraph{Acceleration.} The sign of $H^{-2}\ddot a/a$ (the factors in front are positive, so only the sign matters) from \eqref{eq:c21-accel} with only $m$ ($\omega=0$) and $\Lambda$ ($\omega=-1$):
\[
  \frac{\ddot a}{aH^2}=-\frac12\bigl(1+0\bigr)\Omega_m-\frac12(1-3)\Omega_\Lambda=-\frac12\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda .
\]
So the \emph{zero-acceleration line} is $\Omega_\Lambda=\tfrac12\Omega_m$, ``starts here, goes to the middle, goes up here''; above it the expansion accelerates, below it decelerates (same check of signs) \ts{21}{1}{54:45}\ts{21}{1}{55:05}\ts{21}{1}{55:26}\ts{21}{1}{55:48}\ts{21}{1}{56:11}\ts{21}{1}{56:32}\ts{21}{1}{56:53}\ts{21}{1}{57:14}\ts{21}{1}{57:35}\ts{21}{1}{57:55}\ts{21}{1}{58:17}.

\section{What do the observations say?}\label{sec:c21-obs}

``This is all theory, these are possible universes we might have filled with this toy matter; what do the observations say?'' Observations from various sources (cosmologists believe independent ones: supernovae, galaxy surveys, redshifts and so on) indicate
\[
  \Omega_\Lambda\approx0.7,\qquad\Omega_m\approx0.3,\qquad\Omega_\kappa\approx0,
\]
the last because the point lies (almost) on the flat line, ``it is easy to jump here or there'': a flat universe, spatially infinitely extended, is supported to a big degree (the figure above). Hence $70\%$ of the energy density needs to go into the cosmological constant and $30\%$ comes from matter; ``in the Friedmann equation, $100\%$ is $0\%$ from curvature plus $70\%$ cosmological constant plus $30\%$ matter'' \ts{21}{1}{1:02:33}\ts{21}{1}{1:02:53}\ts{21}{1}{1:03:16}\ts{21}{1}{1:03:37}\ts{21}{1}{1:03:57}\ts{21}{1}{1:04:19}\ts{21}{1}{1:04:44}\ts{21}{1}{1:05:04}\ts{21}{1}{1:05:25}.

It is ``even worse'': the $30\%$ are split into $25\%+5\%$ (for other reasons), and only the $5\%$ comes from what we believe is the matter of the \emph{standard model of particle physics}: all the particles that one gets by shooting ``bananas onto oranges'' at accelerators and studying the products, quantum field theories of the electroweak and strong interactions and the zoo of particles. What we believe in elementary particle physics to be all the matter there is at the highest energies we can produce accounts for only $5\%$ of the matter and energy that one needs to explain the observations \ts{21}{1}{1:05:25}\ts{21}{1}{1:05:51}\ts{21}{1}{1:06:11}\ts{21}{1}{1:06:32}\ts{21}{1}{1:06:52}. (The figures are those of the lecture; current values were not checked.)

The remaining stuff is not of that type; the $25\%$ are called \textbf{dark matter} (like the cosmological constant, which is just one model for dark energy, it ``does not interact'' with our matter); ``it is dark for us because we do not have the right instruments'', being made of the other matter. ``It could be that dark matter interacts with dark matter, very likely it does, but we do not have instruments built of dark matter. \section{Why one may not insert the symmetry into the action}\label{sec:c21-palais}

What was meant (and should have been emphasised): given a field equation, Einstein gravity included, one should insert the symmetry assumptions on the objects the equation talks about (here on $g$) \emph{at the level of the equation}. Recall that a symmetry is something that takes a solution into a solution. The equations come from an action $S_{\mathrm{grav}}+S_{\mathrm{matter}}$ varied with respect to all fields; ``one could say: I implement the symmetry already here and then I need to vary only with respect to the scale factor, the only degree of freedom that survives'': this is \emph{usually illegal} \ts{21}{1}{1:11:43}\ts{21}{1}{1:12:03}\ts{21}{1}{1:12:24}.

The reason, by analogy with classical mechanics: a curve is a solution if any neighbouring curve, however slightly deviating, does not change the value of the action to first order (stationarity). If one puts the symmetry in from the beginning, one only compares with the competitors that satisfy the symmetry condition; ``if you compare to fewer competitors you can more easily be better'': one can get \emph{fake solutions} \ts{21}{1}{1:12:45}\ts{21}{1}{1:13:05}\ts{21}{1}{1:13:26}\ts{21}{1}{1:13:47}.

\begin{aside}[The principle of symmetric criticality]
There is a result, the \textbf{principle of symmetric criticality} (stationarity; tradition says ``criticality''), by Palais (as said in class; not checked), giving the conditions under which one \emph{may} insert the symmetry. Roughly: if the symmetry algebra is that of a \emph{compact} group, one can plug in and vary. How to see compactness: compute the \textbf{Killing metric}, built from the structure constants $f^a{}_{bc}$ of the algebra (``square them up-down-down, up-down-down, contract one with that one and this one with that one, the $a,b$ remain open''): $K_{ab}=f^c{}_{ad}f^d{}_{bc}$ [the contraction pattern as said, not checked in the board]; if it is negative definite the Lie group is compact (``I think that is right''). Example given: the rotation algebra has a negative definite Killing metric, so for rotational symmetry one can do it. ``A very rough statement, only sufficient, and the application requires sufficient care.'' Not said in class: for a semisimple Lie algebra the group is compact exactly if the Killing form is negative definite. In any case, ``that has nothing to do with cosmology specifically: you are not allowed to do that in general'' \ts{21}{1}{1:14:08}\ts{21}{1}{1:14:29}\ts{21}{1}{1:14:49}\ts{21}{1}{1:15:10}\ts{21}{1}{1:15:30}\ts{21}{1}{1:15:50}.
\end{aside}

\subsection*{Exercise 1: true or false}

``An important notion for cosmology is what a maximally symmetric space is, and by maximally symmetric we mean the maximal amount of Killing vector fields'' \ts{21}{2}{0:00}\ts{21}{2}{0:20}.

\paragraph{(A) Maximally symmetric spaces.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Homogeneous and isotropic three-manifolds are maximally symmetric: \textbf{true}. Homogeneity gives three Killing fields (translations) and isotropy three more (rotations; the captions read ``two''): six in total; the maximal number in dimension $d$ is $d(d+1)/2$ (the number of independent components of the metric), which for $d=3$ is $3\cdot4/2=6$ \ts{21}{2}{0:42}\ts{21}{2}{1:03}\ts{21}{2}{1:24}.
  \item A five-dimensional manifold can have at most $10$ linearly independent Killing fields: \textbf{false}, by the same formula the maximum is $5\cdot6/2=15$ \ts{21}{2}{1:44}.
  \item The Riemann curvature on a maximally symmetric manifold contains exactly one derivative of the metric: \textbf{false}, it contains no derivative of the metric [as said: ``we saw that it contains no derivative of the metric'': for a maximally symmetric space $R_{abcd}$ is a constant times a combination of metric products, cf.\ \eqref{eq:c16-K} for two dimensions] \ts{21}{2}{1:44}\ts{21}{2}{2:05}.
  \item A maximally symmetric three-manifold cannot be flat: \textbf{false}, e.g.\ the spatial part of FLRW with $\kappa=0$ \ts{21}{2}{2:05}\ts{21}{2}{2:25}.
  \item A maximally symmetric space has constant sectional curvature: \textbf{true}, the $\kappa=-1,0,1$ introduced in the lecture \ts{21}{2}{2:25}\ts{21}{2}{2:49}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{(B) FLRW cosmology.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A perfect fluid has constant pressure and density over the whole spacetime: \textbf{false}, $P$ is a function of $t$ and so is $\rho$ \ts{21}{2}{2:49}\ts{21}{2}{3:10}.
  \item Homogeneous and isotropic dust and radiation can be modelled as perfect fluids: \textbf{true}, with the equation of state $P=\omega\rho$: $\omega=\tfrac13$ is radiation and $\omega=0$ dust \ts{21}{2}{3:10}\ts{21}{2}{3:32}.
  \item The Friedmann equations are the Einstein equations for a spherically symmetric vacuum: \textbf{false}; ``it is not a vacuum, matter is on the right-hand side, and it is not necessarily spherically symmetric, only in the spatial part'' \ts{21}{2}{3:32}\ts{21}{2}{3:53}.
  \item The FLRW metric with $\kappa=1$ is that of a hyperbolic three-manifold: \textbf{false}, with a minus it would be right; remember the factor $1/(1-\kappa r^2)$: $\kappa=1$ produces a spherical three-manifold \ts{21}{2}{4:13}\ts{21}{2}{4:34}.
  \item All matter densities $\rho(t)$ are proportional to $t^{-2}$: \textbf{true}, ``we calculated that in the lectures'' (for the linear equations of state with $\omega\neq-1$, see \cref{sec:c21-recall}; not said in the tutorial: the cosmological constant is the exception) \ts{21}{2}{4:34}\ts{21}{2}{4:56}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Exercise 2: Killing's equation}

First an interpretation of ``$K$ is a Killing vector field'' in terms of its flow \ts{21}{2}{4:56}\ts{21}{2}{5:17}. The condition is $\mathcal L_Kg=0$, where
\[
  \mathcal L_Kg=\lim_{\lambda\to0}\frac{(h^K_\lambda)^*g-g}{\lambda}
\]
is a differential quotient (a more general one than for functions) \ts{21}{2}{5:17}\ts{21}{2}{5:39}\ts{21}{2}{6:00}. The \textbf{flow} $h^K$ of $K$ is the map $\mathbb R\times M\to M$, $(\lambda,p)\mapsto h^K_\lambda(p)=\gamma_p(\lambda)$, where $\gamma_p$ is the curve whose tangent vector at the point $p$ is $K_p$ (an integral curve); the one-parameter family of integral curves through all points is the flow; $\lambda$ varies along the curve and $p$ fixes the starting point \ts{21}{2}{6:21}\ts{21}{2}{6:42}\ts{21}{2}{7:02}\ts{21}{2}{7:22}\ts{21}{2}{7:43}\ts{21}{2}{8:04}\ts{21}{2}{8:25}. One uses this map to \emph{pull back} the metric at the point $h^K_\lambda(p)$ to the point $p$ and compares it with the metric there: the Killing condition says that they do not differ, ``the metric here and there is the same'' \ts{21}{2}{8:45}\ts{21}{2}{9:06}\ts{21}{2}{9:28}.

\begin{proposition}[Killing's equation]\label{prop:c21-killing}
A vector field $K$ is a Killing vector field if and only if
\[
  \nabla_aK_b+\nabla_bK_a=0 .
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The only thing known is $\mathcal L_Kg=0$; one writes it in coordinates and looks for the equal signs that lead to the covariant derivatives \ts{21}{2}{9:28}\ts{21}{2}{9:48}\ts{21}{2}{10:10}. The Lie derivative of a $(0,2)$ tensor has three components: the derivative of the components along $K$ and one term for each index,
\[
  (\mathcal L_Kg)_{ab}=K^c\partial_cg_{ab}+(\partial_aK^c)\,g_{cb}+(\partial_bK^c)\,g_{ac}
\]
(the tutor writes ``$K^c\,\partial_c g_{ab}$'', then in the second term replaces the index and shifts it, correcting a slip on the board) \ts{21}{2}{10:10}\ts{21}{2}{10:31}\ts{21}{2}{10:53}\ts{21}{2}{11:13}\ts{21}{2}{11:35}.
To produce Christoffel symbols one recalls
\[
  \Gamma^a{}_{bc}=\tfrac12g^{ad}\bigl(g_{bd,c}+g_{cd,b}-g_{bc,d}\bigr)
\]
\ts{21}{2}{11:35}\ts{21}{2}{11:56}\ts{21}{2}{12:18}\ts{21}{2}{12:38}
(the tutor first writes a wrong index, ``the $a$ cannot be down here'', and corrects). The second term is $(\partial_aK^c)g_{cb}$: ``pull the metric in'': by the product rule $(\partial_aK^c)g_{cb}=\partial_a(K_b)-K^c\partial_ag_{cb}$, where $K_b=g_{bc}K^c$; ``we make a mistake and compensate it'': and likewise $(\partial_bK^c)g_{ac}=\partial_bK_a-K^c\partial_bg_{ac}$. Therefore
\[
  (\mathcal L_Kg)_{ab}=\partial_aK_b+\partial_bK_a+K^c\bigl(\partial_cg_{ab}-\partial_ag_{cb}-\partial_bg_{ac}\bigr).
\]
\ts{21}{2}{12:59}\ts{21}{2}{13:20}\ts{21}{2}{13:40}\ts{21}{2}{14:01}\ts{21}{2}{14:23}\ts{21}{2}{14:48}\ts{21}{2}{15:09}\ts{21}{2}{15:30}\ts{21}{2}{15:50}
The bracket is minus the combination in $\Gamma$: using $K^c=g^{cd}K_d$ and the symmetry of the metric in its indices, $K^c(g_{ab,c}-g_{cb,a}-g_{ac,b})=-2\,K_d\,\Gamma^d{}_{ab}$ (the two cancelled sums give twice the symbol: ``here was a factor $\tfrac12$ and here we put the two''; the index structure: $d$ is summed, $ab$ stay) \ts{21}{2}{16:14}\ts{21}{2}{16:34}\ts{21}{2}{16:55}\ts{21}{2}{17:16}\ts{21}{2}{17:37}\ts{21}{2}{17:57}. Distributing the two Christoffel symbols between the two terms,
\[
  (\mathcal L_Kg)_{ab}=\bigl(\partial_aK_b-\Gamma^d{}_{ab}K_d\bigr)+\bigl(\partial_bK_a-\Gamma^d{}_{ba}K_d\bigr)=\nabla_aK_b+\nabla_bK_a,
\]
with the torsion-free symmetry $\Gamma^d{}_{ab}=\Gamma^d{}_{ba}$. Since the start was $\mathcal L_Kg=0$, one has derived Killing's equation (the converse is the same chain read backwards) \ts{21}{2}{17:57}\ts{21}{2}{18:18}\ts{21}{2}{18:39}\ts{21}{2}{19:00}.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Exercise 3: the age of the universe}

One wants to estimate the age of the universe with a specific matter model: first the conservation equation, then the solution of the Friedmann equation, then the age, then the acceleration \ts{21}{2}{19:00}\ts{21}{2}{19:21}\ts{21}{2}{19:42}.

\paragraph{(a) The conservation equation.} The energy-momentum tensor of a perfect fluid is $T^{ab}=(\rho+P)u^au^b+Pg^{ab}$ [the form ``you might know from the lectures'': $u^a$ a timelike vector, $u^a=(1,0,0,0)$ in the comoving coordinates chosen] \ts{21}{2}{19:42}\ts{21}{2}{20:03}. The condition is the energy-momentum conservation, which follows from the twice contracted differential Bianchi identity of last time (chapter~\ref{ch:c15}) together with the Einstein equation: $\nabla_aT^{ab}=0$; it is evaluated for $b=0$ ($\nabla_aG^{ab}=0$ and the contraction with $u$ ``changes nothing but makes it easier'') \ts{21}{2}{20:03}\ts{21}{2}{20:24}\ts{21}{2}{20:44}\ts{21}{2}{21:05}.
\begin{proposition}[The conservation equation]\label{prop:c21-conservation}
In FLRW cosmology with a perfect fluid, $\nabla_aT^{a0}=0$ is equivalent to
\[
  \dot\rho=-3\,\frac{\dot a}{a}\,\bigl(\rho+P\bigr).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $\nabla_aT^{a0}=\partial_aT^{a0}+\Gamma^a{}_{an}T^{n0}+\Gamma^0{}_{an}T^{an}=0$ (the sign convention of the board is irrelevant, ``the other side is zero'') \ts{21}{2}{21:05}\ts{21}{2}{21:26}\ts{21}{2}{21:48}\ts{21}{2}{22:08}\ts{21}{2}{22:29}. The Christoffel symbols are given (``as we showed in the lecture''): $\Gamma^a{}_{a0}=\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\alpha0}=3\,\dot a/a$ (the sum over $a=0,\dots,3$ reduces to the three spatial $\alpha$, and $\delta^\alpha{}_\alpha=3$), $\Gamma^0{}_{\alpha\beta}=a\dot a\,\gamma_{\alpha\beta}$ with $g_{\alpha\beta}=a^2\gamma_{\alpha\beta}$ (the spatial part, multiplied by $a^2$) \ts{21}{2}{22:29}\ts{21}{2}{22:51}\ts{21}{2}{23:11}\ts{21}{2}{23:35}. (The tutor first forgets the zero index and corrects.) In comoving coordinates the energy-momentum tensor has no mixed time-space entries, so $T^{00}=\rho$, $T^{0\alpha}=0$, $T^{\alpha\beta}=Pg^{\alpha\beta}=\frac P{a^2}\gamma^{\alpha\beta}$ \ts{21}{2}{23:55}\ts{21}{2}{24:16}. Then:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\partial_aT^{a0}=\dot\rho$ (only $a=0$ contributes; the spatial derivative term is zero) \ts{21}{2}{24:16}\ts{21}{2}{24:36};
  \item $\Gamma^a{}_{an}T^{n0}=\Gamma^a{}_{a0}\rho=3\frac{\dot a}a\rho$ \ts{21}{2}{24:36}\ts{21}{2}{24:57};
  \item $\Gamma^0{}_{an}T^{an}=\Gamma^0{}_{\alpha\beta}T^{\alpha\beta}=a\dot a\,\gamma_{\alpha\beta}\,\frac P{a^2}\gamma^{\alpha\beta}=3\frac{\dot a}aP$, using $\gamma_{\alpha\beta}\gamma^{\alpha\beta}=3$ \ts{21}{2}{24:57}\ts{21}{2}{25:17}\ts{21}{2}{25:38}\ts{21}{2}{25:59}\ts{21}{2}{26:20}\ts{21}{2}{26:41}\ts{21}{2}{27:03}.
\end{itemize}
Adding up (``the $a^2$ cancel, the dimension $3$ comes out'': the tutor forgets the $P$ and adds it back) $\dot\rho+3\frac{\dot a}a(\rho+P)=0$ \ts{21}{2}{27:24}\ts{21}{2}{27:45}.
\end{proof}
Summary of the tutor: one exploits that the covariant divergence of the energy-momentum tensor vanishes in the time direction (it follows from the contracted Bianchi identity), plays with the indices and looks at the non-vanishing components \ts{21}{2}{28:06}\ts{21}{2}{28:27}.

\paragraph{(b) The first-order equation for $a$.} For $P=\omega\rho$, solve the conservation equation for $\rho$ and use the Friedmann equation with $\kappa=0$ (no curvature term) to get an \emph{autonomous} differential equation for $a$ (only $a$ and its derivatives, constants otherwise) \ts{21}{2}{28:27}\ts{21}{2}{28:47}\ts{21}{2}{29:08}.   \frac{\dot\rho}{\rho}=-3(1+\omega)\frac{\dot a}a\;\Longrightarrow\;\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\ln\rho=-3(1+\omega)\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\ln a,
\]
integrate: $\ln\rho=-3(1+\omega)\ln a+\text{const}$, hence

  \rho=\tilde C\,a^{-3(1+\omega)}
\]
(the constant $\tilde C$ is ``suppressed'' in the video, but it is kept in the lines below) \ts{21}{2}{29:08}\ts{21}{2}{29:28}\ts{21}{2}{29:49}\ts{21}{2}{30:13}\ts{21}{2}{30:33}\ts{21}{2}{30:54}\ts{21}{2}{31:14}. Plugged into the Friedmann equation with $\kappa=0$ and $\Lambda=0$:

\end{equation}

\paragraph{(c) The solution.} The ansatz is $a(t)=C\,t^{\alpha}$. (``the $C$ cancels'') \ts{21}{2}{35:28}\ts{21}{2}{35:48}\ts{21}{2}{36:10}\ts{21}{2}{36:31}\ts{21}{2}{36:52}\ts{21}{2}{37:13}. Today, $H_0=\alpha/t_0$, so the age of the universe is
\[
  t_0=\frac{\alpha}{H_0}=\frac{2}{3(1+\omega)H_0}.
\]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Dust} ($\omega=0$): $t_0=\dfrac{2}{3H_0}$; with the Hubble constant given on the sheet (the numbers read in the video correspond to $1/H_0\approx13\cdot10^9$ years, [the value of $H_0$ on the sheet was not read from the sheet, ?]) this gives about $\tfrac23\cdot13\cdot10^9\approx8.7\cdot10^9$ years \ts{21}{2}{37:13}\ts{21}{2}{37:34}\ts{21}{2}{37:55}\ts{21}{2}{38:17}.
  \item \emph{Radiation} ($\omega=\tfrac13$): $t_0=\dfrac{1}{2H_0}\approx6.5\cdot10^9$ years \ts{21}{2}{38:17}\ts{21}{2}{38:37}.
\end{itemize}
``For different models one gets different results for the age of the universe; of course the whole thing is much more complicated, but it is nice to have such an easy model and get such good results'' \ts{21}{2}{38:37}\ts{21}{2}{38:57}\ts{21}{2}{39:21}.

\paragraph{(e) Acceleration.} Is the expansion accelerating? One needs $\ddot a$:
\[
  \ddot a=\alpha(\alpha-1)\,C\,t^{\alpha-2}.
\]
Since $C>0$ and $t>0$ the sign is that of $\alpha(\alpha-1)$ (``here you see why I wanted $C>0$'') \ts{21}{2}{39:21}\ts{21}{2}{39:41}\ts{21}{2}{40:02}\ts{21}{2}{40:23}. Zero acceleration: $\alpha=1$, i.e.\ $\tfrac23\frac1{1+\omega}=1$, $1+\omega=\tfrac23$, $\omega=-\tfrac13$. For dust ($\omega=0$, $\alpha=\tfrac23$): $\tfrac23\bigl(\tfrac23-1\bigr)<0$, the expansion \emph{decelerates}; on the other side of $\omega=-\tfrac13$ it accelerates \ts{21}{2}{40:44}\ts{21}{2}{41:08}\ts{21}{2}{41:29}\ts{21}{2}{41:51}\ts{21}{2}{42:11}\ts{21}{2}{42:31}. The tutor concludes: it has been measured that the expansion is accelerating, which means that $\omega$ in this simple model must be negative; one cannot associate normal matter to that, ``so this would maybe be some indicator for dark matter or dark energy, but of course only in this very simple model'' \ts{21}{2}{42:31}\ts{21}{2}{42:53}\ts{21}{2}{43:15}.
\begin{coursenote}[Corrections to the tutorial]
(1) In the tutorial $\omega>-\tfrac13$ is written ``larger than $\tfrac13$'' and corrected on the spot to ``minus one third''. (2) Not every negative $\omega$ gives acceleration: one needs $\omega<-\tfrac13$ (and, for the power law above, $\omega>-1$, since for $\omega<-1$ the exponent $\alpha$ is negative and the solution with $C>0$ is a shrinking universe; $\omega=-1$ is the exponential de Sitter case excluded in (c)); this is the region ``above the $\ddot a=0$ line'' of \cref{fig:c21-plane} in the language of the lecture (not said in the tutorial). (3) The identification with ``dark matter'' is loose: the lecture calls the cosmological constant (a dark \emph{energy}) the type with $\omega=-1$ and dark matter the $25\%$ of the pressureless matter of \cref{sec:c21-obs} [not said in the tutorial].
\end{coursenote}

\section*{Key concepts}
\begin{keyconcepts}
\textbf{Retrieval questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define the Hubble function and the density parameters $\Omega_i$, $\Omega_\kappa$, $\Omega_\Lambda$. Which ones are ``fake''?
  \item Write the Friedmann and the acceleration equation in density parameters.
  \item Why does $\Lambda$ not play a role in the early universe? Prove the scaling $\Omega_\Lambda\propto a^2\Omega_\kappa\propto a^3\Omega_m\propto a^4\Omega_\gamma$ and read off the order of dominance.
  \item In the $(\Omega_m,\Omega_\Lambda)$ plane, find the flat line and the zero-acceleration line; on which side is $\kappa>0$, on which is the expansion accelerating?
  \item What do the observations say about $\Omega_m$, $\Omega_\Lambda$, $\Omega_\kappa$, and what is the caveat?
  \item Why is it usually illegal to insert a symmetry ansatz into the action?
  \item Derive Killing's equation from $\mathcal L_Kg=0$; derive $\dot\rho=-3H(\rho+P)$, the power law $a\propto t^{2/(3(1+\omega))}$ and the age $t_0=2/(3(1+\omega)H_0)$.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Concepts.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Hubble function, density parameters, fake density parameters (\cref{def:c21-hubble}, \cref{def:c21-omega}, \cref{def:c21-fake}).
  \item Friedmann and acceleration equations in density parameters (\cref{thm:c21-friedmann}); relative scaling and dominance (\cref{thm:c21-scaling}).
  \item The $(\Omega_m,\Omega_\Lambda)$ plane (\cref{fig:c21-plane}), observations and dark matter/energy (\cref{sec:c21-obs}).
  \item Principle of symmetric criticality (\cref{sec:c21-palais}).
  \item Killing's equation (\cref{prop:c21-killing}); conservation equation and age of the universe (\cref{prop:c21-conservation}, \cref{sec:c21-t15}).
\end{itemize}
\end{keyconcepts}
