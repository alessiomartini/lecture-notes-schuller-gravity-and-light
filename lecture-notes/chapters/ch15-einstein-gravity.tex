\section{From Newton to a guess for the field equation}\label{sec:c15-guess}

``We now have all the important technology collected in order to talk about Einstein gravity'' \ts{15}{1}{0:00}. Recall that in Newtonian spacetime (lecture 9, chapter~\ref{ch:c09}) one could \emph{reformulate}, ``just a reformulation, it is not new physics'', Poisson's law for the gravitational potential $\Phi$ with matter density $\rho$ (the captions read ``d'Alembertian'', but it is the Laplacian of $\Phi$ that is $4\pi G_{\mathrm N}\rho$ [?]) as a statement about the curvature of the Newtonian connection: the $00$ component of its Ricci tensor,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c15-newton}
  R_{00}=4\pi G_{\mathrm N}\,\rho,
\end{equation}
where $G_{\mathrm N}$ is the Newtonian gravitational constant, the one measured in the Cavendish experiment with the two big balls (Schuller first forgets the $G_{\mathrm N}$ and corrects himself) \ts{15}{1}{0:20}\ts{15}{1}{0:44}\ts{15}{1}{1:04}\ts{15}{1}{1:29}\ts{15}{1}{1:50}\ts{15}{1}{2:11}.

Relations of this form prompted Einstein. ``Of course Einstein thought about this in many different ways, so it is difficult to say what exactly prompted him to do what in the course of the development of the theory''; roughly, it prompted him to postulate, in the years before 1915 (``I am not exactly sure when he wrote this down, maybe 1912 or so''; as said in class, not checked), that the relativistic field equations for the Lorentzian metric $g$ of relativistic spacetime (chapter~\ref{ch:c13}) ought to be
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c15-guess}
  R_{ab}=8\pi G_{\mathrm N}\,T_{ab}.
\end{equation}
The $8$ ``has to do with the conventions for the factors''. In \eqref{eq:c15-newton} the $00$ component is special, $\rho$ being (up to the identification with the energy density) the $00$ component of the energy-momentum tensor with respect to some observer; but this split of $00$ has to do with the \emph{absolute time} of Newtonian spacetime, ``and we do not want that direction to be special'', so the equation ought to hold for all components \ts{15}{1}{2:31}\ts{15}{1}{2:53}\ts{15}{1}{3:14}\ts{15}{1}{3:35}\ts{15}{1}{3:55}.
\begin{coursenote}[Why $8\pi$ and not $4\pi$]
(Not said in class.) The factor is consistent with \eqref{eq:c15-newton}: with $T_{00}=\rho$ and the trace-reversed form derived below, \eqref{eq:c15-trace}, $R_{00}=8\pi G_{\mathrm N}\bigl(T_{00}-\tfrac12g_{00}T\bigr)=8\pi G_{\mathrm N}\bigl(\rho-\tfrac12\rho\bigr)=4\pi G_{\mathrm N}\rho$ for dust at rest in a frame with $g_{00}=1$ and all other components of $T$ negligible.
\end{coursenote}
\ts{15}{1}{3:55}
\begin{coursenote}[Chart notation]
Schuller uses indices all the time, ``so I seem to be in a chart all the time''. This is fine: ``you can also look at this as not in a chart at all, that would be Penrose's abstract index notation: a quick way of writing what you want to say''. One takes the view of being in some chart, but the statement is true in all charts, ``so who cares'': it is much quicker than writing everything chart independent, and what is written is chart independent, as one could check explicitly \ts{15}{1}{4:18}\ts{15}{1}{4:38}\ts{15}{1}{4:59}.
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{The problem with the guess.} Take the divergence of \eqref{eq:c15-guess} over the first index, with indices raised. On the right-hand side the meta-theory of chapter~\ref{ch:c14} gives
\[
  \nabla_aT^{ab}=0,
\]
because the matter theory has been formulated from an action that could be formulated without the choice of coordinates, which yields this kind of conservation law (as said in class, via Noether's theorem; see also the remark after the field equation below) \ts{15}{1}{5:20}\ts{15}{1}{5:42}. On the left-hand side one has a different situation: the divergence $\nabla_aR^{ab}$ of the Ricci tensor (``it really is Ricci, not Riemann'') is \emph{not} zero generically; ``in certain situations it might be zero, but generically it is not''. That is not good for an equation: taking the divergence on both sides one would reach a contradiction. Einstein tried to argue this problem away (``gravity is different'': Schuller's paraphrase of the argument ``you all do not understand''), ``but nevertheless it is wrong, the equations cannot be upheld, so those are not the field equations'' \ts{15}{1}{6:03}\ts{15}{1}{6:24}\ts{15}{1}{6:44}\ts{15}{1}{7:08}.
\begin{coursenote}[Why the divergence of the Ricci tensor is not zero]
(Not said in class.) The contracted Bianchi identity, which the lecture quotes only later (see below), says $\nabla_aR^{ab}=\tfrac12\nabla^bR$, so the divergence of $R_{ab}$ vanishes only where the Ricci scalar is constant. Taking the trace of \eqref{eq:c15-guess} gives $R=8\pi G_{\mathrm N}T$, so the guess would force $\nabla^bT=0$: the trace of the energy-momentum tensor constant, which is false for general matter.
\end{coursenote}

\section{Hilbert's action}\label{sec:c15-hilbert}

The solution came from Einstein and, at the same time, from \textbf{Hilbert} (``one of the most famous mathematicians of all time; he was actually a specialist in variational principles''). He recognised that the problem arises because the \emph{right-hand side} comes from an action, and thought: why not derive the left-hand side also from an action? So Hilbert suggested to find the appropriate left-hand side of the gravitational field equations by starting from a variational principle \ts{15}{1}{7:28}\ts{15}{1}{7:50}\ts{15}{1}{8:14}.

Why not write an action for the metric $g$: it is a field theory, so the integral is over the entire manifold. What is the simplest function one can make of the metric? Contracting it with itself gives the dimension, ``a very boring function''. From Riemannian geometry one knows that from the metric and its \emph{first} derivatives one cannot build any tensor (in normal coordinates the first derivatives vanish); from the \emph{second} derivatives one can build the Ricci scalar $R=g^{ab}R_{ab}$, ``the simplest function''. A function integrated over a manifold needs a density to make it work (chapter~\ref{ch:c12}); so, by the ``rather obscure principle of simplicity'', one arrives at:

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the Einstein--Hilbert action]\label{def:c15-eh}
For a Lorentzian metric $g$ on the manifold $M$, the \textbf{Einstein--Hilbert action} is
\[
  S_{\mathrm{Hilbert}}[g]=\int_M\mathrm d^4x\,\sqrt{-\det g}\;g^{ab}R_{ab},
\]
the integral of the Ricci scalar $R=g^{ab}R_{ab}$ with the density $\sqrt{-\det g}$ (the minus sign because of the Lorentzian signature, as in the Maxwell action of chapter~\ref{ch:c14}).
\end{definition}
\ts{15}{1}{8:35}\ts{15}{1}{8:56}\ts{15}{1}{9:17}\ts{15}{1}{9:41}
\begin{coursenote}[On simplicity and beauty]
One can talk about the beauty of equations, their simplicity, their economy, ``all of these are aesthetic arguments that have nothing to do with physics: if physics followed aesthetics, planets would run on circles and not on ellipses, because the circle is more perfect''. So this is obviously not a good guiding principle. On the other hand a mathematician may say: ``these physicists, they just write down equations like that, and they get inconsistencies''; let us see what one gets starting with the simplest one, maybe it gives insight into how the left-hand side must look. Many people have since thought about how to justify the Einstein equations further, and Schuller will probably talk about this at the end of the course if time permits: this action is the \emph{unique} one one can write down if one wants Maxwell theory to be predictive and quantizable on it (the later constructive-gravity chapters of this book, \cref{ch:c29}). ``Anything else is interpreting something into it which is not really in there'' \ts{15}{1}{10:02}\ts{15}{1}{10:23}\ts{15}{1}{10:44}\ts{15}{1}{11:06}.
\end{coursenote}

The aim: vary $S_{\mathrm{Hilbert}}$ with respect to the metric $g_{ab}$. One can show abstractly that varying such a scalar function with respect to a tensor results in some tensor, here with indices up. If there is nothing else, it is $G^{ab}=0$ since no matter action has been added; the question is \emph{what is this tensor $G^{ab}$} that comes out. ``There is only one way to find out: the variation of $S_{\mathrm{Hilbert}}$, we do it'' \ts{15}{1}{11:28}\ts{15}{1}{11:49}\ts{15}{1}{12:09}. (Schuller first writes the result as $G^{ab}=0$ and then corrects himself: better to say that the variation results in some tensor $G^{ab}$; if one adds $S_{\mathrm{matter}}$, the variation of the matter part gives $T^{ab}$ on the right and one has the field equations; with nothing added, $G^{ab}=0$ are the vacuum field equations \ts{15}{1}{14:12}.)

\paragraph{How to vary.} The variation leads to Euler--Lagrange equations; for the unspecified Lagrangian these would be very general. It is quicker to vary directly than to write down the Euler--Lagrange equations: for point particles variation means deforming curves, for fields deforming fields, but there is a quick way by symbolic calculus (``you probably saw all of this before, I just do it again in this quick way because it is really very useful; for a proper mathematical foundation look at any book that does that properly''): at some point one has to say that \emph{variation behaves like differentiation}, which it actually does; in particular it obeys the product rule, ``it varies the first factor and keeps the others, plus it keeps the first and varies the second, and so on'' (``have you seen stuff like this before in classical mechanics? Probably yes: here it is done for fields but it works very similarly''). It is understood that the variation is with respect to the metric, and one requires that it vanish; that gives the field equations \ts{15}{1}{12:29}\ts{15}{1}{12:50}\ts{15}{1}{13:10}\ts{15}{1}{14:34}\ts{15}{1}{14:55}\ts{15}{1}{15:15}\ts{15}{1}{15:35}.
\begin{coursenote}[Why the variation gives the conservation law]
On the \emph{matter} side Schuller states that because the action can be formulated chart free (diffeomorphism invariant), a Noether theorem implies $\nabla_aT^{ab}=0$, ``that is the point, this can really be shown'' (he does not show it) \ts{15}{1}{14:34}\ts{15}{1}{14:55}.
\end{coursenote}

The variation of the integrand is a product of three factors, $\sqrt{-\det g}$, $g^{ab}$ and $R_{ab}$ (which depends on $g$ through its first and second derivatives), so one has three little problems: the variation of $\sqrt{-\det g}$ (term~1), the variation of the \emph{inverse} metric rather than the metric (term~2), the variation of the Ricci tensor (term~3). ``Let us do this one by one'' \ts{15}{1}{15:35}\ts{15}{1}{15:56}.

\begin{lemma}[Variation of the density]\label{lem:c15-det}
$\delta\sqrt{-\det g}=\tfrac12\sqrt{-\det g}\;g^{mn}\,\delta g_{mn}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
It is essentially a derivative: first the outer derivative of the square root, then the derivative of the determinant, which is the determinant itself times the inverse of the matrix of which one takes the determinant:
\[
  \delta\det g=\det g\;g^{mn}\,\delta g_{mn}.
\]
One way to see this: $\det g=\exp\operatorname{tr}\ln g$, so varying gives $\det g$ times $\operatorname{tr}(g^{-1}\delta g)$. Then $\delta\sqrt{-\det g}=\frac{-\delta\det g}{2\sqrt{-\det g}}=\frac{-\det g\;g^{mn}\delta g_{mn}}{2\sqrt{-\det g}}=\tfrac12\sqrt{-\det g}\,g^{mn}\delta g_{mn}$, since $-\det g=\bigl(\sqrt{-\det g}\bigr)^2$ \ts{15}{1}{15:56}\ts{15}{1}{16:17}\ts{15}{1}{16:39}\ts{15}{1}{17:01}\ts{15}{1}{17:21}.
\end{proof}
\begin{coursenote}[Student question: why is $\delta\det g=\det g\,g^{mn}\delta g_{mn}$?]
Via $\exp\operatorname{tr}\ln$: varying the exponential reproduces the determinant, then one has the derivative of the inner function, ``the derivative of $\ln$ is one over, that is the inverse''. Written more precisely, ``the cleanest proof is via the expansion formula for the determinant'' (Laplace expansion along a line with the minors): one crosses out the row and column and arrives in a one-page calculation at this formula. ``This is the quick way, I do not want to go into the detail here'' \ts{15}{1}{30:47}\ts{15}{1}{31:08}\ts{15}{1}{31:31}\ts{15}{1}{31:52}\ts{15}{1}{32:13}\ts{15}{1}{32:33}.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{lemma}[Variation of the inverse metric]\label{lem:c15-inv}
$\delta g^{ab}=-g^{am}g^{bn}\,\delta g_{mn}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
From $g^{ab}g_{bc}=\delta^a{}_c$, vary both sides with the product rule: $\delta g^{ab}\,g_{bc}+g^{ab}\,\delta g_{bc}=0$. Isolate $\delta g^{ab}$ by pulling the $g$ to the other side (multiply by $g^{cp}$): $\delta g^{ap}=-g^{ab}g^{cp}\,\delta g_{bc}$; renaming indices, ``to make the result look more memorable'', gives the claim \ts{15}{1}{17:42}\ts{15}{1}{18:03}\ts{15}{1}{18:23}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Variation of the Ricci tensor]\label{lem:c15-ricci}
At the point where one computes, in normal coordinates,
\[
  \delta R_{ab}=\nabla_m\,\delta\Gamma^m{}_{ab}-\nabla_b\,\delta\Gamma^m{}_{am},
\]
where $\delta\Gamma^m{}_{ab}$ is a $(1,2)$ tensor field, and consequently
\[
  \sqrt{-\det g}\,g^{ab}\,\delta R_{ab}=\sqrt{-\det g}\,\bigl(A^m{}_{;m}-B^b{}_{;b}\bigr)=\partial_m\bigl(\sqrt{-\det g}\,A^m\bigr)-\partial_b\bigl(\sqrt{-\det g}\,B^b\bigr),
\]
with the vector fields $A^m:=g^{ab}\delta\Gamma^m{}_{ab}$ and $B^b:=g^{ab}\delta\Gamma^m{}_{am}$.
\end{lemma}
\ts{15}{1}{18:44}
\begin{proof}
This is more tricky, but doable with the technology of the course. One considers the variation at a point and goes to normal coordinates there. The Ricci tensor is, in short form with a comma for the partial derivative,
\[
  R_{ab}=\Gamma^m{}_{ab,m}-\Gamma^m{}_{am,b}+\Gamma\Gamma-\Gamma\Gamma\quad\text{(with certain contractions)},
\]
and in normal coordinates the $\Gamma\Gamma$ terms vanish \emph{at the point}: only the derivatives remain (they are evaluated at the point, the next point could be different). Now take the variation of the two derivative terms. The variation and the partial derivative commute (``you have to formulate the variation more properly, then you see it does commute'': a variation means choosing two different $\Gamma$'s, i.e.\ two connections). Even if one did not use normal coordinates, the $\Gamma\Gamma$ terms would give terms like $\delta\Gamma\cdot\Gamma+\Gamma\cdot\delta\Gamma$, each with one $\Gamma$ left, which vanishes at the point. Hence at the point
\[
  \delta R_{ab}=\partial_m\,\delta\Gamma^m{}_{ab}-\partial_b\,\delta\Gamma^m{}_{am}.
\]
The important observation: $\delta\Gamma$ is the \emph{difference of two connections} (take one $\Gamma$ and compare it with another). The difference of the connection coefficients $\Gamma^i{}_{jk}$ and $\tilde\Gamma^i{}_{jk}$ of two different connections, in the same chart, are the components of a $(1,2)$ \emph{tensor}: under a change of coordinates $x\to y$ each of the two picks up an ``ugly term'' $\frac{\partial y^i}{\partial x^m}\frac{\partial^2x^m}{\partial y^j\partial y^k}$ in addition to the tensorial transformation, and this extra term does not depend on the connection at all, only on the change of coordinate system, so in the difference the extra terms cancel (see the student question below). ``Two connections differ by a tensor''. Therefore $\delta\Gamma^m{}_{ab}$ is a tensor, and one is allowed to take its covariant derivative; in normal coordinates the covariant derivative and the partial derivative differ by $\Gamma$ terms, which vanish there. So at the point $\partial_m\,\delta\Gamma^m{}_{ab}=\nabla_m\,\delta\Gamma^m{}_{ab}$ and likewise for the second term, ``rather remarkable: there is a $\Gamma$ of which one usually cannot take a covariant derivative because it is not a tensor, but we see that the \emph{variation} is a tensor''. Both sides are tensors, so the equation holds in all charts, not only in normal coordinates. This proves the formula for $\delta R_{ab}$.

For the contraction with $g^{ab}$ one needs a notation: for a $(1,1)$ tensor with components $a^i{}_j$, the covariant derivative in direction $b$ is written $a^i{}_{j;b}$, ``a semicolon says: take the covariant derivative of the \emph{tensor}, and then take that component out of the resulting tensor; do not think you take the covariant derivative of the components''. In this notation $\nabla_m\delta\Gamma^m{}_{ab}=\delta\Gamma^m{}_{ab;m}$ and $\nabla_b\delta\Gamma^m{}_{am}=\delta\Gamma^m{}_{am;b}$. Now pull the $g^{ab}$ inside the semicolon: because the connection is metric compatible, the covariant derivative satisfies a product rule and $\nabla g=0$ and $\nabla g^{-1}=0$, so $g^{ab}\,\delta\Gamma^m{}_{ab;m}=(g^{ab}\delta\Gamma^m{}_{ab})_{;m}$ without harm. This looks much better: in the first term the index $m$ is summed with the derivative, the index $a,b$ with $g^{ab}$, and what is left is a vector $A^m=g^{ab}\delta\Gamma^m{}_{ab}$ \emph{covariantly derived in its own direction}: the divergence of a vector. In the second term the summed indices make $B^b=g^{ab}\delta\Gamma^m{}_{am}$, a different vector, again with a divergence $B^b{}_{;b}$ \ts{15}{1}{18:44}\ts{15}{1}{19:07}\ts{15}{1}{19:29}\ts{15}{1}{19:50}\ts{15}{1}{20:10}\ts{15}{1}{20:36}\ts{15}{1}{20:56}\ts{15}{1}{21:17}\ts{15}{1}{21:38}\ts{15}{1}{21:59}\ts{15}{1}{22:20}\ts{15}{1}{22:42}\ts{15}{1}{23:03}\ts{15}{1}{23:25}\ts{15}{1}{23:46}\ts{15}{1}{24:06}\ts{15}{1}{24:27}\ts{15}{1}{24:49}\ts{15}{1}{25:09}\ts{15}{1}{25:29}\ts{15}{1}{25:50}\ts{15}{1}{26:11}\ts{15}{1}{26:31}\ts{15}{1}{26:52}\ts{15}{1}{27:12}.

Why is this interesting? It is a divergence of a vector, and remarkably, because the indices are summed, the product $\sqrt{-\det g}\,V^m{}_{;m}$ equals the \emph{partial} derivative $\bigl(\sqrt{-\det g}\,V^m\bigr)_{,m}$: the corrections that come from the Christoffel symbols in the covariant divergence are exactly those that come from applying the product rule to $\partial_m\sqrt{-\det g}=\sqrt{-\det g}\,\Gamma^s{}_{sm}$ (the contracted Christoffel symbol). One can check this; Schuller says that the $\sqrt{-\det g}$ taken inside gives the same result and moves on \ts{15}{1}{27:32}\ts{15}{1}{27:53}.
\end{proof}
\begin{coursenote}[Student questions on the difference of connections]

(3) In the step to the semicolon notation the comma is the \emph{partial} derivative and the semicolon the covariant one (Schuller corrects himself on the board: ``I am sorry, only comma as partial''); the product rule then puts the partial on the first factor, which reproduces itself, and on the other factor, which produces the Christoffel symbol that goes with the index, the right contracted $\Gamma$ \ts{15}{1}{28:16}\ts{15}{1}{28:39}\ts{15}{1}{29:02}\ts{15}{1}{29:23}\ts{15}{1}{29:43}\ts{15}{1}{30:03}\ts{15}{1}{30:24}\ts{15}{1}{30:47}\ts{15}{1}{31:08}.

\paragraph{Collecting the terms.} Using the three lemmas, the variation of the action with respect to $g_{mn}$ is
\[
\begin{aligned}
  0=\delta S_{\mathrm{Hilbert}}&=\int\mathrm d^4x\,\Bigl[\underbrace{\tfrac12\sqrt{-\det g}\,g^{mn}\delta g_{mn}\;g^{ab}R_{ab}}_{\text{term 1}}
  +\underbrace{\sqrt{-\det g}\,\bigl(-g^{am}g^{bn}\delta g_{mn}\bigr)R_{ab}}_{\text{term 2}}\\
  &\qquad+\underbrace{\partial_m\bigl(\sqrt{-\det g}\,A^m\bigr)-\partial_b\bigl(\sqrt{-\det g}\,B^b\bigr)}_{\text{term 3}}\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\]
\ts{15}{1}{32:33}\ts{15}{1}{32:57}\ts{15}{1}{33:19}\ts{15}{1}{33:40}\ts{15}{1}{34:00}\ts{15}{1}{34:20}
Term 3 is an integral over a volume of a derivative contracted like a divergence, hence (Stokes) a \emph{surface term}, and so is the other. If one is not very careful, one says they can be discarded because they vanish if there is no boundary; see the remark on boundary terms below. For the \emph{equations of motion} they do not make a difference, so they are taken out. What is left: factor out the arbitrary variation $\delta g_{mn}$ and the density,
\[
  0=\int\mathrm d^4x\,\sqrt{-\det g}\,\Bigl[\tfrac12\,g^{mn}R-R^{mn}\Bigr]\delta g_{mn}.
\]
For an arbitrary variation the integral vanishes only if the square bracket vanishes, so the tensor that comes out of the variation, defined with the minus sign, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c15-einsteintensor}
  G^{mn}=R^{mn}-\tfrac12\,g^{mn}R
\end{equation}
(``I should have written $-G^{mn}$ before; I did not make a sign mistake, it is just a question of how I call this guy'') \ts{15}{1}{34:45}\ts{15}{1}{35:05}\ts{15}{1}{36:06}\ts{15}{1}{36:28}\ts{15}{1}{36:51}\ts{15}{1}{37:11}\ts{15}{1}{37:31}.
\begin{coursenote}[Sign of the variation, and the normalisation of the action]
(Not said in class.) With the sign of the lecture one has $\delta S_{\mathrm{Hilbert}}=-\int\sqrt{-\det g}\,G^{mn}\delta g_{mn}$, which is why the lecture says it is just a question of what one calls the tensor. To obtain the factor $8\pi G_{\mathrm N}$ in the field equation one multiplies $S_{\mathrm{Hilbert}}$ by a constant $1/(16\pi G_{\mathrm N})$ [up to the sign convention of $T$, \cref{def:c14-emt}] and adds $S_{\mathrm{matter}}$; the lecture does not write this constant out, and later sets $8\pi G_{\mathrm N}=1$.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the Einstein curvature and the Einstein equation]\label{def:c15-einstein}
The $(0,2)$ tensor field
\[
  G_{ab}:=R_{ab}-\tfrac12\,g_{ab}\,R
\]
(the same object as \eqref{eq:c15-einsteintensor} with the indices pulled down with the metric) is the \textbf{Einstein curvature tensor}. Hilbert concluded, from this mathematical argument, that one may take as the field equation of gravity coupled to matter
\[
  R_{ab}-\tfrac12\,g_{ab}\,R=8\pi G_{\mathrm N}\,T_{ab},
\]
the \textbf{Einstein (field) equations}, with the energy-momentum tensor of chapter~\ref{ch:c14} on the right. ``This you need to know by heart: this is the key point of this course.''
\end{definition}
\ts{15}{1}{37:53}\ts{15}{1}{38:17}\ts{15}{1}{47:04}\ts{15}{1}{47:26}
In fact Einstein arrived by \emph{physical} arguments at the same result, more or less at the same time. The equations are called the Einstein equations (Hilbert is not mentioned), but the action is today called the \textbf{Einstein--Hilbert action}: ``I think everybody agrees that does both of them justice, because Einstein came here by physical arguments again, and Hilbert, in a sense, scooped Einstein by listening to one of his talks, hearing about this problem, saying let us try this and doing this little calculation'' (as said in class, not checked). ``These are the equations on which general relativity today is based.'' ``Still, there is at this point no better argument for this than saying this is a pretty nice compact action, one can of course argue whether that is a good reason for a physicist or not'' \ts{15}{1}{38:40}\ts{15}{1}{39:00}\ts{15}{1}{39:21}\ts{15}{1}{39:42}.

\paragraph{The contracted Bianchi identity solves the problem.} In the tutorials one will show that the Einstein curvature satisfies, ``that is just the name you find in the books'', the \textbf{contracted (differential) Bianchi identity}
\[
  \nabla_aG^{ab}=0.
\]
``You would not have guessed it.'' So the inconsistency is indeed solved, and it had to be solved: the left-hand side was derived from an action formulated without charts, and the same argument that leads to $\nabla_aT^{ab}=0$ (``that was your question'') leads to this identity; if it did not hold, ``then somebody somewhere made a mistake''. So this dissolves the argument: Einstein really \emph{forgot a term} in \eqref{eq:c15-guess}, which comes up by this consistency argument \ts{15}{1}{40:03}\ts{15}{1}{40:24}\ts{15}{1}{40:45}\ts{15}{1}{41:06}\ts{15}{1}{41:26}\ts{15}{1}{41:47}.
\begin{coursenote}[Other ways to arrive at the same equations]
Over the years people have found other consistency arguments: for instance one can fill spacetime with a fluid and understand how relativistic fluids behave, ``and then you pick up this term''. A recent result, which Schuller promised to talk about at the end of the school (constructive gravity, chapters~\ref{ch:c29} and following), is that one arrives at the Einstein equations by requiring that the dynamics of spacetime be compatible with putting Maxwell theory on it such that Maxwell theory remains predictive and quantizable: ``that already fixes the Einstein equations'' \ts{15}{1}{42:07}\ts{15}{1}{42:29}\ts{15}{1}{42:50}.
\end{coursenote}
\begin{coursenote}[Student question: could one do the same in Newtonian spacetime?]
No: one cannot even construct the curvature scalar there. Newtonian spacetime carries only a \emph{connection}, which does not derive from a metric (the Newtonian spacetime of lecture 9 has no metric: ``I presented the Newtonian picture before we introduced metrics, because I did not want you to get confused''). From a connection one gets the Riemann tensor, and from it, by contraction of the first and third index, the Ricci tensor; but one does not get the two indices of the Ricci tensor contracted away unless one has a metric. In the Newtonian spacetime one found only that the $00$ component of the Ricci tensor is $4\pi G_{\mathrm N}\rho$; ``I could not even say that all the others are zero, nor what the scalar was''. So the relativistic equations rely heavily on the fact that the curvature tensors are built from the Levi-Civita connection of the metric: ``these are the metric curvatures'' \ts{15}{1}{42:50}\ts{15}{1}{43:15}\ts{15}{1}{43:37}\ts{15}{1}{43:57}\ts{15}{1}{44:19}\ts{15}{1}{44:39}.
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{Boundary terms.} The surface terms that were dropped matter for the \emph{value} of the action but not for the equations of motion. In point-particle mechanics one fixes the two endpoints and takes care that things are fixed there; for a field theory properly done one fixes an initial and a final hypersurface, fixes the fields there and varies them in between, ``and then maybe you take the boundary to infinity, which is somewhat subtle''. In the gravitational case the surface terms contain the \emph{derivative} of the metric normal to the boundary, so one cannot fix points and velocities at the same time (``the Hilbert action depends on the metric up to the second derivative; you cannot fix the second derivatives as well; you can fix the metric''), and one has to discuss carefully what happens at the boundaries. ``This was only discovered later that you have to do this'': Gibbons and others looked at these boundary terms (as said in class, not checked), and they play a role in the canonical formulation, which Giulini presents in the satellite lectures (chapters~\ref{ch:c18} and~\ref{ch:c19}), where one takes good care of them. For the equations of motion they drop out: ``they only play a role in the absolute value of the action, not for the equations of motion''. For a careful treatment: Eric Poisson, \emph{A Relativist's Toolkit} (``an excellent book, Cambridge University Press'') \ts{15}{1}{34:45}\ts{15}{1}{35:25}\ts{15}{1}{35:46}\ts{15}{1}{36:06}\ts{15}{1}{45:42}\ts{15}{1}{46:03}\ts{15}{1}{46:23}\ts{15}{1}{54:50}\ts{15}{1}{55:12}.

\section{Variants of the field equations}\label{sec:c15-variants}

``Finally we look at variants of the field equations.'' These are not other gravitational theories: ``the same field equations, a little extended or a little differently written down'' \ts{15}{1}{46:43}.

\subsection{The trace-reversed form}

Take the field equations with $8\pi G_{\mathrm N}$ set to $1$ for the heck of it (``so that I can finally get rid of these annoying factors''; also $c=1$ already: ``who cares''). Contract both sides with $g^{ab}$, a full contraction. The left side gives $R-\tfrac12\cdot4\,R=-R$ (``$g_{ab}g^{ab}$ is the dimension of spacetime, four, and half of it is two'': the lecture writes $R-2R$), the right side the trace $T:=g^{ab}T_{ab}$; hence $R=-T$. Insert into the field equation: $R_{ab}+\tfrac12\,g_{ab}\,T=T_{ab}$, i.e.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c15-trace}
  R_{ab}=T_{ab}-\tfrac12\,g_{ab}\,T=:\hat T_{ab}.
\end{equation}
The right-hand side is a \emph{modified energy-momentum tensor} $\hat T_{ab}$: the energy-momentum tensor minus half its trace times the metric. So one can relate the Ricci curvature directly to a modified energy-momentum tensor, and ``Einstein was right after all, he just took the wrong version of the energy-momentum tensor'', one with a modified trace; of course the divergence of $\hat T_{ab}$ no longer vanishes. ``It is just a simple rewriting''; it is useful to write it like this sometimes, ``just a simple conclusion from the field equations themselves'' \ts{15}{1}{47:04}\ts{15}{1}{47:26}\ts{15}{1}{47:46}\ts{15}{1}{48:07}\ts{15}{1}{48:28}\ts{15}{1}{48:51}\ts{15}{1}{49:12}\ts{15}{1}{49:32}\ts{15}{1}{49:52}.
\begin{coursenote}[Vacuum]
(Not said in class.) In vacuum $T_{ab}=0$ and \eqref{eq:c15-trace} says $R_{ab}=0$: the vacuum field equations in the form that is usually solved (Schwarzschild, chapter~\ref{ch:c22}).
\end{coursenote}

\subsection{The cosmological constant}

The second variant \emph{is} different physics: add a constant term to the Einstein--Hilbert action,
\[
  S_\Lambda[g]=\int\mathrm d^4x\,\sqrt{-\det g}\,\bigl(R+2\Lambda\bigr),
\]
where $\Lambda$ is called the \textbf{cosmological constant}, ``nothing mysterious, it is just a constant that you add'' \ts{15}{1}{49:52}\ts{15}{1}{50:13}\ts{15}{1}{50:34}. (The lecture writes ``$+2\Lambda$'' on the board; the sign and the normalisation of the term relative to $R$ depend on the signature conventions of the course and are not checked here [?].)

\begin{aside}[The cosmological constant]
As said in class (not checked): in 1915 Einstein said it must be there, and with it, because only if one puts the constant there does one get solutions for the cosmology of all of spacetime that produce a \emph{static} universe, a non-expanding one; at the time it seemed ridiculous that the universe should be expanding instead of just sitting there. Later Hubble saw that the universe is expanding, which is perfectly compatible with $\Lambda=0$, and Einstein said it was ``my greatest blunder'' and set $\Lambda=0$; ``there are no doubt many papers saying $\Lambda$ must be zero''. Today $\Lambda$ is supposed to be bigger than zero, to account for an \emph{accelerated} expansion of the universe (details later in the course, chapters~\ref{ch:c20} and~\ref{ch:c21}) \ts{15}{1}{50:34}\ts{15}{1}{50:56}\ts{15}{1}{51:18}\ts{15}{1}{51:39}\ts{15}{1}{52:00}\ts{15}{1}{52:20}.

(Not checked: the usual dating of the cosmological term is Einstein's 1917 cosmological paper, not 1915 [?].)
\end{aside}
\begin{coursenote}[A slip in the captions]
The caption at this point reads that Einstein wanted the constant ``less than zero''; for a static universe one needs a positive $\Lambda$ in the usual sign convention, and the lecture later says that today's value is positive; the captions are garbled here, so the sign statement is not reliable [?] \ts{15}{1}{50:56}.
\end{coursenote}

The problem is that this $\Lambda$ is supposed to be bigger than zero but \emph{very very small}: small compared to any effect one could imagine that produces a non-vanishing $\Lambda$. To clarify: carrying out the variation with the extra term, the $\Lambda$ contribution can be understood as a contribution to \emph{matter}. If one says that this part still belongs to gravity, fine, but it could also belong to a matter action, and $\Lambda\neq0$ can be interpreted as a contribution $-\tfrac12\Lambda\,g_{ab}$ to the energy-momentum of matter in spacetime [sign and factor as written on the board, not checked]. What does that mean? Constantly overall in the universe there is energy provided by $\Lambda$: it is not just where the fields are non-vanishing but \emph{everywhere there is a metric, and a metric is everywhere}. People call this \textbf{dark energy}: it does not interact with anything but it contributes to the curvature of spacetime, ``that means it is dark'' \ts{15}{1}{52:42}\ts{15}{1}{53:04}\ts{15}{1}{53:27}\ts{15}{1}{53:48}\ts{15}{1}{54:09}\ts{15}{1}{54:29}.
\begin{coursenote}[Student question: could $\Lambda$ come from the surface terms?]
No (the answer is partly drowned in the captions). One can carefully treat the surface terms (Poisson's book), ``but the point is this is at the level of the equations of motion, where you identify this: it could not come from the surface terms; the surface terms are the action level, this is the level of the equations of motion''. The origin of the term is plain: the volume of spacetime is the integral of the constant function $1$ against the density, and $2\Lambda$ times this volume in the action, varied, gives a contribution to the field equations; it does not destroy the Bianchi identity (the $g_{ab}$ term has zero covariant divergence). The physical question is what it could be, since nothing so far provides such an energy everywhere \ts{15}{1}{54:50}\ts{15}{1}{55:33}\ts{15}{1}{55:53}\ts{15}{1}{56:14}\ts{15}{1}{56:36}.
\end{coursenote}
\begin{aside}[The worst prediction of physics]
Weinberg and others had the idea that $\Lambda$ could be the \emph{vacuum fluctuations of quantum fields}: electrons and positrons come into existence and annihilate each other again; one calculates the vacuum energy of the standard model fields (``I think from QCD'') and how it would contribute to $\Lambda$. The $\Lambda$ calculated this way is ``a whopping $10^{100}$ times bigger'' (Schuller corrects himself a moment later: $10^{120}$) than the $\Lambda$ observed: ``the worst prediction of physics'' (he starts ``the worst observation''). So one has an idea of the mechanism, but it overestimates by a factor $10^{120}$; hence it is not understood why there is a positive and so small cosmological constant: a puzzle. If one puts it in phenomenologically, it fits the observations of the universe (to come) \ts{15}{1}{56:57}\ts{15}{1}{57:17}\ts{15}{1}{57:40}\ts{15}{1}{58:00}\ts{15}{1}{58:20}\ts{15}{1}{58:41}\ts{15}{1}{59:02}.
\end{aside}
