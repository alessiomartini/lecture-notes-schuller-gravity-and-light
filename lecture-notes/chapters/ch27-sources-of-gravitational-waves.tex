\section{Introduction: the goals, and a sociologist}\label{sec:c27-intro}

The aim is to put the physics, the astronomy and the science goals onto the picture of the lecturer's public lecture of the evening before, and to understand why gravitational waves are detected now. There are two lectures: this one on \emph{sources} (the lecturer says he gave it a slightly different title, about compact objects and their gravitational waves, ``but it is the same subject, a little broader than just compact objects''): the waves generated in astronomy, how strong they can be, what the sensitivity goals of detectors are and why they have to be so sensitive, and what information gravitational waves would give. Very little about the detectors themselves, ``I ask you to accept that we will have detectors that do certain kinds of things''; the second lecture goes into the techniques and the physics challenges of building them \ts{27}{1}{0:00}\ts{27}{1}{0:21}\ts{27}{1}{0:42}\ts{27}{1}{1:03}\ts{27}{1}{1:23}.

A quote from a book by Harry Collins sums it up: gravitational wave detection is ``about seeing the biggest things that ever happen, collisions, explosions and quaking of stars and black holes, by measuring the smallest changes that have ever been measured'': looking for something really important because it is big, with a very sensitive and very difficult apparatus because the changes are very small \ts{27}{1}{1:44}\ts{27}{1}{2:05}\ts{27}{1}{2:26}.

\begin{aside}[Harry Collins and the sociology of a collaboration]
Collins is a sociologist, not a physicist, who at one time gained notoriety in the physics community by a debate in \emph{Physics Today} on whether the world is real and whether what physicists describe is real. He then became interested in gravitational waves from the sociological point of view: the collaboration grew from some 20--30 people to about a thousand worldwide; he has access to the collaboration, to its internal documents and to all meetings, on the agreement that he does not divulge confidential things, and has written three or four books and several articles: ``in a sense we are his lab rats''. He has been ``converted to physics'': at the last big collaboration meeting he said that gravitational waves are out there and ``you guys are going to see them'' \ts{27}{1}{2:46}\ts{27}{1}{3:07}\ts{27}{1}{3:28}\ts{27}{1}{3:48}\ts{27}{1}{4:09}\ts{27}{1}{4:29}.

The serious point set aside: the LIGO Scientific Collaboration is a voluntary organisation of six or seven hundred scientists (with other collaborations having agreements with it), modelled on particle physics collaborations, ``self-governing in a way very different from our political institutions'': entirely voluntary and consensual, a spokesperson elected periodically with almost no power and a lot of work, decisions taken democratically on the basis of the physics, implemented by people who do a lot of work without extra reward. There is a hierarchy of scientific respect and if the top people say what should happen the collaboration generally does it, but in a consensual way, not enforced by a hierarchy; ``it is kind of amazing that it holds together'' \ts{27}{1}{4:49}\ts{27}{1}{5:10}\ts{27}{1}{5:30}\ts{27}{1}{5:50}\ts{27}{1}{6:12}\ts{27}{1}{6:32}\ts{27}{1}{6:52}\ts{27}{1}{7:13}.
\end{aside}

\section{The physics that matters for observation}\label{sec:c27-physics}

The lecturer highlights what is most important from the observational point of view, since the audience has had detailed lectures on gravitational waves and perturbation theory (chapters~\ref{ch:c24} and~\ref{ch:c25}) \ts{27}{1}{7:13}\ts{27}{1}{7:33}. The points are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item General relativity might not be correct; it is certainly not a quantum theory, so there will be corrections at some level, and they could affect gravitational waves, in the \emph{speed} or the \emph{polarization}: one should be alert to this when making observations. The information gravitational waves carry is probably obtainable in no other way \ts{27}{1}{7:54}\ts{27}{1}{8:15}.
  \item The \textbf{equivalence principle} is incorporated in many alternative theories too, and means that gravitational waves operate by \textbf{tidal forces}: in free fall there is no gravity, so a passing wave is not felt, however strong, except by the differential action across the body or apparatus. A detector must look for differences of acceleration at different places, and gains by being bigger, since to first order the effect grows linearly with the length \ts{27}{1}{8:36}\ts{27}{1}{8:56}\ts{27}{1}{9:17}\ts{27}{1}{9:38}.
  \item In general relativity the speed of gravitational waves is the speed of light. The metric is a perturbation on a background, conventionally called $h$, usually a very small perturbation on the effect of the Newtonian potential. There are only \emph{two transverse polarization modes}, and the stretching is, with the conventional $h$,
  \[
    h\;\leftrightarrow\;2\,\frac{\Delta L}{L}
  \]
  (``$h$ turns out to be twice the tidal distortion $\Delta L/L$''; the lecturer a little later says ``I left out the factor of a half'': $\Delta L/L=\tfrac12 h$). The tidal distortion is the gradient of the acceleration, in Newtonian terms the second derivative of the Newtonian potential, in general relativity the \textbf{curvature tensor}: ``so what we are really measuring with gravitational waves directly is the curvature tensor'' \ts{27}{1}{9:38}\ts{27}{1}{9:59}\ts{27}{1}{10:19}\ts{27}{1}{10:39}.
  \item The most sensitive detectors that can be built at the moment are interferometers, which are linearly polarized detectors, as are all detectors built; hence it is important to have detectors with different orientations, to pick up different polarization modes \ts{27}{1}{10:39}\ts{27}{1}{11:00}.
  \item In the \textbf{transverse traceless gauge} for linear perturbations of a flat background, the equations without source are simply the wave equation, ``just like the equations for electromagnetic waves'' \ts{27}{1}{11:00}\ts{27}{1}{11:21}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The polarization diagram.} Imagine a ring of free particles in outer space, with no connections between them, and a gravitational wave coming in onto the plane of the ring. One polarization state distorts the circle into an ellipse; since the wave oscillates one gets an ellipse with the same axes but major and minor axes reversed, then back to the circle: this is the full cycle. The orthogonal polarization is a $45^\circ$ rotation of the axes. Any gravitational wave is a linear combination of these two polarizations \ts{27}{1}{11:42}\ts{27}{1}{12:03}\ts{27}{1}{12:23}\ts{27}{1}{12:43}.

\paragraph{The interferometer.} Simple sketch: light comes in through a beam splitter, goes along two different arms, with the mirrors at the endpoints of the arms. Place the mirrors on the ring of particles and the beam splitter at the centre: when the wave with the first polarization distorts the circle into an ellipse, it moves one mirror further away and brings the other closer, which changes the interference pattern at the beam splitter. If arranged such that without a wave there is complete destructive interference and no light comes out, then with a wave there is light coming out, and its intensity is proportional to the gravitational wave signal. ``Interferometers are essentially ideal for detecting gravitational waves; it is exactly the kind of thing you want to be doing'', with a long history as detectors \ts{27}{1}{13:04}\ts{27}{1}{13:25}\ts{27}{1}{13:45}\ts{27}{1}{14:07}\ts{27}{1}{14:28}.

\paragraph{Standard sirens.} For binary systems in orbit, which are the most common sources expected, shrinking as the Hulse--Taylor binary pulsar does (almost all the binaries to be detected in the near future), the rate of change of the orbit translates into a rate of change of the frequency of the gravitational waves. If this is in the signal then a combination of measurable quantities gives the \emph{distance} to the source: with $f$ the frequency and the \textbf{chirp time} $\tau$ the time scale of the rate of change of the frequency, one has (the lecturer writes only the structure)
\[
  \frac{c\,(\text{number})}{h\,f^{2}\,\tau}\;\sim\;\text{distance},
\]
``a number out in front, one over the frequency squared, the chirp time, and the amplitude $h$ which you are measuring'': since $h$ is dimensionless this combination has the dimensions of a distance, and on cosmological scales it is the \textbf{luminosity distance} [the exact combination is not on the captions]. There are numerical factors depending on the orientation of the source, which must be worked out (hence the polarization has to be measured); they are left out. This is an ``extremely unusual situation in astronomy'': to know how far away the source is from intrinsic information in the signal, a calibration for the distance ladders, which one hopes to tie to those of astronomy. Every neutron star and black hole binary detected by LIGO will be coalescing, so one has these numbers for each source \ts{27}{1}{14:28}\ts{27}{1}{14:50}\ts{27}{1}{15:11}\ts{27}{1}{15:32}\ts{27}{1}{15:54}\ts{27}{1}{16:14}\ts{27}{1}{16:34}\ts{27}{1}{16:55}.

\section{Order of magnitude estimates}\label{sec:c27-estimates}

\subsection{The amplitude}

Generation of gravitational waves, building on known things \ts{27}{1}{17:15}. The \textbf{quadrupole formula} is the fundamental formula for the generation in the slow motion limit, as the dipole formula is for electromagnetism of charged particles. Manipulating it for scalings: (i) replace the integral over the volume of the mass density by the mass $M$ of the system; (ii) replace each coordinate $x$ in the integrand by the size $R$ of the radiating system; (iii) for a \emph{self-gravitating} system replace the time derivatives (basically velocities) by the gravitational potential, using the virial theorem \ts{27}{1}{17:35}\ts{27}{1}{17:57}\ts{27}{1}{18:17}\ts{27}{1}{18:38}\ts{27}{1}{18:59}\ts{27}{1}{19:19}.

\begin{proposition}[Upper limit on the amplitude, order of magnitude]\label{prop:c27-hmax}
With $M$ the mass and $R$ the size of the self-gravitating source and $r$ its distance,
\[
  h\;\lesssim\;\frac{GM}{c^2R}\cdot\frac{GM}{c^2r},
\]
the product of two Newtonian potentials made dimensionless with $1/c^2$: the internal potential of the system and the Newtonian potential of the system at our location (``a very tiny number'').
\end{proposition}
\ts{27}{1}{19:19}\ts{27}{1}{19:40}\ts{27}{1}{20:00}

It is an \emph{upper limit}, because of symmetry: a perfectly spherical body, pulsating or collapsing spherically, radiates no gravitational waves, with the same scaling but $h=0$. So it can go all the way down to zero, but it is attainable: a maximally asymmetric system, two equal mass stars in orbit, is a perfect quadrupole system and the formula works very well. It works only for self-gravity (because of the virial theorem): ``not for springs with masses on them''. It is useful to plug in numbers \ts{27}{1}{20:00}\ts{27}{1}{20:21}\ts{27}{1}{20:42}\ts{27}{1}{21:03}.

\subsection{The energy flux}

As for other wave fields (electromagnetism in terms of the vector potential, a scalar wave) the energy flux is proportional to the square of the time derivative of the amplitude, and so in general relativity,
\[
  F\;=\;\frac{c^{3}}{32\pi G}\,\dot h^{2}
\]
(energy per unit time and area, with the dimensional constant $c^3/G$ and the factor $1/32\pi$; averaging over a period and polarizations understood [not said]). ``I put in $c^3/G$, you can trust me that this comes out with those dimensions; then I need the factor in front, and that is where all the work is, three days of calculating on a piece of paper to get the $1/32\pi$; but if I did not care about it, the rest I can write down without thinking, if I believe that general relativity fits in with all the other classical field theories'' \ts{27}{1}{21:23}\ts{27}{1}{21:44}\ts{27}{1}{22:04}\ts{27}{1}{22:25}\ts{27}{1}{22:45}\ts{27}{1}{23:06}.

\subsection{Neutron star binary and supernova}

Take $M=1.5\,M_\odot$ (a typical neutron star, 1.4 or so), size $R=10$ km (a typical neutron star radius), at $100$ Mpc, which the lecturer mentioned the night before as the distance of the nearest coalescing neutron star binary in a one year observation, from the astronomy of the distance scale. ``I get $h$ around $10^{-21}$'': this tells where the detector sensitivity has to go; no good a detector that detects $h=10^{-12}$ (a tidal effect of $10^{-12}$, a wonderful thing to build but not detecting interesting gravitational waves) \ts{27}{1}{23:26}\ts{27}{1}{23:47}\ts{27}{1}{24:07}\ts{27}{1}{24:27}\ts{27}{1}{24:48}.

\begin{coursenote}[Arithmetic check, not said in class]
With the formula of \cref{prop:c27-hmax} and these numbers one finds $GM/c^2\simeq2.2$ km, so $GM/(c^2R)\simeq0.2$ and $GM/(c^2r)\simeq2.2\,\mathrm{km}/(3\times10^{21}\,\mathrm{m})\simeq7\times10^{-19}$, i.e.\ an upper bound $h\lesssim10^{-19}$, two orders above the $10^{-21}$ quoted: the quoted value is the realistic one (the lecture says explicitly that factors of two and the geometrical factor are not tracked; the orbit size at the merger and the orientation matter) and is what sets the detector goal. The reader should take $10^{-21}$ as the lecture's target, not as the output of the one line formula.
\end{coursenote}

Supernovae used to be very important in the motivation of the detection. The nearest supernova can be a factor of $1000$ closer: $100$ kpc is roughly the distance to the Large Magellanic Cloud, and the most recent nearby supernova was in the Large Magellanic Cloud, $50$ kpc away. Bringing the same numbers a factor of a thousand closer boosts $h$ to $10^{-18}$, which was for a long time the goal of detectors. But this leaves out that it is an upper bound with a geometrical factor that can go to zero: detailed numerical simulations of supernova explosions show they are nearly spherical, not highly asymmetric, and so far from $10^{-18}$; ``again we are pushed down to $10^{-21}$ or smaller for nearby supernovae, and that is the picture across the whole spectrum of different sources'' \ts{27}{1}{25:08}\ts{27}{1}{25:29}\ts{27}{1}{25:49}\ts{27}{1}{26:09}.

\subsection{The peak luminosity of a black hole merger}

``The intrinsically strongest gravitational wave thing we can imagine'': two black holes in orbit about to merge, velocities close to the speed of light, the metric perturbation of order one nearby. Take $h\sim1$ just outside the system; the time derivative is set by velocities of order $c$ over the size of the system, set by the mass $m$ (as a length, $m=GM/c^2$) up to factors of two. Hand waving, the flux is
\[
  F\;\sim\;\frac{c^{5}}{32\pi G\,m^{2}}
\]
(energy per unit time and area on a little sphere just around the system; the extra $c$ relative to $c^3/G$ comes from the time derivative $c/m$), scaling as $1/m^2$ \ts{27}{1}{26:30}\ts{27}{1}{26:50}\ts{27}{1}{27:11}\ts{27}{1}{27:32}. Integrating over a sphere of radius $m$ (multiplying by $4\pi m^2$) gives the total luminosity,
\[
  L_{\max}\;\sim\;\frac18\,\frac{c^{5}}{G}\;\approx\;10^{52}\ \mathrm W,
\]
and the $m^2$ drops out: ``interestingly it is a pure number, a pure combination of fundamental constants, with the dimensions of luminosity; it is a huge number because $G$ is tiny and $c^5$ large'' \ts{27}{1}{27:52}\ts{27}{1}{28:13}\ts{27}{1}{28:33}.

Where does it fit? The luminosity of the sun is $4\times10^{26}$ W, so this is about $10^{26}$ suns. Stars: our galaxy has about $10^{11}$ stars and the observable universe about $10^{11}$ galaxies, so $10^{22}$ suns; this one thing is $10^{26}$ times the luminosity of any one of them. The comparison is of energy, gravitational waves against light, but nevertheless the energy output at the time of the merger is far higher than the output of all the stars in the entire observable universe \ts{27}{1}{28:33}\ts{27}{1}{28:53}\ts{27}{1}{29:13}\ts{27}{1}{29:36}\ts{27}{1}{29:56}. A quasar, the most luminous individual objects (as luminous as galaxies), has $10^{37}$--$10^{38}$ W: ``piddling'' by comparison. It only lasts a tiny time, the light crossing time of the system, so the total energy radiated is a more reasonable amount \ts{27}{1}{30:17}\ts{27}{1}{30:38}\ts{27}{1}{30:59}\ts{27}{1}{31:19}.

\section{The network of detectors}\label{sec:c27-network}

The detectors operating or being upgraded \ts{27}{1}{31:19}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the two \textbf{LIGO} detectors in the United States, four kilometre interferometers, in Washington state and Louisiana;
  \item \textbf{Virgo}, a 3 km interferometer outside Pisa in Italy;
  \item \textbf{GEO600}, a smaller one of 600 m, in Germany near Hanover (an arrow on the slide points wrongly to Switzerland).
\end{itemize}
The two LIGO and GEO form the LIGO Scientific Collaboration (LSC); there is a data sharing and joint data analysis agreement with Virgo, outside the LSC \ts{27}{1}{31:40}\ts{27}{1}{32:00}.

\paragraph{Why so many detectors and why cooperation.} Cooperation is not the rule at CERN: for the Higgs boson two detector groups are rivals, publish independent papers, and the community believes only if both are consistent. Here there is no competition: data are pooled and jointly analysed; LIGO will come online about a year before Virgo, and if the detection is in the first year the Virgo people will still be on the paper even though their detector is not operating. Reasons \ts{27}{1}{32:23}\ts{27}{1}{32:43}\ts{27}{1}{33:04}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Confidence}: the two CERN groups see their own Higgs bosons, many of them; here ``we are looking at the same gravitational wave sources, arriving here and then there'': one must make sure it is the same one, and extract the polarization and the sky location \ts{27}{1}{33:24}\ts{27}{1}{33:45}.
  \item \emph{Duty cycle}: when run for three years the detectors had about 80\,\% duty cycle: they are hard to operate and keep stable, so redundancy matters \ts{27}{1}{33:45}\ts{27}{1}{34:05}.
  \item \emph{Noise}: with all the information one averages over the noise and extracts a waveform \ts{27}{1}{34:05}\ts{27}{1}{34:26}.
  \item \emph{Discrimination against glitches}: ``glitch is a scientific term, if you didn't know'': something went wrong that is not understood; things suddenly appear in the data, a pop that can look remarkably like a gravitational wave, but only in one detector and not in the others, which shows it comes from the detector; with a single detector one could not know. One compares the detectors in \textbf{coincidence mode} \ts{27}{1}{34:26}\ts{27}{1}{34:46}\ts{27}{1}{35:07}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Advanced LIGO and data analysis}\label{sec:c27-adligo}

The upgrade to Advanced LIGO is under way (Advanced Virgo too). The sensitivity diagram, to be explained in the next lecture, shows the strain $h$ ($10^{-23}$, $10^{-22}$) against frequency from $10$ Hz to $1000$ Hz, the main LIGO observing band: the curves are plots of the noise, the lower the more sensitive. The first detectors are hoped to turn on perhaps by the end of the year (2015), with sensitivity factors of three or four better than before, perhaps enough for a first detection (``we don't know''): binary neutron stars up to $60$ Mpc \ts{27}{1}{35:07}\ts{27}{1}{35:27}\ts{27}{1}{35:48}\ts{27}{1}{36:08}.

For normalisation: the Virgo cluster is $20$ Mpc away and contains a couple of thousand galaxies; out to $60$ Mpc, a factor three further, the volume is a factor about $30$ more, so $60\,000$ to $100\,000$ galaxies. A binary neutron star inspiral happens about once per $10^5$ years per galaxy (``we think it happens in our galaxy every $10^5$ years, in a typical galaxy''), so with $100\,000$ galaxies one has a chance of one a year. This is an intermediate step: in a couple of years, with the same laser light and improved low frequency performance, the binary neutron star range goes to $140$ Mpc, and astrophysicists are convinced one has enough range for a few events per year, between the end of 2015 and 2017 \ts{27}{1}{36:30}\ts{27}{1}{36:50}\ts{27}{1}{37:11}\ts{27}{1}{37:31}\ts{27}{1}{37:52}.

\paragraph{Data analysis.} A crucial component: the expected signals, in the raw data, are \emph{below} the noise by factors of five or ten and have to be pulled out by intelligent filtering based on prior knowledge of what the signal looks like. Improving the ability to pull signals out of noise by a factor of two improves the range of the detector by a factor of two and the volume by a factor of eight, ``like putting in a couple of million to upgrade the sensitivity''; more than half of the people in the collaboration work on it. The methods are generally applicable to other astronomy: gamma ray pulsars discovered in gamma ray data and new radio pulsars, the work led by Bruce Allen, one of the directors of the Albert Einstein Institute in Hanover \ts{27}{1}{37:52}\ts{27}{1}{38:13}\ts{27}{1}{38:34}\ts{27}{1}{38:55}\ts{27}{1}{39:15}\ts{27}{1}{39:36}\ts{27}{1}{39:57}\ts{27}{1}{40:17}.

Searches have been slow, the code is validated line by line with internal reviews and fake injections into data, so a huge amount of approval and validation precedes anything released. Now the aim is to give \textbf{alerts within a minute}, with everything validated ahead of time, to astronomers who can look for other signatures that would validate a real event. Initially the data is private; after the first detection there will be public releases (the earlier data, 2005--2010, is public already: one can check whether gravitational waves were missed), eventually everything immediately. There are about $40$ collaborations for sharing data with transient event detectors, building up \textbf{multimessenger astronomy} \ts{27}{1}{40:38}\ts{27}{1}{40:58}\ts{27}{1}{41:19}\ts{27}{1}{41:41}\ts{27}{1}{42:01}\ts{27}{1}{42:22}\ts{27}{1}{42:42}.

\paragraph{Localization.} Short bursts (the clapping demonstration of the evening before) are localized with time delays among detectors: with two detectors one gets a circle on the sky, with three a good position but the error box is large, about $10$ square degrees would be good. Astronomers like telescopes focusing on a few arc minutes and are not happy, but wide field survey telescopes covering the whole sky every few nights can look at ten square degrees. Gravitational wave pulsars, a different story, are localized through the Doppler effect of the Earth moving around the Sun, an imposed phase modulation on a continuous signal, exactly how radio astronomers locate pulsars: down to an arc second. ``It is very different depending on the source'' \ts{27}{1}{42:42}\ts{27}{1}{43:03}\ts{27}{1}{43:23}\ts{27}{1}{43:44}\ts{27}{1}{44:04}\ts{27}{1}{44:26}\ts{27}{1}{44:46}.

Later the network will be Hanford, Livingston and Virgo; GEO will not be part of it: it was able to participate earlier because of sophisticated technologies, developed for the upgrades, and is now left behind by its shorter baseline. Three detectors are the minimum for direction finding. The baseline is $27$ ms (light travel time): the resolving power of a telescope depends on the ratio of the wavelength to the size, here the wavelength is comparable or even larger, hence the weak resolving power. First interferometer observations only set upper limits and no big surprises, so the rates for the next generation could be predicted \ts{27}{1}{44:46}\ts{27}{1}{45:07}\ts{27}{1}{45:28}\ts{27}{1}{45:48}\ts{27}{1}{46:08}.

\paragraph{The antenna pattern.} The sensitivity of a detector flat in the $xy$ plane to gravitational waves, averaged over polarization, in different directions (the ``peanut diagram''): most sensitive along the axis (a source directly above or below), zero in the directions where the wave comes in at $45^\circ$ to the two arms, because that is the wrong polarization state, which influences both arms in the same way; apart from the holes the sensitivity varies by factors of two or three over the sky. Hanford and Livingston together have a clear axis of most sensitive detection (seen from over the North Atlantic); with Virgo added the pattern becomes more symmetrical \ts{27}{1}{46:29}\ts{27}{1}{46:49}\ts{27}{1}{47:10}\ts{27}{1}{47:30}\ts{27}{1}{47:50}\ts{27}{1}{48:11}.

\section{Science from neutron star mergers}\label{sec:c27-ns}

Known: seven systems in our own galaxy already on the way to merger, with remaining lifetimes up to about $10^8$ years, discovered by radio observations, which see only the near part of the galaxy (not even as far as the centre). On the basis of this sample and the selection effects the radio astronomers calculate that the one closest to merger is about $10^5$ years away, i.e.\ one merger per $10^5$ years per galaxy \ts{27}{1}{48:11}\ts{27}{1}{48:31}\ts{27}{1}{48:51}\ts{27}{1}{49:13}. What one will learn with one or two per year \ts{27}{1}{49:34}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \textbf{equation of state} of hot neutron stars, a new form of nuclear matter that cannot be duplicated in the laboratory: many calculations, ``we want to understand the nuclear physics'', and it affects the emitted waves \ts{27}{1}{49:54};
  \item the \textbf{mass distribution} of neutron stars in binaries: a mystery, most binaries with a pulsar have pulsar mass around $1.4\,M_\odot$ though pulsars up to about $2\,M_\odot$ are known \ts{27}{1}{50:16}\ts{27}{1}{50:37};
  \item merging black holes instead: a good test of strong field general relativity and, for the first time, the mass distribution of real black holes, of which there are no observations \ts{27}{1}{50:37}\ts{27}{1}{50:57};
  \item \textbf{short gamma ray bursts}: there is good reason to believe that neutron star mergers produce them (as the gamma ray astronomers believe), but they are beamed, covering probably $1\,\%$, maybe $3\,\%$ of the sky, so one in thirty or one in a hundred events will be accompanied by a visible burst, about one per year in coincidence for the first network at full event rate \ts{27}{1}{50:57}\ts{27}{1}{51:19}\ts{27}{1}{51:39}.
\end{itemize}

The threshold of signal to noise is about $12$ in amplitude; compared to optical astronomy with photon counting (energy based, squared) $12$ corresponds to about $150$, ``not bad: they often deal with much worse''. With, say, $30$ events per year when the network is complete, some are stronger, the strongest maybe $27$ [the lecture also says ``30'' with a smaller error box; the captions are unclear]: a smaller error box, a better distance, identification of the counterpart, a redshift, helping the calibration and the cosmology \ts{27}{1}{52:01}\ts{27}{1}{52:21}\ts{27}{1}{52:41}\ts{27}{1}{53:02}.

\begin{aside}[A simulation of a neutron star merger]
The endpoint of a numerical simulation by Rezzolla (at the Albert Einstein Institute then, now at Frankfurt), in full general relativity with full hydrodynamics and magnetohydrodynamics: the magnetic field carried by the two neutron stars is chaotic and stirred up, but when they merge they eventually form a black hole and the field becomes very ordered, with a polar magnetic field ``remarkably coming out like this; it was not put in by hand'': there, one thinks, is where the gamma ray jets come from, channelled by field lines into the $1\,\%$ or $3\,\%$ of the sky \ts{27}{1}{53:23}\ts{27}{1}{53:44}\ts{27}{1}{54:05}.
\end{aside}

\section{Expanding the network: KAGRA and LIGO India}\label{sec:c27-network2}

\section{Black holes, gravitational wave pulsars, Einstein@Home}\label{sec:c27-bh}

\paragraph{Black hole mergers.} ``To me the most interesting things, because we don't see them in any other way''; no one knows how to estimate the numbers, and the estimates are conservative: the range for black holes is much bigger (they are more massive) but the volume rate is probably lower; the expected detection rate is comparable to that of neutron stars, maybe half of it, but some astronomers think there are many more black hole binaries, so the data could be dominated by black hole mergers; ``we really don't understand the black hole binary formation well at all''. They are expected out to redshifts of order one, giving for the first time a census of black holes, binary black holes and their spins. There are very accurate numerical simulations of black hole mergers to compare with general relativity, to test e.g.\ \textbf{cosmic censorship}: it is a conjecture (``we believe that general relativity leads to no naked singularities but we don't know that, it is not a theorem''); does a merger give a black hole with a nice horizon or something weird? It can be tested. And again the Hubble constant \ts{27}{1}{1:00:14}\ts{27}{1}{1:00:34}\ts{27}{1}{1:00:55}\ts{27}{1}{1:01:15}\ts{27}{1}{1:01:36}\ts{27}{1}{1:01:56}.

\paragraph{Gravitational wave pulsars.} Very difficult to detect because very weak: they are not whole stars but little lumps on stars that radiate, so expected only from rather near systems, probably in our own galaxy. To build up the signal one must integrate coherently for months, during which the signal has a huge imposed phase modulation, so the search dominates the computing requirements; it is not done optimally for lack of computer power. There are upper limits from earlier observations, interesting astronomically, no detections yet. The full search runs over all frequencies for each of about $10^{10}$ different sky locations [as heard] \ts{27}{1}{1:02:17}\ts{27}{1}{1:02:38}\ts{27}{1}{1:02:58}\ts{27}{1}{1:03:18}.

\begin{aside}[Einstein@Home]
Suboptimal searches are largely done in \textbf{Einstein@Home}, a screen saver (``there are times I know when you are not looking at your screens; you are asleep sometimes'') whose hidden task analyses downloaded data and sends candidates back to the big computers; tens of thousands of participants and computers, close to a petaflop (hundreds of teraflops) of donated computing, ``which we could never buy'', and the main benefit is also that ``we could not pay for the electricity: when I say leave your computer on overnight you are paying for the electricity''. Run by Bruce Allen at the Hanover institute. The gamma ray and radio pulsars mentioned above were found this way: ordinary people, accountants in Germany, get an email that they participated in a discovery; for redundancy, ``we do not trust any one person's computer'', three participate in each and get their names on the website: ``citizen science'' \ts{27}{1}{1:03:39}\ts{27}{1}{1:03:59}\ts{27}{1}{1:04:20}\ts{27}{1}{1:04:44}\ts{27}{1}{1:05:05}\ts{27}{1}{1:05:26}\ts{27}{1}{1:05:46}\ts{27}{1}{1:06:06}.
\end{aside}

\section{Supernovae, the stochastic background, the Einstein Telescope}\label{sec:c27-other}

\paragraph{Supernovae.} Once the primary motivation of the early attempts, based on the number $10^{-18}$ (see above); now the goal is rather $10^{-21}$ or $10^{-22}$: if a supernova goes off in our galaxy it will be detected, in coincidence with the neutrino detectors running now, a very clear coincidence signal, very interesting; the waveform is hard to predict, ``a very chaotic event'', one would learn a lot, but not from distant galaxies \ts{27}{1}{1:06:27}\ts{27}{1}{1:06:48}\ts{27}{1}{1:07:09}\ts{27}{1}{1:07:29}.

\paragraph{The stochastic background.} The primordial background from the Big Bang (the hiss played the night before) and cosmic strings: opportunities for new physics. With one detector the hiss would have to be louder than the detector's noise, with a lot of confidence. The same story as the discovery of the cosmic microwave background by Penzias and Wilson (``penus and Wilson'' in the captions): a radio telescope with a hiss in every direction, they believed their receiver was quieter than that, eventually realised they saw a cosmic microwave background. Better: \textbf{cross-correlate} two detectors getting the same hiss from cosmology but with uncorrelated noise: multiply the two data streams and integrate, the uncorrelated noise is suppressed relative to the correlated signal. One may go down to about $10^{-9}$ of the closure density, i.e.\ an energy density $10^{-9}$ of the average of the universe; the cosmic microwave background is about $10^{-5}$, so this is $10^{-4}$ of it, ``quite a large density compared to the predictions'': the predictions are around $10^{-15}$, ``so we don't have a lot of optimism; if we detect something that is great, that is new physics'' \ts{27}{1}{1:07:49}\ts{27}{1}{1:08:10}\ts{27}{1}{1:08:30}\ts{27}{1}{1:08:51}\ts{27}{1}{1:09:11}\ts{27}{1}{1:09:32}.

\paragraph{Third generation: the Einstein Telescope.} After the first detections the community will go back to the funding agencies: ``we have proved that we can do this but we only see a small fraction of the universe, out to $100$, $200$, $300$ Mpc, redshift less than $0.1$; we want to see the whole universe'', i.e.\ ten times better sensitivity. A detailed study funded by the European Union exists: the \textbf{Einstein Telescope} would go underground like in Japan, be cryogenic, have much higher laser power, \emph{cancel local Newtonian noise}, and use squeezed light (next lecture, chapter~\ref{ch:c28}) \ts{27}{1}{1:09:54}\ts{27}{1}{1:10:14}\ts{27}{1}{1:10:36}. Newtonian noise: in the film the evening before the weather pattern was shown, with the gravitational disturbances of weather on time scales of an hour larger than the expected gravitational waves; even on time scales of a second a detector on the ground has problems: a seismic wave running through the rocks is a density wave which changes gravity locally by more than expected, so one would measure it with seismic monitoring and remove it from the data, or cancel it in another way; this extends the frequency range to lower frequencies. It is in a European priority list (the ``Magnificent 7'' of astroparticle physics projects) but one has to get the money, and ``we will not seriously try until after our first detections''. A picture: a couple of hundred metres deep, $10$ km long tunnels with three observing stations, access by elevators, very elaborate engineering, a one billion euro project, justified only by good science: it would detect every neutron star binary coalescence anywhere in the universe during the observation, a huge population, and monitor black holes out to redshift $15$ (``very early in the universe; we don't know when the first black hole mergers began'') \ts{27}{1}{1:10:36}\ts{27}{1}{1:10:57}\ts{27}{1}{1:11:18}\ts{27}{1}{1:11:38}\ts{27}{1}{1:11:58}\ts{27}{1}{1:12:18}\ts{27}{1}{1:12:39}\ts{27}{1}{1:12:59}\ts{27}{1}{1:13:20}.

\section{LISA}\label{sec:c27-lisa}

The space based detector, with the lecture time running short (``15 more minutes'') \ts{27}{1}{1:13:40}. From space one looks at the low frequency range, totally masked by gravity disturbances on Earth: milli-hertz, time scales of an hour. A light crossing time of an hour means a radius of one light hour and a mass of order $10^5$--$10^6$ solar masses: the natural radiators for black hole binaries in the LISA band are \emph{massive black holes} of about a million solar masses. Our galaxy has a black hole of about four million solar masses at its centre and all galaxies seem to have massive black holes in their centres, with a spectrum of masses; the typical masses determined are of order a million solar masses, so the band is perfect to explore the dynamics of the black holes that are a crucial part of the formation of galaxies; when galaxies merge the black holes are brought to merger \ts{27}{1}{1:14:01}\ts{27}{1}{1:14:22}\ts{27}{1}{1:14:42}\ts{27}{1}{1:15:03}\ts{27}{1}{1:15:24}.

LISA would also see \textbf{white dwarf binaries} in our own galaxy, with one hour periods rather than the minute periods of coalescing neutron stars: the galaxy is full of them, so many that at longer periods they give a \emph{confusion noise} limiting the sensitivity of the mission, but at higher frequencies they can be resolved individually over the sky (from the modulation effects), a huge database for the astronomers interested in white dwarf binaries and stellar evolution; and the capture of small black holes by massive ones (``the very dissonant music I played'') \ts{27}{1}{1:15:44}\ts{27}{1}{1:16:04}\ts{27}{1}{1:16:25}\ts{27}{1}{1:16:46}.

\paragraph{The technology.} Different from the ground: one creates regions in space with \emph{pure equivalence principle} and no outside forces. A cube of gold--platinum alloy flies free inside the spacecraft, in a housing, in contact with nothing, the reference point for the interferometry; the spacecraft is built around it and its job is \emph{not to bump into the cube}: there is a force on the spacecraft all the time (pressure from the Sun, \dots), the spacecraft constantly looks where the cube is and fires micro thrusters when pushed toward it. Every arm is terminated by a cube at each end. Many things cannot be tested on the ground (clamping the cube for the rocket ride and releasing it) \ts{27}{1}{1:17:07}\ts{27}{1}{1:17:27}\ts{27}{1}{1:17:48}\ts{27}{1}{1:18:10}\ts{27}{1}{1:18:31}.

\paragraph{LISA Pathfinder.} ESA decided to build a Pathfinder spacecraft with one arm of only half a metre, a cube at each end within the same spacecraft with interferometry between them: not enough to detect gravitational waves, only a test of the technology. It has been a rough ride (``let down by European industry so often because they have never built anything like this''), but it is in the final stages of assembly and will be launched by ESA late this year from Kourou by a Vega launcher to the L1 Lagrange point of the Sun--Earth system [the lecture says ``Earth--Moon system'', which is wrong for L1 of the mission as far as can be said; not checked] and sit there for about six months validating the technologies. LISA is currently number three on the list; if the Pathfinder performs as expected there is a strong argument for an earlier launch. The test mass is the gold--platinum cube with corners where the clamps hold it, inside an electrode housing that senses the position capacitively; contributors in Italy (Trento), the Hanover institute, Astrium in Friedrichshafen \ts{27}{1}{1:18:51}\ts{27}{1}{1:19:13}\ts{27}{1}{1:19:33}\ts{27}{1}{1:19:53}\ts{27}{1}{1:20:14}\ts{27}{1}{1:20:34}\ts{27}{1}{1:20:54}.

\paragraph{LISA and eLISA.} ``Lisa'' and ``eLISA'' are used interchangeably: LISA was the original triangle project of the film; ESA could not afford it alone and wanted a descoped project, so the community gave a project with only two of the three arms (still three spacecraft), eLISA, the minimum, which is going forward; the idea is to bring in other partners (China perhaps, the USA almost certainly) to get the third arm. The goal is a three-armed detector in space. More at the web site elisascience.org \ts{27}{1}{1:21:15}\ts{27}{1}{1:21:36}\ts{27}{1}{1:21:57}\ts{27}{1}{1:22:17}.

\paragraph{Black holes as the main target.} A sensitivity diagram of the two-arm system, ``what sells the project most to the astronomers'': massive black hole systems with two equal masses merging, against redshift, up to $z=20$; around there the universe was reionised, hard for optical and radio observations to see through, yet this is when structure formation happened, small clumps of gas forming fragments that merge into galaxies, with black holes forming and merging as the central black hole of our galaxy did \ts{27}{1}{1:22:38}\ts{27}{1}{1:22:59}\ts{27}{1}{1:23:19}\ts{27}{1}{1:23:40}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Advanced LIGO and ground based detectors explore the corner of low masses ($10^2$--$10^{2.5}$ solar masses) out to redshifts not quite one \ts{27}{1}{1:24:22}.
  \item The Einstein Telescope fills a bigger range, up to $z\approx2$ for black holes of up to almost $10^4$ solar masses \ts{27}{1}{1:24:44}.
  \item Optical astronomers go the other end, seeing $10^{10}$ solar mass black holes in quasars easily, harder and harder as the mass goes down, to high redshift for large masses but only the near universe for small ones \ts{27}{1}{1:25:04}.
  \item Pulsar timing: only the most massive black holes, $10^8$--$10^{10}$ solar masses, out to $z\approx2$, comparable to ET \ts{27}{1}{1:25:26}.
  \item eLISA: contours of signal to noise, $200$, $50$, $20$ and $10$ (the outer limit, roughly the threshold for the ground based detectors); in the range $10^5$--$10^6$ solar masses, where the central black hole of our galaxy sits, one goes out to redshift $20$ with signal to noise $5$ [captions: ``5050''] or more, and LISA is even better, with a broader curve \ts{27}{1}{1:25:47}\ts{27}{1}{1:26:08}\ts{27}{1}{1:26:29}.
\end{itemize}
With these space based detectors one can monitor what actually happened in the early universe as fragments formed their black holes, which merged and gradually built up the central black holes of galaxies, ``a whole story which today we describe only with computer simulations; LISA will make it visible: LISA is the black hole detection machine for astronomy''. There is a region where multimessenger observations (joint with optical and other telescopes) will be possible, and for local black hole mergers the signal to noise goes above $1000$: spins and their directions, masses, distances (and the Hubble constant) to incredible precision, tests of the cosmic censorship and of the no-hair theorem [the captions say ``test her theem'': ``the hair theorem'' as heard; not checked] \ts{27}{1}{1:26:29}\ts{27}{1}{1:26:49}\ts{27}{1}{1:27:13}\ts{27}{1}{1:27:33}\ts{27}{1}{1:27:54}.

\section{Pulsar timing}\label{sec:c27-pta}

Radio astronomers are making progress by pooling data. Pulsars are good clocks: monitor six or eight pulsars around the sky and correlate the arrival times of the pulses; one can predict from some the arrival time of the next pulse of the others, the clocks monitor each other. A gravitational wave coming in distorts the travel distance of the signals in different ways: some delayed, some early, which throws the correlations out; averaging over a year or more removes noise and one can see the correlations. The periods are of the order of a year, so the sources are very large black holes, $10^9$--$10^{10}$ solar masses. These groups are going at it intensively and are not far from the first predictions \ts{27}{1}{1:27:54}\ts{27}{1}{1:28:15}\ts{27}{1}{1:28:35}\ts{27}{1}{1:28:55}.

Alberto Sesana (Albert Einstein Institute) made a detailed study, simulating structure formation in the early universe, of what the pulsar timing people should expect: thousands of model universes with random numbers, an ensemble of predictions of the intensity of the gravitational waves (amplitude of the random background, quite large for these astrophysical backgrounds, unlike the $10^{-15}$ of the cosmological one), all clustered around one region of the diagram. The observers are today encroaching on its beginning, and in three or four years should be well inside: so by the end of the decade pulsar timing should produce its own detection of a random background of gravitational waves from the most massive black holes. ``There is now a race; LIGO does not want to be second; LIGO is turning on but does not know if it will detect for another two years, depending on the source rates; it is very possible that the first detections will be by the pulsar timing people'' \ts{27}{1}{1:29:16}\ts{27}{1}{1:29:37}\ts{27}{1}{1:29:58}\ts{27}{1}{1:30:19}\ts{27}{1}{1:30:39}.

\section{The unexpected}\label{sec:c27-unexpected}

The last slide: what motivates besides what can be predicted is what cannot. Every time a new window opens in astronomy unexpected things happen: x-ray binaries unknown before x-ray telescopes; radio telescope builders astounded at how much was in the radio universe. For gravitational waves one should not expect it with the first systems, which are just barely sensitive to what is expected and need matched filtering, i.e.\ knowing the signal ahead of time; but with high signal to noise platforms like LISA and ET one should expect not just the things known but the unknown ones \ts{27}{1}{1:31:02}\ts{27}{1}{1:31:23}\ts{27}{1}{1:31:43}.

\begin{history}[Windows in astronomy: when opened, when the surprise came]
As said in class (not checked, from the lecture's slide): optical astronomy with Galileo, the first surprise right away (Jupiter's moons), the exception to the rule above; cosmic rays opened in 1912 [the captions give ``192''], the surprising muon in the 1930s; radio telescopes in the 1930s, the big surprises (giant radio galaxies, the cosmic microwave background, pulsars) in the 1950s and 1960s; x-ray observation of the sky began in 1948, the first x-ray binary in 1962 and its understanding with the first satellite in 1969; gamma rays first detected from space in 1961, gamma ray bursts a few years later in the 1960s.
